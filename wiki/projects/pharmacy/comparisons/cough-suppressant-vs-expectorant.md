# Cough Suppressant vs. Expectorant — Comparison (Dextromethorphan / Guaifenesin / Honey)

## Category snapshot

These two mechanisms are marketed for opposite goals — suppressing cough vs. helping it clear mucus — and both carry a real, honestly-documented efficacy gap in this database's own cited RCTs. Pharmacy Times Cough Suppressants (2,315,190/month): Delsym 54%, Mucinex DM 23%, Robitussin 15%, Safe Tussin DM 4%, NyQuil 3%, Tukol Cough & Cold 1%. Expectorants (2,038,866/month): Mucinex 74%, Robitussin 26%.

| Goal | Suppress the cough reflex itself | Loosen/thin mucus to make a productive cough more effective | Soothe throat, reduce cough frequency short-term |

| Best-cited trial's real-world/subjective finding | **No significant difference from placebo** on symptom diary/quality-of-life measures | **No significant difference from placebo** on any objective measure tested | Effective specifically ≤3 days; beyond that, no advantage over placebo |

## The honest finding: neither OTC drug ingredient clearly beats placebo on real symptoms

This is worth stating as plainly as the ingredient pages themselves do, because it cuts against how confidently both are marketed:

- **Dextromethorphan** (Ramsay et al. 2008, RCT, smoker's-cough model): significantly raised the objective cough-reflex threshold vs. placebo — a real pharmacologic effect exists — but on the subjective symptom diary and Leicester Cough Questionnaire, **both dextromethorphan and placebo improved significantly from baseline, with no significant difference between them**. The authors explicitly note this is a recurring pattern in the antitussive literature: cough-reflex-sensitivity effects don't reliably translate to what patients actually notice.
- **Guaifenesin** (Bennett et al. 2015, RCT, single 1200mg dose): no significant effect on mucociliary clearance, cough clearance, or sputum rheology/interfacial tension vs. placebo on any objective measure tested — consistent with StatPearls' own characterization of guaifenesin as having "weak evidence" for effectiveness.
- **Honey**, by contrast, has the most favorable Cochrane-level evidence of the three for short-term use: moderate-certainty benefit vs. no treatment (MD -1.05) and vs. placebo (MD -1.62), and even numerically similar to dextromethorphan (MD -0.07, though low-certainty) in a direct comparison — but **only for up to 3 days**; beyond that window it has no demonstrated advantage over placebo either.

**Net practical read:** in the specific trials cited across this database, honey has the most favorable placebo-controlled evidence of the three for short-term nighttime cough relief in an eligible child — a genuinely notable finding given it's the "natural" option often assumed to be the weakest evidenced. Neither dextromethorphan nor guaifenesin's cited RCT found a clear subjective/functional advantage over placebo, despite both being FDA-approved, long-marketed OTC actives.

## Productive vs. dry cough — does the "match the mechanism to the cough type" logic hold up?

The conventional pharmacy guidance — suppress a dry, disruptive cough with dextromethorphan; use an expectorant like guaifenesin for a "productive" cough with mucus you want to clear — is a real, mechanistically coherent framework, and it's the basis for FDA's approval of both ingredients for "productive cough from the common cold." But given that neither ingredient's best-cited trial in this database found a clear real-world symptom benefit over placebo, that framework should be understood as the physiological rationale for why each drug is approved for its stated indication — not as proof that either reliably delivers a noticeably better outcome than doing nothing. Combination products (Mucinex DM, Robitussin DM) pair both actives together, which doesn't resolve the underlying evidence gap in either ingredient individually.

## Special safety considerations

- **MAOIs:** dextromethorphan is contraindicated with MAOIs or within 2 weeks of stopping one — real serotonin syndrome risk. Guaifenesin is also flagged against concomitant MAOI use per StatPearls, though sourced from a combination-product context.
- **Abuse potential:** dextromethorphan carries a real, documented abuse potential at supratherapeutic doses (dissociative/PCP-like effects at defined "plateau" dose thresholds) despite its OTC status — not shared by guaifenesin or honey.
- **Kidney stones:** guaifenesin is contraindicated in patients with a kidney-stone history (increases urine supersaturation/crystallization risk) — a real, specific contraindication not shared by dextromethorphan.
- **Honey's age-12-months cutoff is categorically different from the other two age restrictions on this page.** Dextromethorphan's and guaifenesin's pediatric age limits reflect dosing/safety-margin caution; honey's is an absolute, non-dose-dependent contraindication — infantile botulism risk exists from spore exposure itself, with no "small amount" exception, per [[honey-pediatric-cough]].
- **Brand-name ambiguity:** "Robitussin" spans both plain dextromethorphan and guaifenesin+dextromethorphan (Robitussin DM) formulations under one brand name — check the specific SKU, a pattern documented in more depth on [[substitutions-and-duplicates]].

## Bottom lines

1. **Child ≥1 year, short-term nighttime cough?** Honey has the most favorable placebo-controlled evidence of the three ingredients compared here — but only for ≤3 days of use, and only ≥12 months old, never younger.
2. **Adult with a disruptive dry cough?** Dextromethorphan has a real, measurable effect on cough-reflex sensitivity, even though the best-cited trial didn't find a clear subjective advantage over placebo — a genuine but modest evidence base, not a strong one.
3. **Adult with chest congestion, want to "loosen things up"?** Guaifenesin is FDA-approved for this, but the most rigorous objective RCT cited on its own page found no significant effect vs. placebo on any measure tested — worth knowing before assuming it will clearly help.
4. **On an MAOI?** Avoid dextromethorphan-containing products; use guaifenesin or honey instead, subject to their own restrictions above.
5. **History of kidney stones?** Avoid guaifenesin specifically.
6. **Infant under 12 months with a cough?** Neither dextromethorphan (not for <4y) nor honey (absolutely contraindicated <12mo) is appropriate — see a pediatrician rather than reaching for an OTC cough product in this age group.
