# GI Acid Reducers — Comparison (Antacids / H2 Blockers / PPIs)

## Category snapshot

These three classes treat the same symptom (heartburn/acid indigestion) at three different points on a speed-vs-durability spectrum, not three interchangeable options. Pharmacy Times Acid Reducers category (1,981,513/month recommendations): Prilosec OTC 37%, Pepcid 35%, Nexium 24HR 14%, Prevacid 24HR 11%, Zegerid OTC 2%, Tagamet HB 200 1%. Antacids and H2 blockers are tracked in their own separate categories too (Antacids: Tums 52%, Gaviscon 19%, Mylanta 16%, Alka-Seltzer 7%, Gelusil 3%, Rolaids 3% — 1,411,765/month; H2 Receptor Antagonists: Pepcid 83%, Tagamet HB 200 9%, Zantac 360° 8% — 1,435,714/month).

| Class | Representative | Onset | Duration | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[antacids]] | Tums (calcium carbonate) | Minutes | ~1–2h (short) | Directly neutralizes existing stomach acid |
| H2 blockers ([[famotidine]]) | Pepcid | ~1h, peak 1–3h | ~10–12h | Blocks histamine (H2) signal to acid-producing cells |
| [[proton-pump-inhibitors]] | Prilosec OTC | 1–4 days for full effect | 24h+, cumulative over a course | Blocks the final acid-producing pump itself — most complete suppression |

## How the three actually differ in use pattern

- **Antacids** are the only one of the three that works on acid already produced — chew 2–4 Tums tablets as symptoms occur, relief in minutes, gone within an hour or two. Best for occasional, mild, already-happening heartburn. Max 15 tablets/24h (10 if pregnant) per [[antacids]]' Tums label data; not meant for chronic daily use.
- **[[Famotidine]]** (H2 blocker) is dosed *before* a trigger — the OTC Pepcid AC Max label directs taking it 10–60 minutes before trigger foods for prevention, or as needed, with effect lasting long enough (~10–12h) to cover a whole meal or a whole night. It's a middle ground: faster onset than a PPI, longer coverage than an antacid.
- **[[Proton-pump-inhibitors|PPIs]]** (Prilosec OTC, Nexium 24HR, Prevacid 24HR, Zegerid OTC) are not an as-needed rescue drug at all — the label is explicit that they are **not for immediate relief** and may take 1–4 days to reach full effect, taken once daily before the morning meal for a full 14-day course (repeatable up to every 4 months). They're built for frequent heartburn (≥2 days/week), not a single bad meal.

**A genuine efficacy caveat worth stating plainly:** per [[proton-pump-inhibitors]], StatPearls reports that **roughly 50% of patients taking a PPI for non-erosive GERD do not achieve complete symptom resolution** — even the most potent class in this comparison has a real, substantial real-world efficacy gap, not a guarantee of full relief.

## Safety differentiators

| Risk | Antacids | H2 blockers (famotidine) | PPIs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chronic-use signal | Aluminum → constipation/toxicity in renal failure/infants; calcium → milk-alkali syndrome, hypercalcemia; not recommended for chronic use in infants/elderly | Thrombocytopenia (documented cases, bone marrow suppression or platelet autoantibodies) with long-term use; agitation notably higher in infants (14% vs <1% adults) | Hypomagnesemia (rare, serious, sometimes not correctable by supplementation alone); increased C. difficile and other enteric infection risk; rebound acid hypersecretion on stopping |
| Long-term-use associations still unsettled | Not applicable at typical intermittent use | Not a major signal in the sources reviewed | Dementia, kidney disease, heart disease — StatPearls explicitly characterizes these as unconfirmed observational associations, not established harms |
| Renal impairment | Aluminum-containing antacids specifically riskier in renal failure | Half-life extends from 2.5–3.5h to **>20h** in severe renal impairment (CrCl <10 mL/min) — dose reduction required at CrCl <50 mL/min | No dedicated renal-dosing flag in the source reviewed beyond general caution |
| Drug interactions | Broad — impairs absorption of digoxin, chlorpromazine, isoniazid, tetracyclines/fluoroquinolones (magnesium salts); increases absorption of pseudoephedrine, levodopa | Mild CYP1A2 inhibitor — significantly raises tizanidine levels (avoid); reduces absorption of cefuroxime, dasatinib, delavirdine, neratinib, pazopanib, risedronate | Delayed clearance of phenytoin, warfarin, diazepam; reduced ketoconazole absorption; potentiated digoxin absorption |
| Pregnancy | Aluminum-based: considered safe including during labor. Calcium-based: caution, capped dosing (10 vs 15 tablets/24h on Tums label) | Crosses placenta; former Category B; considered after risk-benefit assessment, antacids/lifestyle first-line | Not detailed with pregnancy-specific data in the sources reviewed for this page |
| Breastfeeding | Aluminum-based not well studied; calcium-based considered safe | Appears in breast milk at **lower** concentrations than other H2RAs — a preferable H2RA choice in lactation | Not detailed with lactation-specific data in the sources reviewed for this page |

## A brand-identity trap in this exact category

[[Substitutions-and-duplicates]] documents this in more depth, but it belongs here too: **"Zantac" is not one drug.** The original Zantac was ranitidine, withdrawn from the US market in 2019–2020 over NDMA contamination; "Zantac 360°" relaunched in 2021 with an entirely different active ingredient — famotidine — the same molecule as Pepcid. Two products can also share this category and differ meaningfully within the same drug class: Tagamet HB 200 is cimetidine, not famotidine, despite sitting in the same H2-Receptor-Antagonists row as Pepcid — cimetidine carries a notable CYP450 warfarin interaction that famotidine does not share to the same degree. And within antacids, Gaviscon (US) is aluminum hydroxide + magnesium carbonate — not calcium carbonate like Tums/Rolaids — while Alka-Seltzer pairs its antacid action with a genuine 325mg dose of **aspirin** per tablet, a real, separate consideration (see [[aspirin]]) that nothing else in this category shares.

## Bottom lines

1. **Occasional heartburn after a specific meal, want relief now?** An antacid (Tums, Rolaids) — minutes to relief, but short-lived and not meant for daily chronic use.
2. **Know a trigger is coming, or need coverage through a meal/night?** Famotidine (Pepcid), dosed 10–60 minutes ahead — faster than a PPI, longer-lasting than an antacid.
3. **Heartburn ≥2 days a week, ongoing?** A PPI (Prilosec OTC/omeprazole and siblings) is the FDA-OTC-labeled option for that frequency — but expect it to take 1–4 days to fully kick in, not to work like a rescue medication, and know that even at its best it doesn't fully resolve symptoms in roughly half of non-erosive GERD patients per the cited StatPearls figure.
4. **On a low-sodium diet or have CHF/hypertension?** Check the specific product — Zegerid OTC (omeprazole + sodium bicarbonate) carries ~303mg sodium/capsule and Alka-Seltzer ~567mg sodium/tablet, both flagged on their own pages; this is a brand/formulation-specific risk, not a class-wide one.
5. **On other medications?** All three classes interact broadly enough (antacids alone affect digoxin, tetracyclines, pseudoephedrine, levodopa) that the Tums OTC label itself advises asking a doctor/pharmacist before combining with any prescription drug — worth taking literally rather than assuming a "natural" antacid is interaction-free.
6. **Renal impairment?** Reduce/extend famotidine dosing (CrCl <50 mL/min) and use extra caution with aluminum-containing antacids; PPIs have less renal-specific dosing guidance in the sources reviewed but carry their own hypomagnesemia/infection-risk profile.
