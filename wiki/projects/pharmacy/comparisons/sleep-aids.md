## Category snapshot

Two fundamentally different approaches sit in this aisle: first-generation antihistamines (sedating as a side effect, repurposed as a sleep aid) and melatonin (a hormone supplement acting on the body's own sleep-timing signal). ## Evidence: none of the three is clean, but the specific gaps differ

- **Diphenhydramine and doxylamine** share the same core problem: a systematic review of OTC agents for occasional sleeplessness found these commonly used OTC sleep aids "generally lack robust clinical evidence supporting efficacy and safety" for this use — despite FDA recognizing diphenhydramine (up to 50mg) and doxylamine (25mg) as safe/effective OTC sleep aids. A separate review found some positive evidence specifically in older adults — but that finding sits in direct, unresolved tension with the Beers Criteria guidance below. No doxylamine-specific RCT with hard efficacy numbers was independently located for [[doxylamine]]'s page; its evidence tier is inferred from the class-level review that covers diphenhydramine, not from a dedicated doxylamine trial.
- **Melatonin** has the most quantified evidence of the three: a 2024 dose-response meta-analysis (26 RCTs, 1,689 observations) found a real, dose-dependent reduction in sleep-onset latency and increase in total sleep time, peaking at 4mg/day — a positive, if modest, real trial signal the antihistamines' cited reviews don't offer at the same level of detail. But the same meta-analysis found the **common real-world dosing pattern (2mg, 30 minutes before bed) is likely suboptimal** — the evidence-derived ideal is 4mg dosed 3 hours before the desired bedtime, a genuinely actionable and under-followed finding.

## The single biggest safety differentiator: the Beers Criteria vs. melatonin's quality-control problem

**Diphenhydramine and doxylamine (both first-generation antihistamines) are flagged BY THE AGS Beers Criteria — the standard US guideline for potentially inappropriate medications in older adults — for avoidance in most or all older adults.** Both are highly anticholinergic; clearance is reduced with age; tolerance develops with regular hypnotic use (undermining even short-term benefit); and cumulative anticholinergic exposure is associated with increased falls, delirium, and dementia risk — an association reported even in younger adults, per [[oral-diphenhydramine-sleep-aid]]. This directly implicates "PM" combination products (Tylenol PM, Advil PM, Aleve PM) and dedicated sleep aids (Unisom, ZzzQuil, Sominex, Simply Sleep) marketed for and commonly used by exactly this population.

**Melatonin's central risk is different in kind: a documented, real quality-control problem, not a receptor-level toxicity mechanism.** A cited study of 31 melatonin supplements found actual content ranged from **-83% to +478% of the labeled dose** — a substantial real-world concern specific to melatonin's unregulated-supplement status that neither antihistamine shares (both are FDA-monograph OTC drugs with label-accuracy requirements diphenhydramine/doxylamine must meet). Melatonin also shows a striking pediatric-exposure trend: US poison control cases rose from ~8,000 (2012) to over 52,000 (2021), ~15% requiring hospitalization — reflecting accessibility and appealing gummy formulations more than acute lethality (melatonin's acute toxicity is remarkably low — no established LD50 even at 800mg/kg in animal studies).

## Other safety notes

- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** melatonin has insufficient safety evidence and avoidance is recommended. Neither antihistamine page states a blanket pregnancy contraindication for the OTC sleep-aid dose, though both carry the shared first-generation-antihistamine anticholinergic profile to weigh.
- **Combining with other sedatives:** all three interact with CNS depressants (benzodiazepines, alcohol) via additive sedation — a shared caution across the whole category, not unique to one ingredient.
- **Additive-dose risk within the SAME molecule:** diphenhydramine and doxylamine labels both warn against combining with other products containing the same or a related antihistamine — relevant because both molecules appear across many combination products (see [[substitutions-and-duplicates]]).
- **CYP1A2 interaction:** melatonin levels can rise significantly with CYP1A2 inhibitors like fluvoxamine — a drug-interaction pathway the antihistamines don't share.

## A brand-name trap specific to this category

**"Unisom" sells two chemically different molecules under one brand name and one tracked recommendation share (58%):** Unisom SleepGels is diphenhydramine 50mg; Unisom SleepTabs is doxylamine 25mg. This database cannot determine what fraction of Unisom's 58% share reflects each molecule. Separately, **Vick's ZzzQuil and ZzzQuil Pure Zzzs are NOT the same active ingredient** despite the shared brand name — plain ZzzQuil is diphenhydramine, while "Pure Zzzs" is melatonin-based, tracked in an entirely different Pharmacy Times category (Melatonin Sleep Aids vs. Sleep Aids Non-Melatonin). Checking the specific sub-product name matters more than the parent brand here.

## Bottom lines

1. **Older adult?** Both diphenhydramine and doxylamine carry a real AGS Beers Criteria recommendation to avoid — this applies to the whole first-generation-antihistamine class, not just diphenhydramine by name. Melatonin is the pharmacologically gentler option for this population, though it carries its own fall/fracture risk signal and lacks the antihistamines' regulatory quality-control floor.
2. **Want the best-quantified trial evidence?** Melatonin's 2024 dose-response meta-analysis is more detailed and positive than anything cited for diphenhydramine/doxylamine specifically — but only if dosed correctly: 4mg, roughly 3 hours before bed, not the common 2mg-at-bedtime pattern most consumer products default to.
3. **Buying a melatonin supplement?** Content-accuracy is a real, documented problem (-83% to +478% of label in the cited study) — a USP-Verified product offers more assurance than an unverified one.
4. **Young children in the house?** Treat melatonin gummies with the same storage caution as any medication — the poison-control exposure trend (8,000→52,000+/year) is real and recent.
5. **Checking a "PM" combination product (Tylenol PM, Advil PM, Aleve PM)?** The sleep-aid component in all three is diphenhydramine — the choice between them is really a choice of which analgesic (acetaminophen, ibuprofen, naproxen) to pair with the same antihistamine and its Beers Criteria flag.
