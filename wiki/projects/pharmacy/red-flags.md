# Red Flags — When to Stop Self-Treating and Seek Care

**Type:** Analysis (cross-cutting safety synthesis)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Last updated:** 2026-08-28
**Sources:** Aggregated from the Boxed Warnings, Contraindications, Key Safety Data, and Overdose/Toxicity sections already sourced (StatPearls, FDA Drug Facts labels via DailyMed, Cochrane reviews) across the 122 `ingredients/*.md` pages and select `drugs/*.md` pages in this database. No new primary research was performed for this page — it organizes what has already been sourced elsewhere, by symptom/body system, for fast lookup.

---

## How to use this page

Most OTC symptoms are genuinely fine to self-treat. This page exists for the minority of cases where a symptom that looks like an ordinary cold/headache/upset stomach is actually a sign of something the corresponding OTC product's own FDA label says to stop for. **This page is an organizational aid, not a diagnostic tool** — it does not replace calling a doctor, Poison Control, or 911 when in doubt. Every row below is sourced to a specific ingredient (and, where noted, brand) page in this database; follow the link for full dosing/mechanism/citation detail.

**Two numbers worth memorizing:**
- **Poison Control (US): 1-800-222-1222** — call for any suspected overdose or unexpected ingestion, even without symptoms yet.
- **911 / nearest ER** — call for any red flag marked **EMERGENCY** below (difficulty breathing, chest pain, seizure, loss of consciousness, suspected stroke, severe allergic reaction).

## 1. Universal red flags (any OTC product, any category)

| Symptom | Why it's a red flag | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hives, facial/throat/tongue swelling, difficulty breathing after any product | Anaphylaxis — can be fatal, can occur with topical, oral, or vaginal products, not just injectable/prescription drugs | **EMERGENCY — 911** | [[topical-antibiotics]] (bacitracin/neomycin anaphylaxis reports), [[docosanol]] (label allergy alert), [[chlorhexidine]] (FDA added an anaphylaxis warning to topical OTC labels in 2017), [[aspirin]] (1–2% hypersensitivity, mild rash to angioedema/anaphylaxis), [[second-generation-antihistamines]] (rare postmarketing anaphylaxis) |
| Any rash that develops after starting a new OTC drug, especially if spreading or blistering | Early sign of Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN) or DRESS syndrome — both can be fatal, both can start as an ordinary-looking rash | Discontinue immediately and seek care same-day; **EMERGENCY** if blistering, mucous-membrane involvement, or fever accompanies the rash | [[ibuprofen]] ("discontinue at first rash"), [[naproxen]], [[acetaminophen]], [[loperamide]], [[terbinafine]] (fulminant liver failure also reported) |
| Symptoms not improving after the labeled maximum duration (commonly 7–10 days for most OTC categories) | Nearly every FDA OTC label includes this exact instruction — it exists because a symptom that won't resolve with appropriate self-care may not be what you think it is | See a doctor | Standard across essentially all `ingredients/*.md` Contraindications/Key Safety Data sections |

## 2. Cardiovascular

| Symptom / trigger | Ingredient(s) involved | Why | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chest pain, especially with shortness of breath | [[proton-pump-inhibitors]] and any acid reducer | OTC label explicitly instructs: do not use with "chest pain with respiratory symptoms, or frequent chest pain — these suggest a condition beyond simple heartburn" | **EMERGENCY** | [[proton-pump-inhibitors]] |
| Fainting (syncope), palpitations, or known heart-rhythm problem while using an antidiarrheal | [[loperamide]] | **FDA Boxed Warning**: torsades de pointes and sudden death at higher-than-recommended doses — QT/QTc prolongation, especially with abuse-level dosing | **EMERGENCY** if syncope/palpitations occur; ≥24h medical observation required even after naloxone reversal, since loperamide outlasts naloxone | [[loperamide]] |
| New/worsening chest pain, leg swelling, or shortness of breath during regular NSAID use | [[ibuprofen]], [[naproxen]] | **FDA Boxed Warning** (shared class warning): increased risk of MI/stroke, can be fatal, risk can occur early in treatment and increases with duration; ~2x increase in heart-failure hospitalization vs. placebo in ibuprofen data | Stop and call a doctor; **EMERGENCY** if chest pain, slurred speech, or one-sided weakness | [[ibuprofen]], [[naproxen]] |
| Irregular heartbeat, muscle weakness, difficulty breathing during high-dose magnesium (laxative/supplement) use | [[magnesium-hydroxide]], [[magnesium]] | Hypermagnesemia — CNS depression, muscle weakness/paralysis, hypotension, respiratory depression, prolonged cardiac conduction, cardiac arrest in severe cases; real fatal cases reported | **EMERGENCY** | [[magnesium-hydroxide]], [[magnesium]] |
| Pre-existing heart disease, high blood pressure, or thyroid disease and considering a decongestant | [[pseudoephedrine]], [[oral-phenylephrine]], [[oxymetazoline]] | All three are sympathomimetics; every label instructs "ask a doctor before use" for these conditions | Ask a doctor before starting, don't self-start | [[pseudoephedrine]], [[oral-phenylephrine]], [[oxymetazoline]] |
| Asthma + nasal polyps + a reaction to any NSAID or aspirin in the past | [[ibuprofen]], [[naproxen]], [[aspirin]] | Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease / "aspirin triad" — risk of severe, potentially fatal bronchospasm with ANY NSAID, not just the one that caused a prior reaction | Avoid all NSAIDs; **EMERGENCY** if wheezing/bronchospasm occurs | [[ibuprofen]], [[aspirin]] |

## 3. Gastrointestinal bleeding

| Symptom | Why it's a red flag | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vomiting blood, or blood/coffee-ground material in vomit | GI bleed — can be from NSAID-induced ulceration or a more serious underlying cause | **EMERGENCY** | [[famotidine]] ("do not use with...vomiting blood"), [[proton-pump-inhibitors]] |
| Black, tarry stools, or visibly bloody stools | GI bleeding. **Caveat:** bismuth subsalicylate (Pepto-Bismol/Kaopectate) causes harmless temporary black-stool/black-tongue discoloration as an expected side effect — see [[substitutions-and-duplicates]] and [[bismuth-subsalicylate]] for how to tell the difference (harmless discoloration is uniform and painless; true melena is usually accompanied by other symptoms and should still prompt a call if there's any doubt) | See a doctor same-day; **EMERGENCY** if accompanied by dizziness, weakness, or fainting (signs of significant blood loss) | [[famotidine]], [[bismuth-subsalicylate]] |
| Difficulty or pain swallowing, while using an acid reducer | Labeled "do not use" trigger — suggests a condition beyond simple heartburn | See a doctor | [[famotidine]], [[proton-pump-inhibitors]] |
| GI ulcer/bleed/perforation with NO prior warning symptoms during regular NSAID use | Per FDA labeling, this **can occur at any time, with or without warning symptoms**, and elderly patients are at greater risk; real incidence ~1% at 3–6 months of use, ~2–4% at one year (ibuprofen data), and >10-fold higher with a prior ulcer/GI-bleed history | Do not exceed labeled duration/dose; report any new abdominal pain | [[ibuprofen]] |

## 4. Liver

| Symptom | Why it's a red flag | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yellowing of skin/eyes (jaundice), dark urine, unusual fatigue, abdominal pain, especially 1–4 days after a large or repeated acetaminophen dose | Acetaminophen-induced liver injury — the **most common drug-related cause of acute liver failure in the US** (~500 deaths/year, ~50,000 ED admissions/year, >80,000 poison-control cases in 2021). Early phase (0–24h) is often asymptomatic even when a dangerous dose was taken — do not wait for symptoms if a large ingestion is suspected | **Call Poison Control immediately (1-800-222-1222) even with no symptoms yet** — antidote (N-acetylcysteine) is time-critical and most effective started early | [[acetaminophen]] |
| Never combine two different acetaminophen-containing products | Overlapping acetaminophen content across products (e.g., a cold/flu combo plus plain Tylenol) is a **leading real-world cause of unintentional overdose** | Check every label; see [[substitutions-and-duplicates]] | [[acetaminophen]] |
| Jaundice, dark urine, or abdominal pain while using a menopause herbal supplement | Black cohosh — rare case reports of hepatotoxicity have prompted cautionary labeling in some markets | Discontinue and seek care | [[menopause-herbal-supplements]] |
| Severe or persistent taste disturbance plus jaundice while using an oral antifungal | Terbinafine — reports of fulminant liver failure | Discontinue and seek care | [[terbinafine]] |

## 5. Neurological / Psychiatric

| Symptom / trigger | Ingredient(s) | Why | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Currently taking (or stopped within 2 weeks) an MAOI, and considering dextromethorphan, pseudoephedrine, or oral phenylephrine | [[dextromethorphan]], [[pseudoephedrine]], [[oral-phenylephrine]] | Serotonin syndrome risk (dextromethorphan) or hypertensive crisis risk (the two decongestants) — all three are **contraindicated** with MAOIs, not just "use caution" | Do not combine; ask a doctor first | [[dextromethorphan]], [[pseudoephedrine]], [[oral-phenylephrine]] |
| Agitation, confusion, hallucinations, dissociation ("PCP-like" state), or unusual behavior after a cough medicine | [[dextromethorphan]] | Dissociative psychosis at supratherapeutic/abuse doses; also occurs with concurrent SSRIs/SNRIs/TCAs (serotonin syndrome risk, not just MAOIs) | **EMERGENCY** if severe (seizures, hyperthermia, altered mental status) | [[dextromethorphan]] |
| Seizures in a child after a chest vapor rub is applied, ingested, or gets near the nose | Camphor (in [[camphor-menthol-eucalyptus]] products, e.g. Vicks VapoRub) | Real, well-documented pediatric toxicity: progression from sweating/agitation to seizures, cardiac arrhythmias, cardiopulmonary arrest — camphor is toxic even at low concentrations if ingested or absorbed through mucous membranes/broken skin. Contraindicated under age 2; never apply near/inside the nose | **EMERGENCY** | [[camphor-menthol-eucalyptus]] |
| Seizures in a child after DEET-containing insect repellent use | [[insect-repellents-deet-picaridin]] | 17 documented case reports of DEET-associated toxic encephalopathy in children, seizures in 72% of those cases — though EPA's own reviews could not establish causation and maintain DEET does not present a health concern when used as directed. Both facts are real; treat any seizure after DEET use as an emergency regardless of the unresolved causation question | **EMERGENCY** | [[insect-repellents-deet-picaridin]] |
| Confusion, agitation progressing to seizures after an "PM"/nighttime sleep product or an antihistamine overdose | [[oral-diphenhydramine-sleep-aid]], [[doxylamine]], [[dimenhydrinate]], [[meclizine]] | Anticholinergic toxidrome: sedation progressing to agitation/delirium, dry mouth, blurred vision, tachycardia, and in severe cases seizures or cardiac conduction abnormalities | **EMERGENCY** if severe; supportive care is standard, no specific antidote for routine OTC-dose overdose | [[oral-diphenhydramine-sleep-aid]], [[doxylamine]], [[dimenhydrinate]] |
| Confusion, cyanosis (bluish skin), or trouble breathing after a topical/oral local anesthetic (benzocaine-type numbing products) | [[topical-anesthetics]] | Methemoglobinemia — >10% methemoglobin causes unexplained cyanosis, 15–35% headache/tachycardia/dyspnea, >35% CNS effects (confusion, seizures, coma, apnea), >70% can be fatal. **Risk increases ~20-fold with re-exposure within the previous week** | **EMERGENCY** — treated with supplemental oxygen and IV methylene blue | [[topical-anesthetics]] |
| Ringing in ears, dizziness, confusion, rapid breathing progressing to seizures/coma | Salicylate toxicity — [[aspirin]], [[bismuth-subsalicylate]], or high-concentration [[salicylic-acid]] absorbed through skin (especially over large areas in children) | Toxic salicylate level >300 mcg/mL; severe toxicity includes hyperthermia, respiratory alkalosis, high anion-gap metabolic acidosis, seizures, coma, cerebral edema | Mild symptoms (tinnitus, dizziness): call Poison Control. Severe symptoms: **EMERGENCY** | [[aspirin]], [[salicylic-acid]] |
| Anxiety/mood worsening while using an OTC "stress relief" supplement | [[mood-health-supplements]] (a real lawsuit alleges anxiety-relief marketing on one product could actively worsen outcomes by displacing real care) | Not itself a medical emergency, but a genuine reason to seek real mental-health care rather than continuing to self-treat | See a doctor if anxiety/mood symptoms are significant or worsening | [[mood-health-supplements]] |

## 7. Renal (kidney)

| Symptom / trigger | Ingredient(s) | Why | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Using more than one sodium phosphate enema (e.g. Fleet Enema) in 24 hours | [[sodium-phosphate]] | FDA-documented acute phosphate nephropathy — calcium-phosphate crystals deposit in renal tubules, can cause permanent kidney damage requiring dialysis/transplant; FDA issued two safety communications (2006, 2014) and this exact overdose pattern has been associated with death | **EMERGENCY if symptomatic; never exceed one enema/24h in the first place** | [[sodium-phosphate]] |
| Decreased urination, swelling, fatigue in a dehydrated person (e.g., athlete) taking regular ibuprofen | [[ibuprofen]] | Real quantified risk: NNH 5.5 for acute kidney injury in dehydrated ultramarathon runners — illustrates dehydration as a major AKI-risk multiplier on top of NSAID use | Stay hydrated when using NSAIDs during heavy exertion/heat; seek care if urination decreases significantly | [[ibuprofen]] |
| Chronic kidney disease, diabetes, age over 65, or concurrent NSAID/ACE-inhibitor/ARB/diuretic use, and considering a sodium phosphate product | [[sodium-phosphate]] | All are independently identified FDA risk factors for acute phosphate nephropathy | Avoid or use only under medical guidance | [[sodium-phosphate]] |

## 8. Reye Syndrome (pediatric — salicylates)

**The single most important pediatric OTC safety rule in this database.** Children or teenagers with a viral illness (flu, chickenpox, or any flu-like illness) who take a salicylate can develop Reye syndrome — a rare but serious encephalopathy with fatty liver degeneration, **30–45% mortality**.

| Trigger | Products involved | Watch for | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fever/flu-like illness or chickenpox in a child or teen + any salicylate | [[aspirin]] (including combination products like BC Powder/Goody's Powder/Excedrin — see [[substitutions-and-duplicates]]), [[bismuth-subsalicylate]] (Pepto-Bismol, Kaopectate) | Persistent vomiting, unusual behavior/confusion, lethargy during or after the illness | **EMERGENCY if behavior changes with vomiting occur** — this combination is an explicit "stop use and ask a doctor" trigger on the bismuth-subsalicylate label itself |

**Never give aspirin or bismuth subsalicylate to a child or teenager with a current or recent viral illness** — use acetaminophen or ibuprofen for pediatric fever/pain instead (per [[aspirin]], this is exactly why acetaminophen became the preferred pediatric antipyretic).

## 9. Pediatric-specific red flags (beyond Reye syndrome)

| Trigger | Ingredient(s) | Why | Action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accidental ingestion of adult-strength iron tablets by a child | [[iron]] | Iron overdose is a recognized serious pediatric poisoning risk — extremely high doses can cause corrosive intestinal necrosis, multisystem organ failure, and death. Iron has a meaningfully sharper toxicity profile than most other vitamin/mineral supplements | **Call Poison Control immediately, even if the child seems fine** | [[iron]] |
| Camphor-containing vapor rub applied to or near a child under 2, or ingested | [[camphor-menthol-eucalyptus]] | See Neurological section above — contraindicated under age 2 by every label reviewed | **EMERGENCY** if seizure/arrhythmia symptoms occur | [[camphor-menthol-eucalyptus]] |
| Diphenhydramine (oral or topical) given to a young child, especially over-applied topically | [[oral-diphenhydramine-sleep-aid]], [[topical-diphenhydramine]] | Toxic diphenhydramine levels in children can progress to CNS excitation — hallucinations, agitation, ataxia, seizures, coma, cardiopulmonary arrest. A documented pediatric fatality case exists from the **topical** route specifically (over-application, not ingestion) | **EMERGENCY** | [[topical-diphenhydramine]] |
| Loperamide given to a child under 2 | [[loperamide]] | Flatly contraindicated under 2 years; not recommended for infectious diarrhea in pediatric patients generally | Do not use; consult a pediatrician for pediatric diarrhea instead | [[loperamide]] |
| A caregiver mixing up old vs. new liquid acetaminophen concentrations | [[acetaminophen]] | Historical (pre-June 2011) dosing-error risk from two different concentrations ("infant" 80mg/0.8mL vs. "children's" 160mg/5mL); all current products in this database are confirmed standardized to 160mg/5mL, but the underlying lesson (always check concentration, not just "how many mL/drops") still applies whenever an old bottle might be in the house | Check the label's mg/mL concentration every time, not just the dose in mL/drops from memory | [[acetaminophen]] |

## 10. Overdose thresholds — quick reference

| Substance | Toxic threshold | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Acetaminophen | Rare toxicity below 200 mg/kg (child) / 150 mg/kg (adult); labeled adult max 4000mg/day | Poison Control immediately (1-800-222-1222), even before symptoms appear — NAC antidote is time-critical |
| Aspirin / salicylates | Therapeutic 150–300 mcg/mL; toxic >300 mcg/mL | Poison Control for mild symptoms (tinnitus, dizziness); **911** for severe (seizures, altered mental status) |
| Ibuprofen | Severe pediatric toxicity threshold ≥400 mg/kg; labeled adult max 1200mg/day (OTC) / 3200mg/day (physician-directed) | Poison Control; gastric decontamination if <1h post-ingestion |
| Iron | Tolerable Upper Intake Level 45mg (adult) / 40–45mg (child, by age) — supplement-strength overdose is a genuine emergency | Poison Control immediately |
| Loperamide | Torsades/sudden death risk rises sharply above the labeled 8mg/24h (adult) OTC max | **911** if syncope/palpitations; ≥24h observation after any naloxone use |
| Sodium phosphate (enema) | More than one enema per 24h | **911** if symptomatic; do not exceed labeled dose in the first place |
| Diphenhydramine/doxylamine | No fixed number given in sources reviewed — anticholinergic toxidrome risk rises with dose | Poison Control; supportive care |
