## First question: is this a cold, or an allergy?

The right first-line tool differs by cause, though many people use a decongestant for both:

- **Cold/acute congestion (viral, short-term):** a decongestant (oral or nasal spray) or saline are the standard tools — see below.
- **Allergic rhinitis (recurring, seasonal/perennial, often with sneezing/itchy eyes):** an intranasal corticosteroid or a second-generation oral antihistamine is the more evidence-supported first-line choice per [[intranasal-corticosteroids]] ("intranasal corticosteroids are superior to antihistamines specifically at reducing nasal inflammation... more effective than antihistamines at relieving sneezing, rhinorrhea, and nasal pruritus/blockage" — StatPearls) — not just a decongestant, which treats the vascular symptom but not the underlying allergic inflammation.

## Oral decongestants — pseudoephedrine vs. phenylephrine

See [[nasal-decongestants]] for the full comparison. Short version: **pseudoephedrine (Sudafed)** has real positive RCT evidence at its marketed dose in both adults and children, while **oral phenylephrine (Sudafed PE)** does not — the FDA's own 2023 advisory committee unanimously found it not GRASE as a decongestant at any tested oral dose. Pseudoephedrine's tradeoff is a behind-the-counter purchase requirement (US federal law, methamphetamine-precursor control), not an efficacy problem.

## Nasal spray decongestant — oxymetazoline

**Oxymetazoline (Afrin)**, per [[oxymetazoline]], works well for short-term relief but must be limited to **3 consecutive days** — continued use risks rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa), a common (not rare) real-world problem. Good for an acute bad night or a flight; a poor choice as a daily strategy.

## Saline — the lowest-risk option, with real (if modest) evidence

Per [[saline-nasal-sprays]]: most saline nasal products contain no active drug at all and carry essentially no rebound-congestion risk, unlike vasoconstrictor sprays. Cochrane-level evidence supports a real benefit for allergic rhinitis symptom severity (Head et al. 2018) and some benefit for acute upper-respiratory-infection symptoms (King et al. 2015). **Hypertonic saline (e.g., Vicks Sinex Saline, NeilMed NasaMist Extra Strength) measurably outperforms isotonic saline for congestion specifically** in multiple meta-analyses — a real mechanism difference (osmotic fluid draw), not just marketing. Saline is a reasonable first-line option for infants/young children, pregnant people, or anyone wanting to avoid systemic decongestant exposure entirely.

## Allergy-specific: intranasal corticosteroids and antihistamines

- **Intranasal corticosteroids** (Flonase, Nasacort, Nasonex — see [[intranasal-corticosteroids]]) are first-line for allergic rhinitis per StatPearls, but are **not a same-hour rescue product** — full effect can take several days of regular use, unlike an oral decongestant.
- **Second-generation oral antihistamines** (Zyrtec, Claritin, Allegra, Xyzal, Alavert — see [[second-generation-antihistamines]] and [[oral-antihistamines]]) help allergy-driven congestion, sneezing, and itching, though intranasal corticosteroids outperform them specifically for nasal congestion per the mechanism note above.
- **Combination products** (Claritin-D, Zyrtec-D, Allegra-D — antihistamine + pseudoephedrine) exist for people who need both an allergy and a decongestant effect at once; individual labels not independently re-verified per SKU in this database.

## Seek care if — pointer to Layer 5

From this database's own sourced content (see [[red-flags]] for full detail):
- Heart disease, high blood pressure, thyroid disease, diabetes, or an MAOI prescription — **ask a doctor before starting any oral decongestant** (pseudoephedrine or oral phenylephrine); both are flatly contraindicated with MAOIs (hypertensive-crisis risk).
- Nervousness, dizziness, or sleeplessness while on a decongestant, or congestion not improving within 7 days, or congestion with fever — stop and ask a doctor per the pseudoephedrine OTC label.
- Any rash, hives, facial/throat swelling, or difficulty breathing — possible anaphylaxis — **emergency**.
- Using oxymetazoline (Afrin) beyond 3 days and finding congestion is getting worse rather than better — this is the rebound-congestion pattern described above, not a sign you need a stronger dose; stop and consider saline or an intranasal corticosteroid instead while it resolves.
- A child under 6 with congestion — most oral decongestants and oxymetazoline are not labeled for this age without medical guidance; saline is the standard safe first-line option instead.
