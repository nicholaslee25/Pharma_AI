## Ordinary tension/general headache

The standard OTC toolkit is the same four ingredients compared in detail on [[oral-pain-relievers]]: **acetaminophen, ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin.** Pharmacy Times Headache Products category shares (2,475,000/month recommendations): Tylenol 25%, Advil 21%, Excedrin 19%, Motrin 10%, Aleve 9%, Bayer Aspirin 6%, BC Powder 4%, Goody's Powder 2%.

- **[[acetaminophen]]** — no anti-inflammatory effect, but avoids the GI/cardiovascular/antiplatelet risks of the NSAIDs; the only one of the four ACOG considers safe throughout pregnancy.
- **[[ibuprofen]]** or **[[naproxen]]** — an NSAID has a real edge if there's a muscular/tension component with actual inflammation (e.g., neck/shoulder tension contributing to the headache).
- **[[aspirin]]** — comparable milligram-for-milligram analgesia to acetaminophen per the Cochrane data cited on [[naproxen]], but carries aspirin's specific risks (irreversible antiplatelet effect, Reye syndrome in children/teens — see [[red-flags]]).
- **Caffeine-combination products** (Excedrin, BC Powder, Goody's Powder — see [[substitutions-and-duplicates]] for how these three relate) add caffeine to acetaminophen+aspirin, a combination with dedicated headache-specific evidence per [[acetaminophen]]/[[aspirin]].

See [[oral-pain-relievers]] for the full head-to-head efficacy (Cochrane NNT) data, duration-of-action comparison, and the ibuprofen/low-dose-aspirin timing interaction that matters if you're on daily aspirin for heart protection.

## Sinus-pressure or congestion-related headache

If the headache is accompanied by nasal/sinus congestion, treating the congestion is often more useful than a pain reliever alone. See [[nasal-decongestants]] for pseudoephedrine vs. oral phenylephrine vs. oxymetazoline, and [[nasal-congestion]] for the full symptom-first breakdown. Several combination products (Advil Cold & Sinus, Tylenol Sinus Severe, Mucinex D) pair a decongestant with an analgesic specifically for this presentation — formulation not individually re-verified per SKU in this pass.

## Migraine specifically

Migraine Headache Products category (1,809,454/month recommendations): Excedrin Migraine 65%, Advil Migraine 13%, Tylenol 8%, Aleve 7%, Hyland's Migraine Relief 3%, Motrin Migraine 3%, MagniLife Migraine Relief 1%.

- **[[Excedrin Migraine]]** (acetaminophen 250mg + aspirin 250mg + caffeine 65mg — the same formulation as plain Excedrin and Excedrin Tension Headache, see [[substitutions-and-duplicates]]) carries dedicated brand-specific RCT evidence (Lipton et al. 1998, PMID 9482363, 3 placebo-controlled trials) and markets itself as "the only OTC medication with FDA approval specifically for the treatment of migraine."
- **Advil Migraine and Motrin Migraine** (both solubilized ibuprofen 200mg, confirmed identical — see [[ibuprofen]] and [[substitutions-and-duplicates]]) also carry an FDA label "Treats migraine" use claim — a real, **unresolved discrepancy** this database has flagged but not independently adjudicated: Excedrin Migraine's marketing claims exclusivity as the only FDA-approved migraine OTC option, while two ibuprofen-based competitors carry the same "Treats migraine" label language. See [[ibuprofen]] for the full flag.
- Hyland's Migraine Relief and MagniLife Migraine Relief are homeopathic products in this database's broader research pattern — their formulation/evidence was not independently verified in this pass; do not assume they carry equivalent evidence to the three products above.

A genuine migraine (throbbing, often one-sided, with nausea and light/sound sensitivity) is a different clinical entity from tension headache, and recurring migraines are generally better managed with a doctor's guidance on prevention, not repeated OTC dosing — especially since frequent use of any acute headache medication (including OTC analgesics) can itself cause medication-overuse headache, a pattern this database has not independently sourced in depth but is worth flagging as a known general concern.

## Seek care if — pointer to Layer 5

From this database's own sourced content (see [[red-flags]] for full detail):
- Any rash, hives, facial/throat swelling, or difficulty breathing after taking a pain reliever — possible anaphylaxis or a serious drug reaction (SJS/TEN/DRESS) — **emergency**.
- A child or teenager with a current/recent viral illness (flu, chickenpox) — never give aspirin or a bismuth-subsalicylate product (Pepto-Bismol/Kaopectate) for headache; use acetaminophen or ibuprofen instead — see [[red-flags]] Reye syndrome section.
- Headache not improving within the OTC label's stated duration (commonly 7–10 days of intermittent use), or worsening — stop and see a doctor per standard NSAID/acetaminophen labeling.
- Chest pain, vision changes, confusion, slurred speech, or one-sided weakness alongside a headache — **emergency**, per general NSAID/decongestant "ask a doctor" cardiovascular cautions on [[ibuprofen]]/[[pseudoephedrine]].

**Beyond this database's own sourced content:** a sudden, severe "worst headache of my life," a headache following a head injury, or a headache with fever, stiff neck, rash, or confusion are well-established emergency-medicine warning signs (possible hemorrhage, meningitis, or stroke) — but these are general clinical knowledge, not independently sourced from an OTC drug label or study in this database. They're noted here because leaving them out would create a real safety gap in a symptom-first page, not because a specific citation was pulled for this pass.
