## The two OTC mechanisms

Pharmacy Times Antidiarrheals category (1,265,900/month recommendations): Imodium 81%, Pepto-Bismol 10%, DiaResQ 6%, Kaopectate 3%.

- **[[Loperamide]]** (Imodium A-D) — a peripherally-restricted opioid-receptor agonist that slows intestinal transit directly. FDA-approved for traveler's diarrhea, IBS with chronic diarrhea, and acute nonspecific diarrhea (≥2 years). At labeled doses, oral bioavailability is under 1% — almost no active drug reaches systemic/CNS circulation, which is the pharmacokinetic reason this opioid-class drug is safe OTC at normal doses.
- **[[Bismuth-subsalicylate]]** (Pepto-Bismol, and Kaopectate — a confirmed identical 525mg/30mL formulation under a different brand name, see [[substitutions-and-duplicates]]) — works via three antidiarrheal pathways (COX inhibition reducing prostaglandins, stimulated fluid/electrolyte reabsorption, inhibited intestinal secretion) plus a genuine antimicrobial action against some causative bacteria. Broader-spectrum than loperamide in one sense: it's FDA-approved for diarrhea, dyspepsia, heartburn, nausea, and upset stomach all at once, and holds 55% of the separate Upset Stomach Remedies category.

**Choosing between them:** loperamide is the more targeted, purely-antimotility option; bismuth subsalicylate is the broader-symptom-coverage option but carries a real, additional consideration since it's chemically a salicylate (see below).

## Real, serious risks worth knowing before reaching for either

- **Loperamide carries an FDA Boxed Warning** for torsades de pointes and sudden death **at higher-than-recommended doses** — this is specifically a high-dose/misuse risk (driven by non-medical abuse for opioid-withdrawal self-management or euphoria-seeking; a 91% rise in reported exposures 2010–2015 including 8 deaths prompted the FDA's 2019 package-size restrictions), not a typical-dose concern given the sub-1% bioavailability noted above. Still, never exceed the labeled dose (max 8mg/24h OTC for adults).
- **Bismuth subsalicylate is a salicylate** — the same drug family as aspirin, and it carries the **same Reye syndrome risk in children/teens with a current or recent viral illness (flu, chickenpox)** — see [[red-flags]]. It also interacts with anticoagulants, methotrexate, and other salicylate-containing products, and reduces doxycycline absorption.
- **Both are contraindicated with bloody or black stools, or diarrhea associated with a bacterial infection** — per [[loperamide]]'s label, and bismuth subsalicylate's label similarly warns against use with bleeding problems or bloody/black stools (though bismuth subsalicylate itself normally, harmlessly, darkens stool/tongue color — a distinction worth knowing so a patient doesn't mistake expected drug-color-change for GI bleeding, or vice versa).
- **Neither should substitute for oral rehydration** in a case of significant fluid loss — see [[oral-rehydration-electrolytes]] for the actual rehydration-science-backed product category, which addresses the dehydration risk itself rather than just symptom suppression.

## Pediatric-specific notes

Loperamide is contraindicated under age 2 and generally not recommended for infectious diarrhea in pediatric patients even where age-appropriate dosing exists (ages 6–11). Bismuth subsalicylate carries limited pediatric dosing data under 12 per StatPearls, plus the Reye syndrome consideration above — other antidiarrheal approaches (oral rehydration, dietary modification) may be preferable for children specifically, consistent with general pediatric-diarrhea management practice.

## Seek care if — pointer to Layer 5

From this database's own sourced content (see [[red-flags]] for full detail):
- Bloody or black stools, or diarrhea with high fever — a possible bacterial or serious cause that antimotility/antidiarrheal drugs are specifically labeled against treating.
- Signs of significant dehydration (very low urine output, dizziness, extreme thirst, lethargy) — needs oral rehydration and possibly medical evaluation, not just an antidiarrheal.
- A child or teenager with a current/recent viral illness — never give bismuth subsalicylate (or aspirin) — see [[red-flags]] Reye syndrome section.
- Diarrhea persisting beyond the OTC label's stated duration (commonly 2 days) without improvement.
- Any suspicion of high-dose or repeated loperamide misuse, or cardiac symptoms (palpitations, fainting) after taking loperamide — this is the drug's own documented Boxed Warning presentation and warrants emergency evaluation.
