## Three symptom targets, three product classes

Seasonal allergies present with three distinct symptom clusters, and the best-evidenced OTC option differs by which one dominates:

1. **General sneezing/itching/runny nose:** oral second-generation antihistamines (Zyrtec, Claritin, Allegra, Xyzal, Alavert) — see [[oral-antihistamines]] for the full comparison.
2. **Nasal congestion/blockage specifically:** intranasal corticosteroids (Flonase and siblings) — per [[intranasal-corticosteroids]], StatPearls states these are **superior to antihistamines specifically at reducing nasal inflammation** and more effective than antihistamines at relieving sneezing, rhinorrhea, and nasal blockage.
3. **Itchy/watery eyes specifically:** ophthalmic (eye-drop) antihistamines (Pataday, Zaditor, Alaway, Lastacaft) — a topical, eye-only approach when ocular symptoms are the main complaint.

**A genuinely useful finding for anyone with multi-symptom allergies:** the Sousa-Pinto et al. 2024 meta-analysis (151 studies, cited on [[intranasal-corticosteroids]]) found that **azelastine-fluticasone (an antihistamine+steroid combination nasal spray) had the highest probability of moderate-large improvement for eye symptoms specifically** — outperforming fluticasone alone on the ocular domain — while fluticasone furoate and fluticasone propionate alone led on nasal-symptom scores. No single product class wins on every symptom domain.

---

## First-generation vs. second-generation — still the biggest single decision

Per [[oral-antihistamines]], this is the largest, best-evidenced distinction in the oral-antihistamine category: first-generation diphenhydramine (Benadryl) crosses the blood-brain barrier readily, causing significant sedation and anticholinergic burden, and is specifically flagged by the AGS Beers Criteria for avoidance in most/all older adults except for acute severe allergic reactions. Second-generation agents (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine, levocetirizine) avoid most of this burden and are the better default for routine, ongoing seasonal-allergy control.

---

## Does the specific oral antihistamine brand matter?

Per the Vieira et al. 2026 network meta-analysis (74 RCTs) cited on [[oral-antihistamines]]: **differences between individual second-generation antihistamines are mostly trivial or small**, though cetirizine trended slightly favorably for nasal symptoms specifically. Certainty of evidence for most head-to-head comparisons was rated low or very low — so brand loyalty within this class is defensible, but not because one has been clearly proven superior to the others across the board.

---

## Onset-of-action tradeoff: oral antihistamines vs. nasal steroids

This is a genuinely practical distinction worth knowing before choosing: oral second-generation antihistamines act relatively quickly (cetirizine: 20–60 minutes, persisting ≥24h), while **intranasal corticosteroids need to be used regularly and consistently — peak effect may take multiple days to develop**, per StatPearls, even though the Flonase label notes some relief may begin day 1. A nasal steroid is not a same-day rescue option the way an antihistamine can be; it's built for sustained, regular use through an allergy season.

## Safety notes

- **Intranasal corticosteroids** carry a real pediatric growth-rate monitoring recommendation (children 4–11y using beyond 2 months/year) and should prompt medical advice if the user is exposed to chickenpox, measles, or tuberculosis while using the product — an immunosuppressive-adjacent caution specific to this class.
- **Ophthalmic antihistamines** are well-tolerated overall — no serious adverse events identified across the sources reviewed, with mild transient stinging on instillation the most common effect.
- **Pregnancy:** ACOG/ACAAI support cetirizine when an antihistamine is genuinely needed, though some guidance still favors first-generation antihistamines as the historically "safest" option in pregnancy specifically — see [[pregnancy]] for the fuller, preserved tension.
- **Combination products** (e.g., Zyrtec-D, cetirizine + pseudoephedrine) add a decongestant with its own separate risk profile — see [[pseudoephedrine]] and [[nasal-decongestants]].

## Seek care if — pointer to Layer 5

From this database's own sourced content (see [[red-flags]] for full detail):
- Any rash, hives, facial/throat swelling, or difficulty breathing — possible anaphylaxis, not ordinary seasonal allergy — emergency.
- Nasal-steroid symptoms not improving within 7 days, a constant nasal whistling sound, vision changes, or severe/frequent nosebleeds — per the Flonase label's own stop-and-see-a-doctor criteria.
- Eye symptoms with significant pain, light sensitivity, or vision change — beyond simple allergic itch, warrants an eye-care evaluation rather than continued self-treatment with an OTC eye drop.
