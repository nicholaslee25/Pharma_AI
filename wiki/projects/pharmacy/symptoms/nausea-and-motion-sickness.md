## Three overlapping categories, different mechanisms

Pharmacy Times tracks these as three separate categories with heavily overlapping brands: Nausea Remedies (1,704,832/month: Dramamine Nausea 36%, Emetrol 32%, Pepto-Bismol 16%, Alka-Seltzer 7%, Nauzene 5%, Sea-Band 4%), Upset Stomach Remedies (2,398,739/month: Pepto-Bismol 55%, Emetrol 21%, Alka-Seltzer 10%, Kaopectate 9%, Nauzene 5%), and Motion Sickness Remedies (1,384,034/month: Dramamine Motion Sickness 62%, Bonine 24%, Sea-Band Nausea Relief 6%, Emetrol 5%, Motioneaze 2%, Nauzene 1%).

- **General nausea/upset stomach:** [[bismuth-subsalicylate]] (Pepto-Bismol, Kaopectate — see [[diarrhea]] for its full profile) is the dominant option, effective across nausea, dyspepsia, heartburn, and diarrhea simultaneously.
- **Motion sickness specifically:** two chemically distinct first-generation antihistamines, [[dimenhydrinate]] and [[meclizine]], both act centrally on the vestibular system.

## The "Dramamine" brand-name trap — a genuine three-mechanism finding

**"Dramamine" spans at least three mechanistically distinct products** — the most extensive one-brand-multiple-mechanisms finding in this database's GI/nausea research:
- **Dramamine Motion Sickness** (62% of the Motion Sickness Remedies category) = [[dimenhydrinate]] 50mg, dosed every 6–8 hours.
- **Dramamine Nausea** (Long Lasting Formula, 36% of Nausea Remedies) = [[meclizine]] 25mg, dosed once daily — a genuinely different, longer-acting, generally less-sedating molecule than dimenhydrinate despite the shared "Dramamine" name.
- Dramamine Advanced Herbals/Non-Drowsy Naturals = ginger root extract, a non-drug herbal line, not independently researched in this database.

**Bonine** (24% of Motion Sickness Remedies) is a second confirmed meclizine 25mg product, directly competing with Dramamine Nausea on the identical active ingredient and dose — Bonine's own label claims "less drowsy vs. Dramamine® dimenhydrinate," a comparison against the *other* Dramamine molecule, not the meclizine-based one, worth reading carefully given the brand confusion this creates.

## Dimenhydrinate vs. meclizine — which one for motion sickness

| | [[Dimenhydrinate]] | [[Meclizine]] |
|---|---|---|
| Dosing frequency | Every 6–8h | Once daily (up to 24h duration) |
| Sedation | More sedating | Generally less sedating |
| Pediatric age cutoff | Down to age 2 (Dramamine for Kids) | Not recommended under 12 |
| Direct efficacy trial | Strongest located RCT (Uhlig 2009) studied pediatric **gastroenteritis-related vomiting**, not motion sickness specifically — authors' own conclusion: "overall benefit is LOW" since it didn't improve rehydration success or clinical outcome despite reducing vomiting frequency (69.6% vomiting-free vs. 47.4% placebo) | No dedicated motion-sickness RCT with hard efficacy numbers was located in this pass — evidence rests on established StatPearls mechanism/dosing rather than a specific trial |

**Stated plainly, as both ingredient pages state it:** neither has a dedicated, rigorous motion-sickness-specific efficacy trial with hard numbers in this database's sources — a real evidence gap for an extremely commonly used OTC indication, not papered over here. Both have an established pharmacologic mechanism (central H1/anticholinergic blockade of vestibular signaling) that makes clinical sense and is the basis of their FDA OTC approval, but "makes mechanistic sense and is FDA-approved" is a different claim from "has a dedicated RCT proving it works for this specific use."

## A non-drug option worth naming

**Sea-Band** (acupressure wristband, 4–6% share across both nausea/motion-sickness categories) is a real, non-drug mechanical alternative — per this database's own brand research, it carries a "weak/inconsistent independent evidence base," a genuinely different (and less certain) evidence picture than either antihistamine, despite sitting in the same product category.

## Safety notes

- **Anticholinergic side effects** (drowsiness, dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention) are shared by both dimenhydrinate and meclizine — the same drug-class mechanism, just different molecules.
- **Paradoxical excitation** (the opposite of expected sedation) is specifically documented in children on dimenhydrinate — a real counseling point since parents may not anticipate it.
- **Pregnancy:** meclizine's own sourced caution notes some older monograph language describes it as contraindicated in women who are or may become pregnant — flagged as a real, sourced caution not independently re-adjudicated against current consensus in this pass. Per [[pregnancy]], doxylamine is StatPearls' generally-preferred antihistamine in pregnancy for nausea, with dimenhydrinate/meclizine not independently addressed with pregnancy-specific data.
- **Bismuth subsalicylate** carries the Reye syndrome consideration in children/teens with viral illness — see [[diarrhea]] and [[red-flags]].
- Do not combine either antihistamine with alcohol, sedatives, or other anticholinergic drugs — additive CNS depression, a shared class-level caution.

## Seek care if — pointer to Layer 5

From this database's own sourced content (see [[red-flags]] for full detail):
- Persistent vomiting preventing fluid intake, or signs of dehydration — needs oral rehydration/medical evaluation beyond an antiemetic.
- Nausea/vomiting accompanied by severe abdominal pain, chest pain, or a severe headache — a possible sign of a more serious underlying condition than simple motion sickness or upset stomach.
- A child or teenager with a current/recent viral illness given bismuth subsalicylate — Reye syndrome risk, use an alternative.
- Vomiting blood or black/tarry vomit — emergency, do not self-treat with an OTC antiemetic.
