## The adult toolkit

All four ingredients compared in detail on [[oral-pain-relievers]] are FDA-approved for fever reduction, not just pain: [[acetaminophen]] (Tylenol), [[ibuprofen]] (Advil/Motrin), [[naproxen]] (Aleve), and [[aspirin]] (Bayer). The efficacy/safety tradeoffs are the same ones detailed on that page (NSAID GI/cardiovascular/antiplatelet risk vs. acetaminophen's liver-toxicity ceiling) — see [[oral-pain-relievers]] for the full head-to-head comparison rather than repeating it here.

**One fever-specific consideration:** naproxen's longer half-life (12–17h, supporting q8–12h dosing per [[oral-pain-relievers]]) can mean fewer doses to maintain fever control over a day compared to ibuprofen or acetaminophen's q4–6h schedules — a real practical consideration distinct from which drug lowers temperature "better," which this database's sources don't quantify head-to-head for fever specifically.

## Pediatric fever — the one that matters most

Pharmacy Times Children's Analgesics category (1,700,837/month): Children's Tylenol 57%, Children's Motrin 25%, Children's Advil 10%, DrKids 3%, Genexa 2%, Little Remedies Infant Fever + Pain Reliever 2%, FeverAll 1%.

- **Acetaminophen and ibuprofen** are both standard, appropriate pediatric fever reducers — the two dominant options by a wide margin (82% combined share).
- **Never aspirin.** This is this database's single most emphasized pediatric safety rule: aspirin (or any bismuth-subsalicylate product, since it's also a salicylate) given to a child or teenager with a current or recent viral illness (flu, chickenpox) carries a real Reye syndrome risk — encephalopathy with fatty liver degeneration, **30–45% mortality**. Per [[aspirin]], Reye syndrome incidence has fallen substantially precisely *because* of the shift to acetaminophen/ibuprofen for pediatric fever — this is a genuine public-health success story, not just a label warning. See [[red-flags]] for the full detail.
- **FeverAll** is a genuinely different administration route worth knowing about — a rectal acetaminophen suppository (80mg/120mg/325mg by age tier), useful specifically when a child can't keep oral medication down.
- **Concentration standardization:** per [[acetaminophen]], the pediatric liquid concentration was standardized industry-wide to 160mg/5mL (Children's Tylenol, Genexa, DrKids, and — despite "Infant" branding — Little Remedies Infant Fever/Pain Reliever are all confirmed at this same concentration). This standardization happened specifically because a prior era's dual concentrations (a more concentrated "infant drops" formulation alongside a less concentrated children's syrup) caused real dosing-error and overdose incidents — worth knowing if an older bottle or an unfamiliar brand is ever in the house, since assuming "infant" automatically means a different, more concentrated dose is exactly the assumption that caused problems before the standardization.

## What temperature actually needs treatment

This database's own sourced ingredient pages document dosing and mechanism for fever reduction but do not independently source a specific temperature threshold for when fever itself requires treatment or becomes an emergency — **stated here plainly as a gap rather than inventing a number.** General pediatric and emergency-medicine guidance on when a fever needs urgent evaluation (very young infant age cutoffs, specific high temperatures, fever duration) is well-established clinical knowledge, but treat any specific threshold you've heard elsewhere as something to confirm with a pediatrician or your child's actual history, not something this database has independently verified from a primary source in this pass.

## Seek care if — pointer to Layer 5

From this database's own sourced content (see [[red-flags]] for full detail):
- A child or teenager with a current/recent viral illness — never give aspirin or a bismuth-subsalicylate product for fever; use acetaminophen or ibuprofen instead.
- Any rash, hives, facial/throat swelling, or difficulty breathing after a fever reducer — possible anaphylaxis or a serious drug reaction — emergency.
- Fever accompanied by a stiff neck, severe headache, confusion, or a rash that doesn't fade under pressure — beyond this database's own sourced OTC-label content, these are well-established general emergency-medicine warning signs (possible meningitis or a serious systemic infection), noted here because omitting them would leave a real safety gap, not because a specific citation was pulled for this pass — see [[headache]] for the same honest-labeling approach applied there.
- Fever not improving with an appropriate OTC dose within the timeframe your doctor or the product label indicates, or a very young infant with any fever — these situations warrant a call to a pediatrician rather than continued self-treatment.
