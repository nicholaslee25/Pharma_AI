## Overview
Caltrate is confirmed to use **calcium carbonate** as its calcium source (now sold by Haleon, having passed through Wyeth/Pfizer/GSK ownership), spanning multiple formulations (tablets, gummies, soft chews) including dedicated pregnancy, children's, and elderly product lines. Calcium carbonate requires stomach acid for dissolution/absorption — a real, mechanistically meaningful limitation relative to category-rival [[Citracal]]'s calcium citrate, which a controlled PK study found produced 2.5x greater serum calcium AUC than a carbonate comparator (Os-Cal) in postmenopausal women (not Caltrate by name, but the same carbonate salt class). This matters specifically for older adults (often with reduced stomach acid) and anyone on acid-suppressing medication (PPIs, H2 blockers — see [[proton-pump-inhibitors]]), who may absorb carbonate-based calcium less reliably than citrate-based alternatives; carbonate should generally be taken with food to maximize the acid available for dissolution. No brand-specific recall was located in this pass. Calcium Supplements (23%) is covered by [[calcium]].

## Pros
- Calcium carbonate delivers more elemental calcium per tablet than citrate (typically ~40% elemental calcium by weight vs. ~21% for citrate), meaning fewer tablets are needed to reach a given elemental-calcium target — a real practical/cost advantage when absorption conditions are favorable (taken with food, normal stomach acid)
- States a Supplier Quality Management System for raw-material quality/purity oversight
- Broad product-line coverage (pregnancy, children's, elderly-specific formulations) under one brand
- No recall or lawsuit history was found for this brand in this pass

## Cons
- **Calcium carbonate requires stomach acid for dissolution** — a real, sourced absorption limitation not shared by [[Citracal]]'s calcium-citrate formulation, particularly relevant for older adults and PPI/H2-blocker users; must be taken with food to maximize absorption, unlike citrate which can be taken with or without food
- No Caltrate-brand-specific comparative absorption trial was located (the located citrate-vs-carbonate PK study used Os-Cal, not Caltrate, as its carbonate comparator) — the absorption disadvantage is an ingredient-class finding applied to Caltrate via its confirmed carbonate formulation, not a Caltrate-named trial
- No independent third-party quality certification (USP Verified, NSF) was located for Caltrate in this pass beyond its own stated internal quality-management system

## Use Cases
Calcium supplementation — best absorbed when taken with food given its carbonate form's acid-dependence; see [[calcium]] for the general evidence base.

## Allergies / Warnings
See [[calcium]] — take 2+ hours apart from dolutegravir, levothyroxine, lithium, or quinolone antibiotics to avoid absorption interference. Take with food to maximize carbonate absorption.

## Symptoms Treated
Not applicable in the drug sense — dietary supplements are not FDA-indicated to treat symptoms or disease; see [[calcium]] for the specific evidence-supported use (bone health).

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
No discrepancy found, but a real, worth-flagging formulation contrast with its category rival: Caltrate's calcium carbonate is confirmed to have a real absorption limitation (acid-dependence) relative to [[Citracal]]'s calcium citrate, per the ingredient-class evidence — this doesn't make Caltrate ineffective, but it does mean the two leading brands in this category are not interchangeable in absorption profile, and Caltrate's should be taken with food to perform as intended.
