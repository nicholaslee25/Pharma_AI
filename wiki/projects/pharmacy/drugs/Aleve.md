## Overview
Confirmed via DailyMed (per [[naproxen]]'s existing entry): Aleve's active ingredient is **naproxen sodium 220 mg**, pain reliever/fever reducer, manufactured by Bayer. **Real, genuinely unresolved regulatory history found in this pass:** in 2004, the FDA issued a warning after the NIH halted a colon-adenoma-prevention clinical trial (~2,500 patients) that found naproxen use (2 tablets/day for 3 years) associated with a 50% higher risk of heart attack/stroke (~70 of 2,500 patients suffered stroke or MI; 23 died) — leading to the FDA's recommendation that Aleve not be used for more than 10 days without medical direction. However, a **2014 FDA joint advisory committee** later reviewed post-2005 cardiovascular data specifically comparing naproxen to other NSAIDs, and many committee members felt the evidence suggested naproxen might carry a *more favorable* (lower) cardiovascular risk profile than other NSAIDs — but the committee's official (non-unanimous) recommendation was that the data did **not** meet the evidentiary bar to support a formal label claim that naproxen has lower thrombotic risk than other NSAIDs. The FDA's subsequent July 2015 label update strengthened the heart attack/stroke warning uniformly across all non-aspirin NSAIDs rather than treating naproxen differently. **Bottom line, presented without resolving the tension:** naproxen has both a well-documented CV-risk-elevation history (the 2004 trial finding) AND a contested, debated body of more recent evidence some experts read as relatively favorable compared to other NSAIDs — the FDA's own official position, after review, does not endorse naproxen as safer, and current labeling treats it the same as other non-aspirin NSAIDs for this specific warning.

## Pros
- Confirmed FDA Drug Facts label with a well-established NSAID active ingredient, backed by [[naproxen]]'s Cochrane postoperative-pain evidence (Mason 2004, 996 participants, NNT 2.6 for naproxen sodium 550mg)
- Naproxen's longer half-life (12–17h vs. ibuprofen's ~2h, per [[naproxen]]) means less frequent dosing (q8–12h) — a genuine convenience advantage for chronic arthritis-type pain over shorter-acting NSAIDs
- Leading share of Oral Anti-Inflammatory Products (24%, tied with Advil) and Oral Arthritis Pain Relievers (20%) in this database

## Cons
- **The 2004 FDA cardiovascular warning is real and brand-relevant** — a halted NIH trial found a 50% higher heart attack/stroke risk with naproxen at chronic, above-OTC-label doses (2 tablets/day for 3 years, higher than the 660mg/day OTC max); this triggered the FDA's "not more than 10 days" OTC guidance still reflected in [[naproxen]]'s dosing table
- The subsequent "naproxen may be safer for the heart" narrative that circulated after 2014 was NOT formally endorsed by the FDA's own advisory committee vote — presenting it as settled fact would overstate the evidence; the honest picture is contested and unresolved
- Naproxen's longer duration of action also means a longer post-dose adverse-event exposure window (time to remedication 7.6h vs. placebo's 2.6h per the Cochrane data on [[naproxen]]) compared to shorter-acting NSAIDs
- Carries the full class-wide NSAID risk profile (GI bleed, renal risk, CV risk) documented on [[naproxen]] and [[ibuprofen]]

## Use Cases
General analgesia, fever reduction, and anti-inflammatory relief — arthritis pain, muscular aches, backache, menstrual cramps, headache, toothache, cold-symptom aches. Longer dosing interval makes it a common choice for all-day/overnight arthritis pain control.

## Allergies / Warnings
Per label: NSAIDs (except aspirin) increase risk of heart attack, heart failure, and stroke, which can be fatal — risk increases with longer use; do not use for more than 10 days unless directed by a doctor; ask a doctor/pharmacist before use if taking a blood thinner, another NSAID/aspirin-containing product, or consuming ≥3 alcoholic drinks/day. Full contraindication/special-population detail per [[naproxen]] applies (avoid ≥30 weeks pregnancy, CrCl <30 avoid, age ≥60 flagged as a stomach-bleeding risk factor).
