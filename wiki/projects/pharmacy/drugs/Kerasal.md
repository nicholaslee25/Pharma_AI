## Overview
**Kerasal Intensive Foot Repair Ointment** (the SKU relevant to this category — distinct from Kerasal's separate Athlete's Foot antifungal line, which uses tolnaftate) contains **Salicylic Acid ~5% + Urea ~10%** in a soft white petrolatum base, per the brand's own ingredient list and multiple third-party retail/pharmacy sources (kerasal.com, drugs.com) — not independently pulled from a DailyMed label in this pass, so treat the exact percentages as reported-not-confirmed. Salicylic acid is covered at [[salicylic-acid]]; urea has no dedicated ingredient page in this database (a gap, consistent with the general pattern of urea/barrier-type foot-care ingredients not yet built out — see also [[Dr Scholl's Ultra Hydrating]] and [[Flexitol Heel Balm]]). **Real discrepancy flagged (also noted on [[salicylic-acid]]):** that ingredient page's Compound W label explicitly contraindicates salicylic acid use "if you have diabetes or circulatory problems" — yet Kerasal Intensive Foot Repair is positioned and heavily recommended (5% share) specifically within the "Diabetic Foot Cream" category, marketed toward the same population the wart-remover label warns against. A podiatry trade-publication commentary piece (two podiatrists' clinical opinion, not a controlled trial) states the concentration in Kerasal's foot ointment "does not appear to have deleterious effects in the diabetic patient" and that one of the two "suggests it to almost every diabetic patient" — but this is expert opinion based on clinical experience, not a resolution of the underlying labeled warning, and the two products differ in salicylic acid concentration (Compound W 17% for wart removal vs. Kerasal's ~5% for keratolytic exfoliation) and indication. Both facts are preserved here rather than silently reconciled.

## Pros
- Combines a keratolytic (salicylic acid) with a humectant (urea) and an occlusive (petrolatum) — a mechanistically complementary three-part approach to thick, cracked heel skin
- Long-standing use in podiatric practice per the clinical commentary found (one podiatrist reporting over a decade of recommending it with no observed side effects)
- Lower salicylic acid concentration (~5%) than wart-removal products (~17%), plausibly reducing (but not eliminating, absent a trial) systemic-absorption risk

## Cons
- **Real, unresolved labeling discrepancy:** salicylic acid's own well-documented OTC label (Compound W) warns against use in diabetes/circulatory-impairment patients specifically — this product's core market position and pharmacist-recommendation base is diabetic foot care, warranting explicit awareness of this conflict, not a "no discrepancy" reading
- Percentages not confirmed via an independently pulled DailyMed label in this pass — treat as reported-but-not-primary-source-confirmed
- No controlled trial (RCT) evidence specific to this ointment in diabetic populations was found — only physician clinical-experience commentary
- Salicylic acid's own systemic-toxicity profile (see [[salicylic-acid]]) is a real, if concentration-dependent, consideration for any keratolytic in patients with impaired healing/circulation

## Use Cases
Thick, dry, cracked heel skin and calluses, marketed specifically toward diabetic foot-care routines.

## Allergies / Warnings
External use only; do not use on irritated, infected, or opened skin; avoid eye contact (rinse thoroughly if exposed); stop use and ask a doctor if redness, discomfort, or irritation develops. Per [[salicylic-acid]]'s more general labeling data: diabetes and circulatory problems are treated as contraindications on at least one major salicylic-acid product's own FDA label (Compound W) — patients with diabetes or neuropathy should not self-exfoliate without consulting a doctor first, per general foot-care guidance (see [[diabetic-foot-care]]).

## Symptoms Treated
Dry, cracked, thickened skin on heels and feet; callus softening.
