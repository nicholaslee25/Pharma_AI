## Overview
Confirmed via DailyMed: Alocane Max's own FDA OTC Drug Facts label lists **benzalkonium chloride 4% (first aid antiseptic) and lidocaine HCl 4% (external analgesic)** as active ingredients — 4% is the FDA-permitted maximum lidocaine concentration for OTC products. This is a genuinely different antiseptic chemistry from every other antiseptic-class brand in this database: benzalkonium chloride is a **quaternary ammonium compound**, mechanistically distinct from both bacitracin/neomycin/polymyxin (peptide antibiotics, see [[topical-antibiotics]]) and chlorhexidine gluconate/povidone-iodine (see [[Hibiclens]]/[[Betadine]]) — meaning Alocane's 2% share of "Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics" reflects an antiseptic+anesthetic combination product, not a topical antibiotic at all, despite sharing a category with Neosporin/Bacitraycin Plus/Polysporin. The lidocaine component links directly to [[topical-anesthetics]], where lidocaine's much lower methemoglobinemia risk relative to benzocaine is already documented. No brand-specific clinical trial for Alocane specifically was identified beyond the general benzalkonium chloride/lidocaine ingredient-level evidence.

## Pros
- Confirmed FDA Drug Facts label with a clearly disclosed dual-action formula: antiseptic (infection-risk reduction) plus anesthetic (pain relief) in one product, addressing two burn/sunburn symptom needs at once
- Uses lidocaine rather than benzocaine for the anesthetic component — the safer choice per the methemoglobinemia data on [[topical-anesthetics]]
- Present across three related but distinct categories (Burn Treatments, Sun Burn Relief, Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics), reflecting one consistent formula's applicability across overlapping minor-skin-injury use cases rather than three different products

## Cons
- Benzalkonium chloride is a genuinely different antiseptic mechanism than the bacitracin/neomycin/polymyxin combination products (Neosporin, Polysporin, Bacitraycin Plus) it's benchmarked against in the Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics category — comparing Alocane's real-world performance to those products' evidence base (e.g., the Smack 1996 RCT on [[petrolatum]]/[[topical-antibiotics]]) is not a like-for-like comparison
- Smallest share (2%) in the category where its mechanism differs most from its peers
- No brand-specific RCT identified; evidence basis is entirely ingredient-level (benzalkonium chloride antisepsis, lidocaine anesthesia) rather than a tested combination-product outcome trial

## Use Cases
First aid antiseptic plus temporary pain relief for minor cuts, scrapes, and first-degree burns (flame, sun, electrical, heat, light, friction) per label.

## Allergies / Warnings
Per label: for external use only; do not use if allergic to lidocaine or other local anesthetics of the amide class; discontinue and consult a doctor if condition worsens, symptoms persist beyond 7 days, or signs of infection develop. Benzalkonium chloride carries its own rare but documented contact-sensitivity risk, distinct from the antibiotic-allergy risk profile of Neosporin/Polysporin/Bacitraycin Plus.

## Symptoms Treated
Pain, burning, and infection risk from minor cuts, scrapes, and first-degree burns (including sunburn) — dual antiseptic + anesthetic action.

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
**Discrepancy flagged on this page and [[topical-antibiotics]]:** Alocane's inclusion in the "Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics" pharmacist-recommendation category is accurate in the broad "antiseptic" sense but should not be read as evidence-equivalent to the bacitracin/neomycin/polymyxin-based products dominating that same category — a genuinely different antiseptic chemical class (quaternary ammonium vs. peptide antibiotic) combined with an anesthetic most of its category-mates lack.
