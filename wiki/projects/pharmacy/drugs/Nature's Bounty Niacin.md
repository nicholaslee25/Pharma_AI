## Overview
**Real, significant discrepancy found here.** Nature's Bounty Niacin (500mg) is confirmed to be a **"flush-free" formulation using inositol hexanicotinate**, not standard nicotinic acid (the form with actual established prescription-level cholesterol evidence). This is a genuinely well-documented pharmacological problem, not a minor hedge: a controlled head-to-head comparison found inositol hexanicotinate "showed no evidence of bioavailability" (researchers could not detect meaningful free niacin in the blood after dosing) and performed "no better than placebo" for lipid improvement; a separate crossover trial (n=59) found no significant effect on total cholesterol and a slight HDL *decrease*; and a peer-reviewed 2006 Preventive Cardiology paper is literally titled **"Flush-Free Niacin: Dietary Supplement May Be Benefit-Free."** NIH's own sourcing confirms inositol hexanicotinate absorption runs ~30% lower than standard niacin/nicotinamide. The flush caused by standard niacin and its cholesterol-modifying effect share an overlapping mechanism (prostaglandin-mediated vasodilation tied to nicotinic acid's action) — removing the flush by using a different chemical form appears to have removed the therapeutic effect along with it. This directly undercuts the product's placement in this database's Cholesterol Management category (3% share).

## Pros
- Avoids the uncomfortable skin-flushing side effect of standard immediate-release niacin — a real, if consequential (see Cons), tolerability trade-off
- Provides vitamin B3 (niacin) nutritionally, independent of any specific cholesterol claim
- Gluten-free, no artificial flavors/colors, per label

## Cons
- **Real, well-documented, and significant discrepancy: the "flush-free" form (inositol hexanicotinate) this product uses has NOT been shown to meaningfully lower cholesterol** — a controlled comparison found no detectable bioavailability of free niacin and no better-than-placebo lipid effect; another trial found a slight HDL decrease; and an academic paper is literally titled "Flush-Free Niacin: Dietary Supplement May Be Benefit-Free"
- This directly contradicts the product's placement in a "Cholesterol Management" category — the flush-free chemistry that makes this product tolerable is the same reason it likely does not deliver the cholesterol benefit associated with standard niacin
- Standard (flushing) niacin has real prescription-level evidence for lipid modification, but at doses/forms that carry their own real risks (flushing, hepatotoxicity at high doses) not applicable to this specific flush-free product — meaning neither this product's safety profile nor its efficacy profile matches standard niacin's evidence
- No independent third-party quality certification (USP Verified, NSF) was located specifically for this product in this pass

## Use Cases
Vitamin B3 (niacin) nutritional supplementation — **not well-supported for cholesterol management specifically**, despite its category placement, per the inositol hexanicotinate evidence described above.

## Allergies / Warnings
Standard niacin (nicotinic acid) is associated with flushing and, at high supplemental/prescription doses, potential hepatotoxicity — neither risk is confirmed to apply the same way to this flush-free inositol hexanicotinate form, which also appears to lack the corresponding benefit. Consult a physician before relying on this product for cholesterol management specifically.
