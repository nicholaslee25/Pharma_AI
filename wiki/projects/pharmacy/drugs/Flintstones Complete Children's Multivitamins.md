## Appears In
Children's Multivitamins - 41%

## Overview
Confirmed via manufacturer labeling: Flintstones Complete spans **two formulations with materially different nutrient panels** — the **chewable tablet** (Vitamins A, B6, B12, C, D, E, K, Biotin, Calcium, Copper, Folic Acid, Iodine, **Iron**, Niacin, Pantothenic Acid, Riboflavin, Thiamin, Zinc) and the **gummy** (Vitamins A, C, D, E, B6, Folate, B12, Biotin, Pantothenic Acid, Iodine, Zinc — **no Vitamin K, no Calcium, no Copper, and notably no Iron**). This is the largest single-brand share (41%) in the entire Pediatrics/Children's Multivitamins category, and its own two formats are not nutritionally interchangeable — a caregiver switching from tablet to gummy format (a common real-world substitution, often for palatability) could unknowingly drop their child's only dietary iron supplementation source if the multivitamin was serving that role, given iron's frequent omission from gummy vitamin formulations generally (gummies are harder to stabilize with iron, which can also cause off-tastes/discoloration).

## Pros
- Long-established, most widely recommended pediatric multivitamin brand in this database (41% share) with a broad nutrient panel across formats
- Chewable tablet format includes iron, calcium, and vitamin K — a more nutritionally complete panel than most gummy-format competitors in this same category
- Per [[multivitamins]]'s general evidence review: no significant safety concerns were found in the large COSMOS adult RCT, a reassuring (if not pediatric-specific) safety signal for typical multivitamin-mineral dosing

## Cons
- **The tablet-vs-gummy nutrient gap (iron, calcium, vitamin K, copper present in tablets but absent from gummies) is a real, brand-specific formulation inconsistency** not disclosed by the shared "Flintstones Complete" branding — flagged here as a genuine caregiver-relevant distinction, not a minor SKU footnote, since iron deficiency is a real pediatric nutritional concern this database has not previously connected to a specific brand's format choice
- Per [[multivitamins]]'s own evidence review: general multivitamin use has NOT been shown in the large COSMOS RCT to reduce cancer, cardiovascular disease, or all-cause mortality risk (adult data; no comparably large pediatric outcome trial exists for either indication or for general child multivitamin supplementation)
- No brand-specific pediatric RCT was located; evidence is limited to per-nutrient ingredient pages (e.g. [[iron]], [[vitamin-d]], [[calcium]]) rather than a trial of this specific combination product
- Gummy formulation contains added sugars (sorbitol/glucose syrup/sucrose-based) — a general dental/caloric consideration for daily long-term pediatric use, not independently quantified in this pass

## Use Cases
General pediatric nutrient-gap supplementation; the tablet format specifically for children needing dietary iron/calcium supplementation, subject to a doctor's guidance.

## Allergies / Warnings
Keep out of reach of children — iron-containing supplements are a leading cause of fatal pediatric poisoning if a large quantity is ingested at once (accidental overdose), per general iron-supplement safety data (see [[iron]] if further detail is needed). Verify format-specific nutrient content against the actual product label before assuming tablet and gummy versions are interchangeable.

## Symptoms Treated
General nutrient-gap correction; not a treatment for any specific disease or symptom.

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
**New finding, added to both this page and [[multivitamins]]:** the tablet/gummy nutrient-panel inconsistency within a single "Flintstones Complete" brand name is a real, previously undocumented instance of the one-brand-multiple-formulations pattern recurring throughout this database — here specifically consequential because the omitted nutrient (iron) has genuine clinical relevance for pediatric deficiency risk.
