## Appears In
Eczema Care/Relief Products - 5%

## Overview
**Real brand-name/active-ingredient mismatch, actively flagged.** Confirmed via DailyMed (labeler: Eczema Honey CO LLC, Hayward, CA — product listed as "SKIN SOOTHING CREAM," NDC 62742-4190-1): the sole FDA-recognized **active ingredient is colloidal oatmeal 1%** (purpose: skin protectant) — the exact same active ingredient and concentration as [[Aveeno Eczema Therapy]] (see [[colloidal-oatmeal]]). Honey ("organic honey," unspecified varietal) appears only in the **inactive ingredients** list, alongside olive oil, almond oil, aloe vera juice, beeswax, and preservatives. A brand built around "honey" in its name and marketing is, per its own FDA label, not actually an FDA-recognized honey-based drug product at all — it is a colloidal-oatmeal skin protectant with honey as one of many inert/cosmetic additives.

**Separate brand-name-confusion finding:** a different product line, "Kenkoderm Eczema Honey Soothing Body"/"Kenkoderm Eczema Honey Intensive Soothing Balm" (labeler: Guangzhou Ariel Biotech Co., Ltd.), independently uses the words "Eczema Honey" in its own product naming and also lists colloidal oatmeal (1.0%, plus allantoin 0.5% in one SKU) as active. This is a distinct, seemingly unaffiliated brand from the Pharmacy Times-tracked "Eczema Honey" (Eczema Honey CO LLC) — a real, findable case of two unrelated-appearing product lines sharing near-identical naming on the same skin-protectant category, which could confuse a pharmacist or consumer searching by name alone.

No Eczema-Honey-brand-specific clinical trial was located. General honey-for-atopic-dermatitis trial evidence is mixed and does not clearly support this brand's actual formulation: a small (n=14) manuka-honey mechanistic study (Alangari 2017) found reduced inflammatory markers in vitro/ex vivo but was not a clinical efficacy RCT, while a pilot RCT of medical-grade Kanuka honey (Fingleton 2014) found **no evidence of efficacy above a plain aqueous cream control**. Neither trial used manuka/kanuka honey (the two varietals with any dedicated evidence), and this brand's own honey is unspecified/generic and inactive by label — so none of this honey literature actually supports or tests this specific product.

## Pros
- Confirmed FDA OTC skin-protectant active ingredient (colloidal oatmeal 1%) is the same well-evidenced ingredient used in the category-leading [[Aveeno Eczema Therapy]] — see [[colloidal-oatmeal]] for the Lisante 2017 non-inferiority RCT and other supporting trial data
- National Eczema Association Seal of Acceptance and dermatologist-tested (repeated insult patch test) claims per manufacturer, suggesting some independent safety/tolerability review, though this pass did not independently verify the seal criteria or patch-test data
- Simple, short inactive-ingredient list relative to many cosmetic moisturizers, which may reduce fragrance/preservative sensitization risk for some users (not independently quantified in this pass)

## Cons
- **Brand name centers "honey" as the active/therapeutic story, but honey is an inactive ingredient on the brand's own FDA label** — the actual active ingredient (colloidal oatmeal) is identical to competitor brands not marketed around honey at all, meaning a consumer choosing this brand specifically for honey's purported therapeutic effect is not getting an FDA-recognized honey-based drug
- The honey used is unspecified/generic, not manuka or kanuka — the only honey varietals with any published eczema-relevant trial data, and even that data is thin (one small mechanistic study, one RCT showing no efficacy advantage over plain aqueous cream)
- A separate, unaffiliated-seeming "Kenkoderm Eczema Honey" product line exists with near-identical naming, creating real potential for consumer/pharmacist brand confusion
- No Eczema-Honey-brand-specific clinical trial located; evidence is entirely at the [[colloidal-oatmeal]] ingredient level, which itself has a smaller trial base (no Cochrane review) than most other ingredients in this database
- Only 5% recommendation share — among the smallest in this category, despite prominent honey-forward marketing

## Use Cases
Temporary relief of minor skin irritation and itching due to rashes and eczema, via colloidal oatmeal's occlusive/anti-inflammatory skin-protectant mechanism (see [[colloidal-oatmeal]]) — not via any independently-active honey mechanism, per its own FDA label.

## Allergies / Warnings
Per label: external use only; avoid eyes; keep out of reach of children, contact Poison Control if swallowed; stop use and consult a doctor if condition worsens or symptoms persist beyond 7 days or clear up and reappear within a few days. Per [[colloidal-oatmeal]]: oat/cereal-grain allergy carries a theoretical (uncommon) contact-allergy risk. Additionally, this product's inactive-ingredient honey introduces a distinct, real allergen not present in most competing colloidal-oatmeal products — individuals with known bee-product/honey allergy should note it is present even though it is not the labeled active ingredient, since inactive ingredients are not exempt from allergic-reaction risk.

## Symptoms Treated
Minor skin irritation and itching from eczema and rashes, via colloidal oatmeal's skin-protectant mechanism.
