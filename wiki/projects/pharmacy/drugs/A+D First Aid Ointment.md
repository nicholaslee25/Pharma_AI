## Overview
**Discrepancy flagged.** Confirmed via DailyMed: A+D First Aid Ointment's (Bayer) own FDA OTC Drug Facts label lists **lanolin 15.5% and petrolatum 53.4%** as active ingredients, both classified as **skin protectants** — not an antibiotic, antiseptic, or anesthetic of any kind. This is mechanistically identical to the plain "A&D" product (see [[A&D]]) and to the [[petrolatum]] ingredient page's occlusive/moisture-barrier mechanism, not to any of the antimicrobial or pain-relief mechanisms that dominate the rest of the Burn Treatments category it shares 18% of with Neosporin (triple antibiotic), Bacitraycin Plus/Polysporin (antibiotic ± anesthetic), and Bactine MAX (antiseptic + anesthetic). A+D is a barrier/moisturizing ointment, not a treatment aimed at infection prevention or pain relief — its presence at #3 in this category (18% share, behind only Neosporin and Dermoplast) reflects real-world use as a soothing/protective burn-care product, consistent with StatPearls' own general burn-management note (on [[burn-treatment]]) that "petroleum gauze" is a legitimate first-degree-burn management option, but it is a fundamentally different mechanism from its higher- and lower-share category neighbors.

## Pros
- Confirmed FDA Drug Facts label; simple, well-characterized, decades-old formulation (lanolin + petrolatum)
- Petrolatum's occlusive barrier mechanism has real supporting RCT evidence for uncomplicated wound care (Smack et al. 1996, documented on [[petrolatum]]) — a moisture-retentive barrier is a legitimate, evidence-consistent approach to minor burn/wound care, not a placebo product
- No antibiotic or anesthetic ingredient means no antibiotic-allergy or methemoglobinemia/local-anesthetic risk exposure, unlike several of its Burn Treatments category neighbors

## Cons
- **Category-placement mismatch:** filed under "Burn Treatments" alongside antibiotic and anesthetic-containing products, but contains neither an antimicrobial nor a pain-relieving active ingredient — a consumer expecting infection prevention or pain relief from this specific product, based on its category placement next to Neosporin/Bacitraycin Plus/Bactine MAX, would be mistaken about what it actually does
- Lanolin carries its own known (if uncommon) contact-allergy risk, distinct from the antibiotic-allergy risks discussed elsewhere in this category
- No brand-specific clinical trial for burn care specifically was identified; the supporting evidence (Smack et al. 1996) tested plain white petrolatum in a post-surgical wound context, not this specific lanolin-petrolatum combination product in a burn context

## Use Cases
Skin protectant/moisture barrier for minor cuts, scrapes, and burns; also FDA-labeled for diaper rash prevention/treatment (per the closely related "A and D Original" label) — a use case entirely outside the Burn Treatments category context this brand is tracked under here.

## Allergies / Warnings
Per label: for external use only; discontinue if condition worsens or does not improve; some users may have lanolin contact sensitivity. Contains cod liver oil-derived vitamin A/D in inactive ingredients (fragrance-related, not itself an active ingredient).

## Symptoms Treated
Not antimicrobial or analgesic — provides a protective moisture barrier over minor burns/cuts/scrapes to support the skin's own healing environment, consistent with petrolatum's general occlusive wound-care mechanism.

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
**Real discrepancy, flagged on this page, [[A&D]], and [[burn-treatment]]:** A+D First Aid Ointment is a skin-protectant product (lanolin + petrolatum) miscategorized alongside antibiotic/anesthetic products in the Burn Treatments pharmacist-recommendation list — not a factual error in the data (pharmacists genuinely do recommend it for burns), but a mechanism distinct from every other product it's benchmarked against in that category. This should be understood as "moisture-barrier burn care," not "antimicrobial or pain-relief burn care."
