## Overview
**Correction to this database's own prior speculation, and a genuine discrepancy in its own right:** [[salicylic-acid]]'s ingredient page previously noted "ProVent Wart is plausibly salicylic-acid-based by category placement, not individually confirmed against a label." That speculation is now confirmed WRONG. ProVent Wart Remover (manufacturer: Quest Products) is a **homeopathic product containing NO salicylic acid and no FDA-monograph wart-removal active ingredient at all**. Its confirmed ingredient list: **Thuja Occidentalis 6X (HPUS)**, Chelidonium Majus extract (greater celandine), Melaleuca Alternifolia (tea tree) extract, Phytolacca Decandra (pokeweed), Ricinus Communis (castor) seed oil, and Thuja Occidentalis (cedar) leaf oil. Marketing explicitly emphasizes "100% natural oils, no harsh medicines, no acids, no chemicals, no freezing or burning" — directly contrasting itself with both other brands in this category (Compound W's salicylic acid "chemical," Dr. Scholl's Freeze Away's "freezing").

**Dilution note:** Thuja Occidentalis at 6X (1:1,000,000 dilution) is far more dilute than the 1X arnica preparations already documented as having an unsupported-efficacy evidence gap on [[homeopathic-arnica]] — meaning this product's homeopathic dilution is even further from any plausible active-molecule concentration. No independent clinical trial for this specific ingredient combination, or for Thuja Occidentalis specifically as a wart treatment at any dilution, was located in this pass. No FDA Drug Facts panel or NDC number was located for this product, consistent with its unregulated homeopathic/cosmetic status — unlike Compound W (genuine OTC monograph drug) and Dr. Scholl's Freeze Away (FDA-cleared device).

**Real, separate pricing-model flag:** retail/coupon-comparison sites list ProVent Wart Remover at approximately **$154.69 for ten 3mL bottles** (with "savings card" discounted pricing around $125), and note it is "accepted at over 35,000 pharmacies" via prescription-discount-card channels — a notably high price point and pharmacy-benefit-style distribution model for a product with no confirmed FDA-evaluated active ingredient. This pricing/distribution pattern (routing an unregulated homeopathic product through prescription-discount-card infrastructure) is a genuinely different and separately concerning finding from the efficacy-evidence gap itself, and was not previously documented in this database.

## Pros
- Chelidonium majus (greater celandine) and tea tree oil have some traditional/folk-medicine association with wart treatment, giving the formula a plausible traditional rationale even without modern clinical validation
- Marketed as painless with no freezing or chemical burning, a genuine usability differentiator for users who find salicylic acid's skin irritation or cryotherapy's freezing sensation unpleasant
- Castor oil and tea tree oil are generally well-tolerated topically at cosmetic concentrations

## Cons
- **No salicylic acid, no cryotherapy, no FDA-monograph active ingredient of any kind — a homeopathic product with no confirmed Drug Facts panel or NDC**, correcting this database's own prior placeholder assumption that it was "plausibly salicylic-acid-based"
- **No independent clinical trial located** for this ingredient combination or for Thuja Occidentalis as a wart treatment at any dilution — a meaningfully weaker evidence base than either Compound W (Cochrane-reviewed salicylic acid) or Dr. Scholl's Freeze Away (Cochrane-reviewed cryotherapy) in the same category
- Chelidonium majus (greater celandine) has documented case reports of hepatotoxicity when taken orally in concentrated form in the broader herbal-medicine literature — not independently verified as a risk at this product's topical/diluted use, but a real ingredient-safety fact worth noting given the "100% natural" marketing framing that could imply zero-risk
- **Notably high price point ($150+ for ten 3mL bottles) distributed through prescription-discount-card channels** for a product with no confirmed FDA-evaluated efficacy — a real consumer-cost concern distinct from the efficacy question itself
- Smallest confirmed share (4%) and weakest evidentiary basis of the three brands in this category
