## Overview
No FDA drug label exists — Airborne is a **dietary supplement**, not an FDA-approved drug. Manufacturer Supplement Facts (chewable tablet) confirm: vitamin A 2000 IU, vitamin C 1000mg, vitamin E 30 IU, riboflavin 2.8mg, magnesium 40mg, zinc 8mg, selenium 15mcg, manganese 3mg, plus a proprietary herbal blend (~350mg, including echinacea and ginger) and an amino acid blend. This confirms vitamin C ([[vitamin-c]]) and zinc ([[zinc]]) as real, meaningful-dose components — not previously linked at all in the hedged version of this page (there was no prior ingredient pointer). No brand-specific label to save to raw/ (standard manufacturer ingredient list, not a regulatory document).

## Pros
- Multi-nutrient formula covers vitamin C, zinc, and several other micronutrients in one product, unlike single-ingredient competitors
- Echinacea and ginger are included, though their specific doses within the "herbal blend" aren't disclosed on the label (proprietary blend) — this limits how confidently any single herbal ingredient's evidence can be applied to Airborne specifically

## Cons
- **Airborne was the subject of a well-publicized 2008 false-advertising class-action settlement** ($23.3 million, part of a total $30 million FTC-related settlement fund) over unsubstantiated cold-prevention claims — the FTC specifically found the company's "clinically proven to treat colds" claim rested on an unreliable, non-peer-reviewed 2-person study. This is a genuinely brand-specific historical fact, not an ingredient-level one, and worth flagging prominently since it directly bears on how much weight to put on this brand's marketing claims versus the underlying nutrient evidence
- **A second, more recent (2026) class-action lawsuit** alleges Airborne's front-of-package vitamin C dosage claims mislead consumers into believing the displayed amount applies to each individual gummy/tablet, when it actually reflects the full multi-unit serving size — directly relevant to [[Airborne Vitamin C]], a separately-tracked SKU in this database's High-Potency Vitamin C Supplements category
- No brand-specific clinical trial for Airborne itself was identified; see [[vitamin-c]] and [[zinc]] for the actual ingredient-level evidence, which shows only modest effects (vitamin C: ~8% cold-duration reduction in adults; zinc: more meaningful effect if started within 24-48h of symptom onset, per those pages) — not the strong prevention effect implied by "Immune Support" marketing
- Proprietary blend dosing (echinacea, ginger, amino acids) is not disclosed, so individual ingredient doses can't be checked against their own evidence bases

## Use Cases
General multi-nutrient immune support; not established as a cold-prevention product despite historical marketing to that effect (see Cons).

## Allergies / Warnings
No FDA-mandated warnings panel (dietary supplement). Contains soy in some formulations per label ingredient lists found. Zinc content (8mg/serving) is well under the 40mg/day adult tolerable upper limit at labeled dosing, so zinc toxicity risk is low unless significantly overdosed.

## Symptoms Treated
Not FDA-labeled to treat specific symptoms; marketed for general immune support.

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
No ingredient-page discrepancy — Airborne's vitamin C and zinc content don't contradict [[vitamin-c]] or [[zinc]], they're just delivered in a multi-nutrient blend rather than a single-ingredient product. The 2008 false-advertising settlement is the standout Airborne-specific finding and belongs on this page rather than either ingredient page, since it's about brand marketing conduct, not the nutrients' pharmacology.
