## Overview
**Discrepancy flagged.** Confirmed via DailyMed/FDA labeling: Hibiclens' own FDA OTC Drug Facts label lists **chlorhexidine gluconate 4.0% w/v solution** as its active ingredient, used as an antimicrobial skin cleanser for skin wound and general skin cleansing, surgical hand scrub, and healthcare-personnel handwash — a **topical skin antiseptic**. This is the same active-ingredient family (chlorhexidine gluconate) covered on [[chlorhexidine]], but that existing ingredient page's entire evidence base (the James et al. 2017 Cochrane review, the Peridex label safety data) concerns a **0.12% ORAL RINSE formulation for gingivitis** — a genuinely different concentration (4.0% vs. 0.12%, roughly 33x higher), different route (topical skin vs. oral mucosa), and different indication (skin antisepsis/surgical scrub vs. gingivitis adjunct treatment). The plaque/gingivitis/tooth-staining evidence on [[chlorhexidine]] does not transfer to Hibiclens' skin-antisepsis use case, and vice versa — these are two materially different products sharing one active-ingredient name. One safety data point does carry over: [[chlorhexidine]] already notes anaphylaxis/serious allergic reactions reported with chlorhexidine-containing dental products; separately, FDA added an anaphylaxis warning specifically to chlorhexidine gluconate topical/skin-antiseptic OTC Drug Facts labels (2017) — meaning the anaphylaxis risk is a genuine cross-formulation concern for chlorhexidine generally, not specific to either the oral or topical use case alone.

## Pros
- Confirmed FDA Drug Facts label with a well-established, broad-spectrum topical antimicrobial (bacteria, some viruses/fungi) used extensively in clinical surgical-prep and handwashing contexts
- Category leader by a wide margin in Antibacterial Soaps (43%) and a meaningful share of Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics (8%)
- Distinct antiseptic mechanism (biguanide) from the peptide-antibiotic products (Neosporin, Polysporin) and quaternary-ammonium products (Bactine, Alocane) also in the Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics category

## Cons
- **The only existing ingredient-evidence page for chlorhexidine in this database ([[chlorhexidine]]) is built entirely around the oral-rinse/gingivitis use case, not the topical skin-antisepsis use case Hibiclens actually represents** — a real evidence-page/product mismatch flagged here rather than silently assumed to transfer
- FDA-mandated anaphylaxis warning (added 2017) applies to chlorhexidine gluconate topical antiseptic OTC labels specifically — a serious, if rare, risk that should be understood as chlorhexidine-class-wide, not unique to either formulation
- Label warns against use in the eyes, ears, or mouth — can cause serious/permanent eye injury or deafness (via a perforated eardrum) if it enters those areas, a real and specific safety consideration for a rinse-off product used near the face/head
- No brand-specific outcome trial for Hibiclens' specific topical formulation was located beyond its long clinical-use history

## Use Cases
Antimicrobial skin cleansing for general skin/wound care, pre-surgical skin preparation (surgical hand scrub), and healthcare-personnel handwash — a topical antisepsis use case, not an oral/dental one.

## Allergies / Warnings
Per label: keep out of eyes, ears, and mouth (risk of serious/permanent eye injury or deafness through a perforated eardrum); may cause a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) — FDA added this warning to chlorhexidine gluconate topical OTC labels in 2017 following increased anaphylaxis reports; discontinue and seek immediate care if signs of allergic reaction occur.

## Symptoms Treated
Reduces bacterial (and some viral/fungal) skin-surface contamination; used preventively/prophylactically rather than to treat an existing infection or specific symptom.

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
**Real discrepancy, flagged on this page and on [[chlorhexidine]]:** the existing chlorhexidine ingredient page's evidence grade, dosing, and efficacy data are all specific to the 0.12% Peridex oral rinse for gingivitis — none of that data should be read as applying to Hibiclens' 4.0% topical skin-antiseptic formulation. The two products share an active-ingredient name but occupy separate use cases, concentrations, and evidence bases. The anaphylaxis-risk note is the one safety fact confirmed to be genuinely shared across both formulations.
