## Appears In

## Overview
This is the Airborne brand's dedicated vitamin-C product line, distinct from the multi-nutrient "Airborne" immune-support product tracked separately in this database's Cold Remedies/Immune Support categories (see [[Airborne]] for that page's full brand history). **A real, current, and directly relevant 2026 class-action lawsuit** alleges Airborne's front-of-package vitamin C dosage claims mislead consumers into believing the displayed milligram amount applies to each individual gummy/tablet/effervescent unit, when it actually reflects the total multi-unit serving size — a real per-unit-vs-per-serving labeling-clarity allegation directly on point for a "High-Potency Vitamin C" product where the specific dose matters. This follows the brand's separate, older (2008) $30 million FTC settlement over unsubstantiated "clinically proven to treat colds" claims (see [[Airborne]]) — meaning Airborne has now faced two separate, real regulatory/legal challenges over how it communicates dosage and efficacy to consumers, in two different eras. High-Potency Vitamin C Supplements (10%) is covered by [[vitamin-c]].

## Pros
- Vitamin C itself has real (if modest) evidence for cold-duration reduction — see [[vitamin-c]]
- "High-potency" framing at least signals a higher per-serving dose than a standard multivitamin's vitamin C content, assuming the serving-size dosage is read correctly (see Cons)

## Cons
- **Real, active (2026) class-action lawsuit alleging misleading front-of-package dosage labeling** — specifically that the displayed vitamin C milligram amount may not reflect what a single gummy/tablet actually contains, unlike what reasonable consumers would assume — directly relevant to a "High-Potency" product where consumers may be dosing based on the front-label number alone
- **This is the second separate, real legal/regulatory challenge to this brand's dosage/efficacy communication** (the other being the 2008 FTC "clinically proven to treat colds" settlement affecting the parent "Airborne" brand, see [[Airborne]]) — a real, recurring pattern of marketing/labeling-clarity issues specific to this brand across two different eras
- No Airborne-Vitamin-C-specific clinical trial was located; see [[vitamin-c]] for the general ingredient evidence, which shows only a modest (~8%) cold-duration reduction in adults — not necessarily what "high-potency" framing might imply to a consumer
- As of this pass, the 2026 lawsuit is unresolved — an active allegation, not an adjudicated fact
