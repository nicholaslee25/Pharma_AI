## Overview
Nizoral (Janssen Pharmaceuticals for the Rx line; OTC "Nizoral A-D" line under McNeil/Kenvue-family OTC marketing) is confirmed as **ketoconazole**-based, but spans **two different concentrations under two different access tiers**:

- **OTC (US): Nizoral A-D, ketoconazole 1%** — the product actually purchasable without a prescription and driving this brand's 33% category-leading recommendation share. Used twice weekly, ages 12+, per manufacturer product information (not independently pulled as a full DailyMed label in this pass).
- **Prescription (US): Nizoral 2% shampoo** — the DailyMed label independently pulled this pass, manufactured by Janssen Pharmaceuticals. Its on-label FDA indication is specifically **tinea (pityriasis) versicolor**, not dandruff — a genuinely important distinction from the OTC product's dandruff indication, even though 2% ketoconazole is also the concentration used in most of the seborrheic-dermatitis/dandruff efficacy literature (see [[ketoconazole]]).

**The key efficacy nuance, confirmed via [[ketoconazole]]'s dose-response literature:** the strongest clinical trial results for ketoconazole against dandruff/seborrheic dermatitis (including its head-to-head win over zinc pyrithione 1%) used the **2% concentration** — the Rx-only strength in the US — not the 1% OTC strength that most consumers actually buy as "Nizoral A-D." A real, easily-missed access/efficacy gap: the product driving this category's top recommendation share is not the same concentration shown to be most effective in the underlying trials.

## Pros
- The only ketoconazole-based brand in this category, giving consumers antifungal-mechanism access without a prescription (at 1%)
- Ketoconazole has a genuine, well-documented dose-response relationship and RCT support (see [[ketoconazole]]), unlike coal tar's thinner trial-level evidence elsewhere in this category
- Twice-weekly dosing (rather than daily) may improve adherence relative to more frequent regimens

## Cons
- **OTC concentration (1%) is not the concentration with the strongest trial support (2%)** — a consumer choosing Nizoral A-D specifically because of "ketoconazole beats zinc pyrithione" trial data circulating in consumer health content would actually be using a lower-strength product than the one tested in that trial
- No brand-specific (as opposed to general-ketoconazole) clinical trial was located for Nizoral A-D 1% specifically
- Pediatric use under 12 not established; hair discoloration/texture changes and contact-dermatitis risk are real, labeled adverse effects (see [[ketoconazole]])
- The OTC/Rx concentration-and-indication split (1% dandruff vs. 2% tinea versicolor) is not obviously flagged in typical consumer-facing "Nizoral" marketing, which could lead a consumer to conflate the two products' evidence bases

## Use Cases
Dandruff treatment via ketoconazole's antifungal action against *Malassezia* yeast (OTC 1%, twice weekly); tinea versicolor treatment (Rx 2%, single or doctor-directed application) — two related but formally distinct indications under one brand name.

## Allergies / Warnings
Per the 2% Rx label: severe hypersensitivity reactions including anaphylaxis have been reported; discontinue and seek medical care if rash, swelling, angioedema, or breathing difficulty occur; avoid eye contact; hair discoloration/abnormal texture, itching, burning, contact dermatitis, rash, and urticaria are labeled adverse effects; pediatric safety not established. OTC 1% product warnings not independently re-verified against a full pulled label this pass but expected to carry a similar core profile per [[ketoconazole]].

## Symptoms Treated
Dandruff (flaking, itching) via the OTC 1% line; tinea (pityriasis) versicolor via the Rx 2% line.

This is preserved as a genuine access/efficacy gap rather than resolved in either direction.
