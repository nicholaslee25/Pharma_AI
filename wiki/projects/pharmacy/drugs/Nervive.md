## Overview
**Major within-brand mechanism/regulatory-category split, confirmed via DailyMed and manufacturer sourcing:** "Nervive" covers two fundamentally different products under one umbrella brand (Procter & Gamble):

1. **Nervive Pain Relieving Cream / Liquid Roll-On** (drives the Topical Analgesics 1% share) — confirmed via DailyMed: **lidocaine HCl 4% + menthol 1%**, a genuine FDA OTC-monograph topical anesthetic/counterirritant drug. See [[topical-anesthetics]] and [[topical-counterirritants]].
2. **Nervive Nerve Relief** (drives the Nerve Health 68% share — the dominant brand in that entire category) — an oral tablet **dietary supplement**, not an FDA-evaluated drug: **alpha lipoic acid (ALA) 600mg** plus B-vitamins (B1/B6/B12), turmeric, and ginger. Dosing per manufacturer: 3 tablets daily for the first 7 days, then 1 tablet daily thereafter.

This is a real, consumer-relevant discrepancy: the same "Nervive" brand name spans one FDA-regulated topical OTC drug (a numbing/counterirritant cream, symptomatic-only) and one unregulated oral dietary supplement (marketed to address "nerve discomfort" from the inside, via ALA's antioxidant/nerve-metabolism mechanism) — two categorically different regulatory statuses, delivery routes, and claimed mechanisms, marketed under near-identical "nerve relief" language that could easily lead a consumer to conflate them or assume equivalent evidentiary backing.

**Genuine brand-specific trial found for the supplement:** the manufacturer's own clinical-science page cites **Ziegler et al., "Oral Treatment With α-Lipoic Acid Improves Symptomatic Diabetic Polyneuropathy: The SYDNEY 2 trial," Diabetes Care 2006;29(11):2365-2370** — a real, peer-reviewed RCT of oral ALA (600mg/day, matching Nervive's dose) showing significant improvement in diabetic polyneuropathy symptoms. **Important population-mismatch caveat:** the SYDNEY 2 trial enrolled patients with diagnosed diabetic polyneuropathy specifically, while Nervive Nerve Relief is marketed broadly to consumers with "occasional nerve discomfort" of any origin (aging, general "pins and needles," etc.) — a narrower trial population than the product's general marketing claim implies, though the mechanism (ALA's antioxidant effect on peripheral nerves) is plausibly generalizable. This is a rare case in this database where a dietary supplement cites a real, appropriately-dosed, peer-reviewed RCT rather than no trial or a mismatched-dose citation — a genuine positive, with the population caveat noted rather than overlooked.

No Nervive-brand-specific trial (beyond the cited ALA molecule-level SYDNEY 2 study) was located for the topical lidocaine/menthol cream; that formulation's evidence rests on the general lidocaine/menthol literature already documented on [[topical-anesthetics]] and [[topical-counterirritants]].

## Pros
- **Topical cream:** confirmed FDA OTC-monograph drug (lidocaine 4% + menthol 1%), consistent formulation, lower-methemoglobinemia-risk anesthetic per [[topical-anesthetics]]
- **Oral supplement:** cites a real, peer-reviewed, appropriately-dosed RCT (SYDNEY 2, Ziegler et al. 2006) for its core ingredient and dose (600mg ALA) — genuinely stronger sourcing than most supplement brands in this database
- Dominant share (68%) of the Nerve Health category, suggesting broad pharmacist familiarity with the oral product specifically

## Cons
- **Two chemically, mechanistically, and regulatorily unrelated products share one brand name and near-identical "nerve relief" marketing language** — a real consumer-confusion risk distinct from (and arguably more consequential than) typical same-brand-different-SKU splits elsewhere in this database, since one product is an FDA drug and the other is an unregulated supplement
- The oral supplement's cited trial (SYDNEY 2) was conducted in diagnosed diabetic polyneuropathy patients, a narrower and more clinically-defined population than the product's general "occasional nerve discomfort" marketing claim addresses
- As a dietary supplement, Nervive Nerve Relief is not FDA-evaluated for safety or efficacy (DSHEA framework) — the "Clinically Studied" marketing language refers to the ALA molecule/dose, not an independent trial of the finished multi-ingredient product (which also adds B-vitamins, turmeric, and ginger, none of which were part of the cited single-ingredient ALA trial)
- No brand-specific trial exists for the topical lidocaine/menthol cream beyond general ingredient-level evidence

## Use Cases
**Topical cream:** temporary relief of minor muscle/joint pain and neuropathic burning/tingling/numbness discomfort via local anesthesia and cooling counterirritation. **Oral supplement:** marketed to reduce "occasional nerve discomfort" (stabbing, pins-and-needles, burning sensations in hands/feet) via ALA's antioxidant mechanism — not a treatment for diagnosed neuropathy, diabetes, or any disease.

## Allergies / Warnings
**Topical cream:** do not apply to broken/irritated skin or use with a heating pad; avoid if allergic to lidocaine or other amide-class anesthetics; consult a doctor if pregnant/breastfeeding. **Oral supplement:** as a dietary supplement, consult a doctor before use if pregnant, breastfeeding, taking other medications (ALA may affect blood glucose/thyroid levels per general ALA literature — not independently re-verified against this product's own label in this pass), or if managing diabetes, given the ALA-diabetes connection in its own core trial evidence.

## Symptoms Treated
**Topical cream:** minor muscle/joint pain, neuropathic burning/tingling/numbness (symptomatic, local). **Oral supplement:** occasional nerve discomfort (stabbing, pins-and-needles, burning) in hands and feet — general/aging-related framing, not disease-specific.

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
**Real discrepancy flagged:** "Nervive" spans an FDA-regulated topical OTC drug (lidocaine/menthol) and an unregulated oral dietary supplement (ALA/B-vitamins/turmeric/ginger) under one brand identity with overlapping "nerve relief" marketing language — a materially different situation from most single-mechanism-name-drift cases in this database because the two products differ not just in active ingredient but in fundamental regulatory category (drug vs. supplement) and route of administration (topical vs. oral). A consumer should not assume the topical cream's local, temporary numbing effect and the oral supplement's systemic, longer-term antioxidant claim are interchangeable or equally evidenced — the supplement has a genuine RCT behind its core ingredient (SYDNEY 2), narrowed to a diabetic-neuropathy population, while the cream's evidence is purely ingredient-class-level.
