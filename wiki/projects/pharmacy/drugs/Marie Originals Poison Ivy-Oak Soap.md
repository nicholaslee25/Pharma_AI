## Overview
**Homeopathic product marketed with unsupported-strength claims, confirmed via manufacturer/retail ingredient sourcing:** Marie Originals Poison Ivy/Oak Soap lists **Salix Alba 1C (white willow), Sassafras Albidum 1C (sassafras root bark), and Grindelia Camporum 1C (gumweed)** as homeopathic "active ingredients," at 1C dilution (1:100 — a low-potency homeopathic dilution, though still a meaningful dilution factor). The bar-soap base additionally contains **bentonite clay**, marketed as physically removing urushiol oil from skin — a mechanical mechanism analogous to Zanfel's surfactant-binding approach, though bentonite clay's urushiol-binding efficacy was not independently verified against a controlled trial in this pass. The product's own manufacturer disclosure states plainly: **"These statements have not been evaluated by the FDA. This remedy is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease... these ingredients may not yet have been clinically tested by the FDA or the HPUS for these uses"** — yet the same manufacturer's marketing separately describes the product as delivering ingredients that "instantly eliminate itching and pain" and brands it "the #1 Treatment for Poison Ivy & Oak." **This gap between the label's own FDA disclaimer and the marketing copy's confident therapeutic claims is a real, direct discrepancy worth flagging** — a pattern already seen with other homeopathic products in this database (Curoxen Mouth Sore Relief, Boiron Arnicare) but here paired with unusually assertive "#1"/"instantly eliminate" language for a product that in the same breath disclaims any evaluated treatment claim.

**Real, independently-verified ingredient-sourcing finding:** sassafras root bark's primary chemical component, **safrole, was banned by the FDA as a food/beverage additive in 1958-1960 (later extended specifically to sassafras bark in teas) after being classified a hepatocarcinogen in animal studies** (National Toxicology Program: "reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen"). This ban applies to food/beverage use, not topical homeopathic use, and the 1C dilution here means only a small fraction of raw sassafras material is present — this is NOT evidence of meaningful topical carcinogenic exposure, and should not be overstated. But it is a genuinely notable, previously undocumented-in-this-database finding that a botanical ingredient with an FDA food-safety ban history for carcinogenicity appears, even at low homeopathic dilution, as a named "active ingredient" in a topical consumer product — worth a consumer's awareness even though the practical risk at 1C dilution in a rinse-off soap is very likely minimal.

No independent clinical trial for this product (or any 1C-dilution combination of white willow/sassafras/grindelia for poison-ivy relief specifically) was located in this pass.

## Pros
- Bentonite clay's mechanical oil-binding/removal mechanism is chemically plausible and analogous to established urushiol-removal approaches (compare [[Zanfel]], [[Tecnu]])
- Soap/bar format is simple, familiar, and doesn't require the anesthetic-safety cautions (systemic absorption, burn risk) documented for several chemical-drug competitors in this category
- Grindelia (gumweed) has some traditional/folk-medicine use specifically for poison-ivy relief, giving the formula a plausible traditional rationale even without modern clinical validation

## Cons
- **No FDA-evaluated active ingredient and no independent clinical trial** — explicitly disclaimed by the manufacturer's own label language, in direct tension with the same manufacturer's "#1 Treatment"/"instantly eliminate itching and pain" marketing claims
- **Sassafras root bark contains safrole, an FDA-recognized hepatocarcinogen banned from food/beverage use** — present here only at 1C homeopathic dilution and in a topical rinse-off product (a very different exposure profile from oral consumption), but a genuinely notable ingredient-sourcing fact not previously documented in this database, worth flagging for a fuller-picture understanding of the product's ingredient list even though it does not establish meaningful topical risk at this dilution
- 1C dilution is a comparatively low-potency homeopathic dilution (1:100) — more dilute than Boiron Arnicare's 1X (also 1:10, roughly equivalent order of magnitude to 1C) but still far short of any measured, standardized therapeutic dose of white willow, sassafras, or grindelia
- Smallest confirmed share (2%) of any brand in this category, alongside the weakest evidentiary basis

## Use Cases
Marketed for cleansing/removing poison ivy/oak/sumac oils from skin and homeopathic relief of associated itching and pain — not an FDA-evaluated treatment for any disease or condition per the product's own label disclaimer.

## Allergies / Warnings
No FDA Drug Facts panel exists to independently cite (homeopathic/unevaluated product). General bar-soap precautions apply (avoid eyes; discontinue if irritation occurs). No safrole-specific topical-exposure warning was identified on the product's own labeling in this pass.

## Symptoms Treated
Marketed claim only, not independently verified: itching and pain from poison ivy/oak/sumac exposure, via a combination of mechanical oil removal (bentonite clay) and homeopathic-dilution botanical ingredients.

## Brand-Specific Alignment Check
**Real discrepancy flagged on two fronts:** (1) the product's own FDA disclaimer directly contradicts its marketing's confident "#1 Treatment"/"instantly eliminate" language — a consumer reading only the marketing copy would not know the same company's label disclaims any evaluated treatment claim; (2) sassafras root bark, one of three named homeopathic "active ingredients," is the source of safrole, a compound with an FDA food-safety ban history for hepatocarcinogenicity — a genuinely surprising ingredient-sourcing fact for this database to have surfaced, even though the topical/1C-dilution exposure here is not equivalent to the oral-ingestion risk that prompted the original food-additive ban.
