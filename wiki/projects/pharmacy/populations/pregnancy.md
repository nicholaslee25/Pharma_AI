# Pregnancy — OTC Decision Guide

**Type:** Analysis (special-population synthesis)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Last updated:** 2026-08-28
**Sources:** Synthesized entirely from the "Special Population Flags" (and related Contraindications/Key Safety Data) sections already sourced across `ingredients/*.md` pages in this database. ## How to read this page

Ingredients are grouped into three tiers based on what this database's own sourced content (StatPearls, FDA labels, ACOG/CDC/AAFP guidance cited on the individual ingredient pages) actually says — not a fresh clinical judgment made for this page. Many entries are genuinely "ask a doctor first" rather than a clean safe/avoid split; that ambiguity is preserved rather than flattened. **A note on FDA Pregnancy Categories:** several ingredient pages cite "former FDA Category B/C/X" language — the FDA retired the letter-category system in 2015 in favor of narrative labeling (the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule), but many product labels and secondary sources still reference the old categories, so they appear here as sourced.

**This page is not a substitute for an obstetric provider's guidance** — it exists to organize what this database already knows, not to replace prenatal care.

## Tier 1 — Generally considered safe / preferred in pregnancy

| Ingredient | Note | Source |
|---|---|---|
| [[acetaminophen]] | ACOG's long-standing position: safe in pregnancy, the only OTC antipyretic/analgesic ACOG endorses across pregnancy since NSAIDs/aspirin carry documented fetal risks. **Live caveat:** the FDA initiated a label-change process (2025-09-22) citing an observational association between prenatal exposure and later ASD/ADHD diagnosis — but the FDA's own announcement explicitly states "a causal relationship has not been established," and ACOG and other medical societies have pushed back. Treat as: genuinely useful, first-line, with an active and unresolved regulatory conversation happening around it — not a settled risk in either direction. | [[acetaminophen]] |

| [[calcium]] | Supplementation may reduce preeclampsia risk specifically in those with inadequate baseline intake | [[calcium]] |
| Topical hydrocortisone | Maternal use at any potency not associated with significant adverse pregnancy outcomes | [[hydrocortisone]] |
| [[guaifenesin]] | Normal OTC doses considered acceptable (StatPearls) | [[guaifenesin]] |
| [[bisacodyl]] | Minimal systemic absorption supports general safety; dietary modification/fiber still first-line | [[bisacodyl]] |

| Topical [[clotrimazole]] (not oral) | Not known to cross the placenta; considered safe in 2nd/3rd trimester; CDC recommends a 7-day topical azole course for pregnant women with vulvovaginal candidiasis specifically; use only if indicated in the 1st trimester | [[clotrimazole]] |

| Ingredient | Note | Source |

| [[dimenhydrinate]] / [[meclizine]] | Doxylamine is StatPearls' generally-preferred antihistamine in pregnancy; diphenhydramine and loratadine have also been used safely — dimenhydrinate and meclizine themselves were not specifically/independently addressed with pregnancy-specific data in the sources reviewed | [[dimenhydrinate]], [[meclizine]] |
| [[dextromethorphan]] | No blanket contraindication per StatPearls, but CYP2D6/CYP3A activity is elevated during pregnancy — individualized consideration warranted | [[dextromethorphan]] |

## Bottom lines

1. **Pain/fever:** acetaminophen is the ACOG-endorsed default across all of pregnancy; ibuprofen, naproxen, and general-dose aspirin all carry real, gestational-age-specific fetal risks that acetaminophen does not share. See [[oral-pain-relievers]] for the full non-pregnancy-specific comparison.
2. **Low-dose aspirin is a special, separate case** — genuinely recommended for preeclampsia prevention in high-risk pregnancies, but only at 81mg/day, doctor-directed, and gestational-age-windowed (12–28 weeks) — it should not be confused with using regular-strength aspirin for a headache.
3. **Heartburn/GERD:** lifestyle changes and antacids (aluminum-based preferred; calcium-based capped and used with caution) are first-line; famotidine is a reasonable step-up after a risk-benefit conversation.
4. **Constipation:** PEG 3350 is explicitly AAFP's drug of choice in pregnancy; bisacodyl is also considered generally safe with minimal absorption.
5. **Allergies:** cetirizine is ACOG/ACAAI-supported when needed, though this database's sources note first-generation antihistamines have a longer safety track record specifically in pregnancy — a real, unresolved preference tension, not a contraindication either way.
6. **When a label just says "ask a health professional before use"** (a large share of Tier 2), that is often because no dedicated pregnancy-specific trial exists for that OTC ingredient — not necessarily because a specific risk was found. Treat these as genuinely needing a real conversation with a provider, not as a red flag by default.
