# Liquid Bandages (Film-Forming Skin Sealants)

**Type:** Product Class (not a single active ingredient — spans antiseptic film-formers and cyanoacrylate skin adhesives)
**Category:** First Aid
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Moderate — one real RCT (162 volunteers) shows a cyanoacrylate liquid bandage matches standard adhesive-bandage healing rates while adding real hemostasis/pain-relief benefits; the antiseptic-film-forming sub-class (benzethonium chloride + nitrocellulose) has no comparable dedicated RCT located in this pass

## Indication
Flexible, waterproof protective covering for minor cuts, scrapes, and abrasions — an alternative to adhesive-strip bandages, often preferred for awkward-to-bandage areas (fingertips, knuckles, joints) or where an adhesive strip won't stay in place.

## Product Chemistry — genuinely TWO different mechanisms share the "liquid bandage" name
This category is not one ingredient class, unlike most pages in this database. Brand-page research in this pass identified at least two chemically distinct approaches:
1. **Antiseptic film-formers** (e.g., [[New-Skin]]): active ingredient is an antiseptic (benzethonium chloride 0.2%), suspended in a nitrocellulose/acetate/alcohol solvent base that evaporates to leave a flexible protective film. The FDA-recognized active ingredient here is the antiseptic, not the film-forming solvent — the film is more like a delivery vehicle/physical barrier than the "active" component.
2. **Cyanoacrylate skin adhesives** (e.g., Band-Aid Liquid Bandage, the product tested in the RCT below; also present in some SKUs described by brand-family research as "kills 99.9% of germs" claims, e.g. [[Bactine MAX]]'s Liquid Bandage SKU): octyl-2-cyanoacrylate, FDA-approved for incision/laceration closure, forms an adhesive polymer film via a genuine wound-closure mechanism (not merely antiseptic), sloughing off naturally in 1-3 days as skin heals underneath.
A given "liquid bandage" brand/SKU should not be assumed to work via either mechanism without checking its specific label — the two approaches (antisepsis-via-film vs. adhesive-wound-closure) have different evidence bases and different active ingredients.

## Efficacy Evidence — the one real RCT located

**Buchanan/Eaglstein et al. 2002 (PMID 11896780, Dermatologic Surgery)** — RCT, 162 volunteers with minor cuts/abrasions, liquid adhesive bandage (LAB, octyl-2-cyanoacrylate) vs. standard Band-Aid brand adhesive bandage:
- **Primary endpoint (complete healing at day 12): NO statistically significant difference** between LAB and standard bandage — i.e., liquid bandage does not heal wounds faster or better than a normal bandage.
- **Secondary endpoints: only the LAB group showed significant hemostasis (bleeding control) and pain relief** — a real, specific, quantifiable advantage over a standard adhesive bandage on these measures, even though overall healing outcomes were equivalent.
- **Authors' conclusion:** LAB is as effective as a standard bandage for healing, with added bleeding-control and pain-relief benefits the standard bandage doesn't provide.

No comparably designed RCT for the antiseptic-film-forming sub-class (benzethonium chloride + nitrocellulose, e.g. New-Skin) was located in this pass — that sub-class's evidence rests on benzethonium chloride's general antiseptic profile rather than a liquid-bandage-specific trial.

## Key Safety Data
- **New-Skin's FDA label carries an unusual, specific warning: "intentional inhalation misuse can be harmful or fatal"** — reflecting the volatile solvent base (nitrocellulose + acetates + SD alcohol) common to film-forming liquid bandages, a real, quantified-by-label-inclusion risk distinct from the antiseptic active ingredient's own safety profile.
- Cyanoacrylate-class adhesives (the wound-closure sub-type) are associated, in the broader adjacent literature, with accidental ocular instillation injuries when packaged in dropper/bottle formats resembling eye drops — historically documented mostly for generic "superglue" repackaged into eye-drop-style bottles rather than FDA-regulated liquid-bandage brush applicators specifically; flagged as adjacent-class context, not a confirmed brand-specific incident in this database.
- Both sub-classes carry standard "do not use in eyes, on mucous membranes, over infected/draining wounds, or combined with other first aid creams/lotions" label warnings (per New-Skin's label).
