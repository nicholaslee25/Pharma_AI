# Proton Pump Inhibitors (Omeprazole / Esomeprazole / Lansoprazole)

**Type:** Active Ingredient (class)
**Drug Class:** Proton pump inhibitor (PPI)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Strong for acid suppression as the most potent class available; long-term-use safety signals (dementia, kidney disease, heart disease) are from confounded observational studies that have not held up in prospective research, per StatPearls' own characterization
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/proton-pump-inhibitors/statpearls-ppi-class.md, raw/primary-sources/proton-pump-inhibitors/dailymed-prilosec-otc.md

## Indication
FDA-approved: esophagitis, non-erosive reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, NSAID-induced ulcer prevention, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, H. pylori triple-therapy component, pediatric GERD (short-term), pediatric eosinophilic esophagitis healing. OTC (omeprazole/Prilosec OTC): frequent heartburn occurring ≥2 days/week.

## Mechanism
Prodrugs that block the H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump) — the final step of gastric acid secretion — in parietal cells, after acid-catalyzed activation in the secretory canaliculi. This is the most potent acid-suppression mechanism among OTC/prescription options, more complete than H2RAs like famotidine.

## Dosing (representative — Prilosec OTC)
Adults ≥18y: one 20mg tablet daily with water, before the morning meal (proton pumps activate with food, so pre-meal dosing matters). Swallow whole. Complete a full 14-day course; do not exceed 1 tablet/day; may repeat the 14-day course every 4 months maximum. **Not for immediate relief — may take 1–4 days for full effect** (this is a fundamentally different use pattern than an antacid or H2RA taken as-needed).

## Efficacy / Real-World Limitation
Per StatPearls: **approximately 50% of patients taking a PPI for non-erosive GERD do not achieve complete symptom resolution**, often requiring dose escalation and clinical reassessment. This is a substantial real-world efficacy gap for the "most potent" acid-suppression class, worth stating plainly rather than implying PPIs universally resolve symptoms.

## Pharmacokinetics
Hepatic metabolism, primarily via CYP2C19 (varies by specific agent — this class page covers the shared mechanism; individual agents like omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole, dexlansoprazole differ in exact PK). Patients of Asian ethnicity show increased bioavailability and may need lower initial doses; elderly patients also show increased bioavailability, requiring closer monitoring.

## Contraindications
Known PPI hypersensitivity. Severe hepatic disease is a relative contraindication (used clinically with increased monitoring despite this). OTC label: do not use with swallowing difficulty, bloody vomit/stools, chest pain with respiratory symptoms, or frequent chest pain — these suggest a condition beyond simple heartburn.

## Key Safety Data
- **Hypomagnesemia:** rare but serious — can drop magnesium to levels not correctable by supplementation alone, requiring PPI discontinuation; risk of tetany, seizure, muscle weakness, delirium, cardiac arrhythmias
- **Infection risk:** increased C. difficile infection, enteric foodborne infections, possibly increased community-acquired pneumonia — reduced gastric acidity permits bacterial overgrowth/aspiration
- **Rebound acid hypersecretion:** stopping after prolonged use can raise acid above baseline (via elevated gastrin/ECL cell proliferation) — complicates discontinuation even though it doesn't reflect reduced efficacy during treatment
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency:** mechanistically plausible (acid needed to release B12 from R-factor protein) but clinical deficiency remains rare
- **Iron deficiency:** reported with prolonged use, mechanism unclear; calcium malabsorption also occurs but usually subclinical
- **Osteoporosis/fractures:** conflicting data, no clearly established causal mechanism despite several proposed pathways
- **Dementia, kidney disease, heart disease:** isolated retrospective/observational links have NOT been confirmed in prospective studies — significant confounding, inconsistent findings. This vault treats these as unresolved associations, not established harms, consistent with StatPearls' own framing
- **Malignancy:** initial animal-model gastrin concern not demonstrated in human patients; fundic gland polyps increase with prolonged use but are not linked to malignancy
- **Common:** headaches, dizziness, skin rash, abdominal pain, diarrhea, back pain, upper respiratory infections
- OTC label also flags possible severe skin reactions (reddening, blisters, rash)

## Drug Interactions
Delayed clearance (caution): phenytoin, warfarin, diazepam. **Ketoconazole:** decreased absorption — use an alternative antifungal during long-term PPI therapy. **Digoxin:** absorption potentiated — extreme caution given digoxin's narrow therapeutic index. **Note:** the StatPearls source used for this page does not explicitly address the well-known clopidogrel–PPI CYP2C19 interaction commonly discussed elsewhere in the cardiology/GI literature, despite CYP2C19 being central to PPI metabolism — this is flagged as a specific gap in this particular source rather than something fabricated here.

## Special Population Flags
- **Ethnicity:** patients of Asian ethnicity show increased PPI bioavailability, may need lower initial doses
- **Elderly:** increased bioavailability, closer monitoring/dose adjustment needed
- **Kidney transplant patients:** magnesium-level monitoring specifically supported by evidence
- **Long-term therapy generally:** B12 monitoring "more controversial but reasonable to consider" in select cases; bone density scanning/calcium supplementation currently have limited evidence supporting effectiveness specifically for PPI-associated bone risk

## Overdose / Toxicity
No FDA-approved antidote exists.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Prilosec OTC recommendation shares (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed): Proton Pump Inhibitors category 54%, Acid Reducers (general) category 37%. These are pharmacist recommendation shares, not response/cure rates.
