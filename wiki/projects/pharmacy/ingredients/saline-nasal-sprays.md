## What This Page Covers
Most brands in this category contain **no active drug ingredient** — they are saline (sodium chloride) solutions, explicitly marketed and regulated as non-drug/"drug-free" (an FDA drug label was not located for most SKUs; where one exists, e.g. some NeilMed/generic saline products, it lists no "Active Ingredient" section at all). This is mechanistically and regulatorily distinct from the decongestant nasal sprays in this database's separate "Decongestants, Nasal Spray" category (Afrin/oxymetazoline, etc. — see [[oxymetazoline]]), which do contain a real vasoconstrictor drug.

## Formulation Variants — a real mechanism distinction within one Pharmacy Times category
- **Isotonic saline (~0.65-0.9% NaCl)** — matches the body's natural salinity; purely moisturizes/rinses without an osmotic pull. Confirmed for: Simply Saline (0.65%), NeilMed NasaMist standard (9 mg/mL = 0.9%), Mucinex Sinus (water + sodium chloride only, no preservatives), Little Remedies for Noses (with glycerin/preservatives), Zarbee's Soothing Saline Mist (with aloe).
- **Hypertonic saline (~2.65-2.7%, "3X concentrated" per Vicks' own marketing)** — a real osmotic-decongestant mechanism: the higher salt concentration draws fluid out of swollen nasal mucosa cells, reducing congestion via osmosis rather than vasoconstriction. Confirmed for: **Vicks Sinex Saline** (marketed "3X concentrated vs. isotonic saline," explicitly framed by the manufacturer as having a decongestant-like effect despite being "drug-free") and **NeilMed NasaMist Extra Strength** (27 mg/mL = 2.7%, marketed as a "drug-free nasal decongestant"). **This is a genuine mechanism difference from the isotonic products above, not just a stronger version of the same thing** — per the evidence below, hypertonic saline has a measurably different (better, for congestion specifically) effect profile than isotonic saline.
- **Buffered saline irrigation packets (neti pot format)** — SinuCleanse: sodium bicarbonate 703mg + sodium chloride 2300mg per packet, dissolved in a larger volume of water for full nasal cavity irrigation rather than a spray mist — same basic saline chemistry, different delivery volume/mechanism (bulk rinse vs. mist).
- **Gel format** — Ayr Saline Nasal Gel: a glycerin/aloe-based gel (not a spray at all in this SKU) for moisturizing dry nasal tissue, using different inactive vehicle ingredients (glycerin, carbomer, xanthan gum) than the aqueous sprays — designed to stay in place longer for chronic dryness rather than rinse/irrigate.

## Efficacy Evidence

**Cochrane, saline nasal irrigation for acute upper respiratory tract infections (King et al. 2015)** — 5 RCTs, 544 children and 205 adults, saline irrigation vs. routine care or other nasal sprays (not placebo-controlled in most included trials): some symptom benefit reported, though the comparator was usual care rather than a placebo spray in most trials, a real limitation on how strong a claim this supports.

**Cochrane, saline irrigation for allergic rhinitis (Head et al. 2018)** — saline irrigation may reduce patient-reported disease severity vs. no saline irrigation, at up to 3 months, in both adults and children, with no reported adverse effects — a real, if modest, benefit for allergic rhinitis specifically.

**Hypertonic vs. isotonic saline meta-analysis (Kanjanawasee et al. 2018, and a separate 2024 meta-analysis of 9 trials/645 patients)** — hypertonic saline showed **greater improvement in nasal congestion and nasal secretion reduction** than isotonic saline, and a separate 4-trial/351-patient meta-analysis found hypertonic saline improved nasal symptom scores and reduced rescue antihistamine use rates vs. isotonic. **Mechanism:** hypertonic solution draws water out of nasal mucosa cells via osmosis, reducing mucosal swelling — a real, if modest, decongestant-like effect that isotonic saline does not share.

**Bottom line:** saline nasal products as a class have real, Cochrane-supported evidence for allergic rhinitis symptom severity and some benefit for acute URI symptoms — a notably more favorable evidence picture than some other "recommendation share concentrated in one thin-evidence product" categories in this database (e.g. [[cerumenolytics]]). Within the class, hypertonic products (Vicks Sinex Saline, NeilMed NasaMist Extra Strength) have real trial support for a congestion-specific advantage over isotonic products — this is not just marketing language.

## Key Safety Data
Adverse effects of nasal saline irrigation are rare and generally mild — ear fullness, mild stinging of the nasal mucosa, occasional epistaxis (nosebleed). No serious adverse events identified across the sources reviewed. Because most formulations contain no active drug, there is no rebound-congestion risk analogous to vasoconstrictor decongestant sprays (see [[oxymetazoline]]) — a real safety advantage for the whole saline class, applicable regardless of isotonic/hypertonic concentration.

## Contraindications
None established at the class level in the sources reviewed; irrigation-format products (SinuCleanse) carry a general caution to stop and consult a doctor if washing is uncomfortable or symptoms aren't relieved, and (per general nasal-irrigation practice, consistent with [[cerumenolytics]]'s ear-irrigation caution) distilled/sterile water should be used to prepare any home-mixed solution to avoid rare but serious waterborne-organism risk (a general nasal-irrigation safety point, not specific to any one brand reviewed here).

## Special Population Flags
**Pediatric/infant:** several brands (Little Remedies for Noses, Zarbee's, NeilMed NasaMist pediatric concentration, Boogie Mist) are specifically marketed for infants/young children — isotonic saline is the standard safe choice for this population; hypertonic formulations are generally marketed toward older children/adults given the stronger osmotic effect.
