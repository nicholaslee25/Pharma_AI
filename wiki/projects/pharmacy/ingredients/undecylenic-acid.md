# Undecylenic Acid

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Fatty-acid antifungal (10-undecenoic acid, an 11-carbon monounsaturated fatty acid derived from castor oil), FDA OTC monograph
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Moderate for tinea pedis (real placebo-controlled trials from the 1970s-80s show a substantial effect over vehicle/placebo) but explicitly **not FDA-labeled as curative for nail fungus** — only for improving fungal-nail appearance; also generally regarded as less effective than modern azole/allylamine/benzylamine antifungals
**Last updated:** 2026-08-27
**Sources:** DailyMed (undecylenic acid 25% toenail products, Fungi-Nail label conventions), historical placebo-controlled trials (Smith 1977; Chretien 1980) cited in ScienceDirect/Wiley review sources

---

## Indication
FDA OTC monograph: tinea pedis (athlete's foot) treatment, and — at a materially weaker claim level — helping to inhibit fungal growth and **improve the cosmetic appearance** of nails affected by fungus (discoloration, thickening, splitting, crumbling, brittleness). This is the confirmed active ingredient in [[Fungi-Nail]] (25% concentration) and a secondary sub-line of [[FUNGICURE]].

## Mechanism
A medium-chain fatty acid that disrupts fungal cell membranes, inhibiting fungal growth (fungistatic) rather than directly killing existing fungal cells the way azole (clotrimazole, [[miconazole-nitrate-topical]]) or allylamine ([[terbinafine]]) antifungals do. In use since 1949, one of the oldest topical antifungal mechanisms still marketed OTC. Reported to be roughly 6x more antifungal than caprylic acid, and its zinc-salt form (zinc undecylenate) up to 4x more fungicidal than the free acid — the two are frequently combined in older-generation OTC powder formulations.

## Efficacy Evidence
- **Controlled clinical trial (104 patients, mycologically confirmed tinea pedis):** undecylenic-acid powder achieved **53% clinical and mycological cure vs. 7% for talc vehicle/untreated** — a real, substantial effect over placebo, though the absolute cure rate (53%) is materially lower than modern comparator cure rates documented elsewhere in this database for terbinafine/butenafine (see [[terbinafine]], [[butenafine]]).
- **Double-blind trial (151 tinea pedis patients), undecylenic acid 2% + zinc undecylenate 20% powder:** **88% negative fungal cultures at 4 weeks vs. 17% for placebo** — the combination formulation performed considerably better than undecylenic acid alone in the trial above, suggesting the zinc-salt combination materially improves on the free-acid-alone result.
- **No modern (post-1980s) head-to-head RCT against azole/allylamine/benzylamine antifungals was located in this pass** — the evidence base for this ingredient is notably older than for the ingredients dominating this database's other antifungal categories, a real, documented "comparatively dated evidence base" pattern already seen for [[tolnaftate]] in the Jock Itch/Antifungal Products category.
- **Nail-fungus (onychomycosis) claim is explicitly weaker than a cure claim**: FDA OTC labeling for undecylenic-acid nail products states the product "helps inhibit the growth of fungus and improves the appearance" of affected nails — it does **not** claim to cure onychomycosis. This is a materially important, real distinction: standard dermatology guidance holds that OTC topical antifungals generally cannot cure onychomycosis (nail penetration is too limited), and undecylenic acid's own FDA label reflects this by making only a cosmetic-appearance claim rather than a cure claim — a genuine effectiveness gap that could be missed by a consumer buying a "toenail fungus treatment" expecting a cure.

## Key Safety Data
Generally well-tolerated; standard external-use fatty-acid antifungal precautions apply. No major safety signal beyond standard topical-antifungal conventions was located in this pass.

## Contraindications / Warnings
External use only; not for use on children under 2 without doctor guidance (per DailyMed label conventions); discontinue if irritation develops; seek medical care if no improvement within the labeled treatment duration.
