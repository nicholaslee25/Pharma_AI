# Insect Bites (General Symptom Management)

## Indication
Itching, swelling, and erythema (redness) from insect bites/stings, most commonly mosquitoes. StatPearls frames the broader arthropod-bite category (bites, stings, envenomations) as four distinct injury mechanisms: mechanical trauma, allergic response to salivary antigens, toxic venom injection, and disease-vector transmission — most OTC-relevant cases fall into the first two categories.

## Mechanism (of the bite reaction, not of any specific OTC treatment)
Mosquito/fly bites: minimal mechanical trauma but intense pruritus and urticarial wheals, driven by anti-saliva IgE antibodies and histamine release (per the Karppinen RCT background). Histologically, early lesions show dermal edema and neutrophilic infiltrate progressing to dense perivascular inflammation with eosinophils.

## Efficacy Evidence — real, quantified antihistamine data

**Karppinen et al. 2000 (PMID 10921468, Allergy)** — double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover RCT, loratadine 0.3 mg/kg in 28 mosquito-bite-sensitive children (ages 2-11), exposed to lab-reared Aedes aegypti mosquitoes:
- **Wheal size reduced by 45% (P<0.001) at 15 minutes** vs. placebo
- **Pruritus reduced by 78% (P=0.011) at 15 minutes** vs. placebo
- 24-hour delayed bite lesion size also significantly reduced (P=0.004) — though no significant change was seen at the 2h or 6h timepoints specifically, an interesting biphasic pattern
- Well tolerated, no marked side effects
- This is real, quantified pediatric RCT evidence that a systemic (oral) antihistamine meaningfully reduces both the immediate wheal/itch response and the 24h delayed reaction — a stronger evidence base than exists for most single OTC topical bite products

No comparably detailed RCT for topical hydrocortisone or calamine specifically on insect-bite symptoms (as opposed to other dermatologic uses) was located in this pass — see [[hydrocortisone]] for that ingredient's own broader evidence base.

## Typical Treatments (StatPearls)
**First-line supportive care:** wound cleansing (soap/water), ice application, tetanus-immunization status check, pain management (acetaminophen/NSAIDs), elevation of edematous areas.
**Topical:** calamine lotion for pruritus, intermediate-potency corticosteroids (see [[hydrocortisone]]), topical antibiotics only if secondary infection develops.
**Systemic:** oral antihistamines (H1 and H2 blockers) for moderate-to-severe pruritus — directly supported by the Karppinen RCT above; systemic corticosteroids reserved for severe itching.

**Critical framing (carried forward from this database's prior correction and still accurate): none of these typical treatments "neutralize" insect venom after the fact — they address symptoms only, not the underlying bite/sting reaction mechanism itself.**

## Key Safety Data — when this is NOT a simple OTC case
StatPearls draws a hard line between routine bite symptom management and situations requiring emergency care:
- **Anaphylaxis** requires immediate intramuscular epinephrine, repeated every 5–15 minutes if needed — adjuncts (steroids, albuterol, antihistamines) should NEVER delay epinephrine administration. This is a real, life-threatening complication, not a rare theoretical risk to mention in passing.
- **Brown recluse spider bites** can progress to full-thickness skin necrosis requiring debridement/grafting — early surgical excision is specifically NOT recommended.
- **Black widow spider bites** progress from a sharp pinprick sensation to muscle spasms and chest/abdominal pain within 1–6 hours, persisting 12–48 hours.
- FDA-approved antivenoms exist for Centruroides scorpions and Latrodectus (black widow) spiders, reserved for severe systemic toxicity, requiring poison control consultation.
- Local complications: secondary bacterial infection (S. aureus, S. pyogenes), impetiginization, cellulitis, lymphangitis, scarring, post-inflammatory pigmentation.
- Systemic complications beyond anaphylaxis: serum sickness-like reactions, vasculitis, and vector-borne disease transmission (malaria, Lyme disease, dengue, yellow fever, Chagas disease, West Nile virus, and others).
- Scale of the problem: the American Association of Poison Control Centers documented 28,087 arthropod-exposure cases in 2015 alone — likely a substantial undercount of actual encounters given underreporting.
- Risk factors for severe outcomes: extremes of age, immunocompromise, cardiovascular comorbidities, allergic conditions.
