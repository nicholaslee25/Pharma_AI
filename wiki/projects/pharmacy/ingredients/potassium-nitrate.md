# Potassium Nitrate

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Tooth-desensitizing agent
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Mixed/genuinely uncertain — the Cochrane review itself concludes "no clear evidence" despite finding a statistically significant effect on objective (air blast/tactile) sensitivity measures; patient-reported (subjective) benefit was NOT statistically significant. This is a real downgrade from a simple "moderate-to-strong evidence" characterization.
**Last updated:** 2026-08-26
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/potassium-nitrate/dailymed-sensodyne.md, raw/primary-sources/potassium-nitrate/cochrane-poulsen-2006.md

## Indication
Tooth (dentine) sensitivity to cold, heat, acids, sweets, or contact — typically from exposed dentine due to worn enamel or receding gums.

## Mechanism
Reduces nerve excitability in the dentinal tubules, per the ingredient's general pharmacologic rationale. Per the Arm & Hammer Sensitive Teeth and Gums FDA label (2026-08-27 pass): potassium salts travel along the dentinal tubules and block intradental nerve-fiber excitability, directly interrupting the pain-signaling pathway rather than physically occluding the tubule (contrast with stannous fluoride's tubule-occlusion mechanism, see [[stannous-fluoride]]). The label explicitly notes this benefit requires a period of use (14–28 days) to establish — consistent with the "stop use after 4 weeks if unresolved" warning standard across potassium-nitrate brands.

## Dosing
Per Sensodyne label (potassium nitrate 5% / sodium fluoride 0.24%): apply at least a 1-inch strip to a soft-bristle toothbrush, brush thoroughly for ≥1 minute, twice daily (morning/evening), no more than 3x/day. **Stop use and consult a dentist if sensitivity persists after 4 weeks** — a real, specific label timeline consistent with the "benefit builds with repeated use, not an immediate fix" framing for this ingredient.

## Efficacy Evidence

**Poulsen et al. 2006 Cochrane review (PMID 16855970, updating a 2001 review, PMID 11405992)** — RCTs of potassium-containing toothpaste vs. non-potassium control toothpaste for dentine hypersensitivity, sensitivity assessed via thermal/tactile/air-blast/subjective methods:
- 6 studies in meta-analysis
- **Air blast sensitivity: SMD -1.25 (95% CI -1.65 to -0.851) in favor of treatment at 6-8 weeks — statistically significant**
- Tactile sensitivity: also statistically significant at 6-8 weeks
- **Subjective assessment (what the patient actually reports) FAILED to reach statistical significance at 6-8 weeks** — a real, important disconnect between instrument-measured sensitivity and patient-perceived relief
- **Authors' own conclusion: "no clear evidence is available for the support of potassium containing toothpastes for dentine hypersensitivity,"** citing the small number of individuals studied and the fact that the effect varies depending on which assessment method is used
- The earlier 2001 version found a similar pattern (4 pooled studies, air blast SMD -1.51, 95% CI -2.09 to -0.94) with the same "no strong evidence" conclusion language
- Disclosed conflict of interest: one review author received a prize from a toothpaste manufacturer (Zendium Inc.) in 1997 — old and minor, but disclosed and worth noting for transparency

**Honest bottom line:** this is a case where instrument-based measures show a real, sizeable statistical effect, but the review authors themselves are unwilling to call the evidence for potassium nitrate's dentine-hypersensitivity benefit "clear" or "strong" — largely because patients' own subjective reports didn't confirm it and the underlying trial base is small. This is a meaningfully more cautious picture than this database's prior "moderate-to-strong evidence" framing suggested, and the correction is preserved here rather than smoothed over.

## Key Safety Data
No specific adverse-effect incidence data was located in the sources gathered. Label directs seeking immediate medical attention if swallowed in amounts exceeding normal brushing use, and stopping use if the sensitivity problem persists or worsens (a possible sign of a more serious underlying dental issue, per the label's own framing).

## Contraindications
Not specifically addressed beyond the general label warning above. Children under 12 should consult a dentist or doctor before use per the Sensodyne label.

## Formulation Caveat
Most commercial potassium-nitrate desensitizing toothpastes (including Sensodyne, per the label pulled above) are COMBINATION products with sodium fluoride for anticavity benefit — the efficacy discussion above concerns the potassium-nitrate/desensitizing component specifically, not the fluoride/anticavity component (see [[fluoride-toothpaste]] for that ingredient's own, much stronger evidence base).

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Per this database's own subcategory file (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed): **Sensodyne — 65%** of Toothpaste, Sensitive Gums/Teeth recommendations (1,030,462/month category volume), far ahead of Colgate Sensitive (15%), Crest Gum & Sensitivity (12%), Arm & Hammer Sensitive (5%), Tom's of Maine (3%). This is a pharmacist recommendation share, not an efficacy rate — and per the Cochrane evidence above, no brand's market dominance should be read as proof of a clearly-established clinical effect.
