# Salicylic Acid

**Drug Class:** Keratolytic (beta hydroxy acid)

## Indication
Common and plantar warts, acne, hyperkeratosis, corns/calluses, seborrheic dermatitis/dandruff. This page's strongest documented evidence is for wart removal specifically (17% concentration, per the DailyMed label pulled).

## Mechanism
Keratolytic action — breaks down the outer skin layer (stratum corneum). Salicylic acid is a beta hydroxy acid (BHA) with a hydroxyl group on the second carbon; it has higher lipophilicity than glycolic acid (an alpha hydroxy acid), allowing deeper skin penetration.

## Dosing
Per Compound W label (salicylic acid 17% w/w liquid): wash affected area (optional 5-minute warm-water soak), dry thoroughly, apply one drop at a time to cover each wart, allow to dry, repeat once or twice daily **as needed for up to 12 weeks**.

## Efficacy Evidence

**Kwok et al. 2012 Cochrane review (PMID 22972052)** — 85 trials, 8,815 participants, topical treatments for cutaneous non-genital warts in healthy immunocompetent adults/children:
- **Salicylic acid vs. placebo, all sites: RR 1.56 (95% CI 1.20–2.03)** — a significant, real increase in wart clearance. Pooled cure rate across placebo-controlled trials: **73% (SA) vs. 48% (control)** — closely matching StatPearls' independently-cited "60-80% cure rate in 12 weeks" figure for keratolytic wart treatment generally.
- **Site matters a lot: hands RR 2.67 (95% CI 1.43–5.01) vs. feet RR 1.29 (95% CI 1.07–1.55)** — salicylic acid is considerably more effective for hand warts than plantar (foot) warts, though both reach statistical significance.
- **Cryotherapy vs. placebo: RR 1.45 (95% CI 0.65–3.23) — NOT significant** (favored neither treatment nor control) — a real, sometimes-overlooked finding, since cryotherapy is often assumed to be the "stronger" option.
- **Cryotherapy vs. salicylic acid head-to-head: no significant difference, RR 1.23 (95% CI 0.88–1.71)** — the two are roughly comparable overall, contrary to a common assumption that freezing is clearly superior.
- Aggressive cryotherapy beat gentle cryotherapy (RR 1.90, 95% CI 1.15–3.15), but with more adverse effects — a real efficacy/tolerability tradeoff.
- **Combining SA + cryotherapy beat SA alone: RR 1.24 (95% CI 1.07–1.43)** (2 trials, 328 participants) — combination therapy has a genuine edge over either single approach.
- Many included trials were judged high risk of bias in at least one domain — a real quality caveat on this otherwise large evidence base.

No comparably detailed RCT/meta-analysis data specific to salicylic acid for ACNE was located in this search pass — consistent with this database's standing framing that acne benefit is generally less robust/well-documented than for benzoyl peroxide or retinoids (see [[benzoyl-peroxide]]). This is flagged as a real evidence gap for the acne indication rather than papered over with the wart data, which does not transfer across indications or concentrations.

## Key Safety Data — systemic salicylate toxicity risk (real, from aspirin/salicylate pharmacology)
Topical salicylic acid is absorbed percutaneously, and excessive application (especially high concentration over large body-surface areas, e.g., in children) carries a real, described systemic salicylate toxicity risk — the same underlying compound class as aspirin overdose, per StatPearls' salicylate pharmacology data (drawn from the aspirin-focused literature since it shares the toxic mechanism):
- Therapeutic salicylate range: 150–300 mcg/mL; **toxic level: >300 mcg/mL**
- Mild toxicity: tinnitus, dizziness, lethargy, nausea, vomiting
- Severe toxicity: hyperthermia, tachypnea/respiratory alkalosis, high anion gap metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, hypoglycemia, seizures, coma, cerebral edema
- Management: urinary alkalinization, activated charcoal/bowel irrigation, airway protection; hemodialysis for severe cases
- Elimination kinetics switch from first-order to zero-order at higher concentrations — toxicity can escalate disproportionately with repeated/excessive use, not just a single large exposure

## Contraindications
Per Compound W label: do not use on irritated, infected, or reddened skin; on moles, birthmarks, hairy warts, genital warts, or facial warts; or if you have diabetes or circulatory problems (both conditions impair wound healing and increase infection/ulceration risk from keratolytic tissue destruction).

## Drug Interactions
Not specifically addressed in the sources gathered beyond the general systemic-salicylate caution above.

## Special Population Flags
Diabetes and circulatory problems are explicit label contraindications, not merely relative cautions — impaired healing/sensation in these populations raises real injury risk from a keratolytic product. No specific pregnancy/pediatric dosing guidance was located in the sources gathered for topical salicylic acid specifically.

## Overdose / Toxicity
See Key Safety Data above (systemic salicylate toxicity). Label directs avoiding eye contact (flush 15 minutes if exposed) and not inhaling vapors.

## Formulation Caveat
Product concentration and body-surface area matter significantly — the evidence above is specifically for 17% salicylic acid liquid used on warts per the labeled 12-week course; this should not be assumed to transfer to lower-concentration acne formulations or to different application patterns.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)

## Discrepancy Flag (real, unresolved — 2026-08-26)
This page's own Compound W label lists diabetes and circulatory problems as explicit contraindications for salicylic acid use (see Contraindications above). Kerasal Intensive Foot Repair — a lower-concentration (~5%) salicylic acid product — is nonetheless marketed and heavily pharmacist-recommended specifically for diabetic foot care (5% share of the "Diabetic Foot Cream" Pharmacy Times category). A podiatry trade-publication commentary (two podiatrists' clinical-experience opinion, not a controlled trial) states the lower concentration "does not appear to have deleterious effects" in diabetic patients, but this does not formally resolve the labeled contraindication — the underlying evidence base does not include a trial testing salicylic acid specifically in diabetic feet at either concentration. This tension is preserved rather than resolved in either direction; see `drugs/Kerasal.md` for the full brand-side discussion.
