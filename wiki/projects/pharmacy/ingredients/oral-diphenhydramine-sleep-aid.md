# Oral Diphenhydramine (Sleep Aid Use)

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** First-generation H1-antihistamine, used off-label-by-convention as an OTC nighttime sleep aid
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Low — the FDA recognizes diphenhydramine up to 50mg as a safe/effective OTC sleep aid, but independent systematic reviews describe the evidence base as lacking robust support for efficacy, and the ingredient carries a well-established, guideline-level "avoid in older adults" safety flag distinct from any efficacy question

## Indication
OTC nighttime sleep aid for occasional sleeplessness, frequently combined with an analgesic (acetaminophen, ibuprofen, or naproxen sodium) in "PM"-branded combination products for simultaneous pain relief and sleep aid. See [[topical-diphenhydramine]] for the separate topical-route use (itch/pain relief) of the same molecule — mechanistically and risk-profile distinct from this oral/systemic sleep-aid use.

## Mechanism
First-generation H1-antihistamine; readily crosses the blood-brain barrier (unlike second-generation antihistamines, see [[second-generation-antihistamines]]) producing central sedation as its dominant OTC sleep-aid effect. Also has anticholinergic (muscarinic-receptor-blocking) activity, the basis of both its "drying" side effects (dry mouth) and its more serious cognitive/functional risks in vulnerable populations (see Key Safety Data).

## Dosing
FDA-recognized OTC nighttime sleep-aid dose: diphenhydramine HCl up to 50mg at bedtime for adults. Commercial "PM" combination products typically use a lower 25mg (or molar-equivalent 38mg of the citrate salt) dose paired with an analgesic, rather than the full 50mg single-ingredient sleep-aid dose. Some evidence suggests a 100mg dose is not substantially more effective than 50mg, while anticholinergic side effects may increase with the higher dose.

## Efficacy Evidence
A systematic review of OTC agents for occasional disturbed sleep/transient insomnia found that commonly used OTC sleep aids, diphenhydramine included, generally **lack robust clinical evidence supporting efficacy and safety** for this use. A separate systematic review focused on older adults found some positive evidence: diphenhydramine and melatonin appeared to improve certain sleep measures with minimal adverse effects specifically in older people in the studies reviewed — but this finding sits in real tension with the Beers Criteria safety flag below (see Key Safety Data), since the same population shown modest sleep benefit in that review is the population guideline bodies recommend AVOID diphenhydramine for, on anticholinergic-risk grounds. This tension is presented here rather than resolved — the efficacy-in-elderly finding and the avoid-in-elderly safety guidance both come from real, cited sources and are not straightforwardly reconcilable within the scope of this pass.

## Key Safety Data — Anticholinergic Burden and the AGS Beers Criteria (the most clinically important safety finding for this ingredient)
The **American Geriatrics Society (AGS) Beers Criteria** — the standard US clinical guideline for potentially inappropriate medications in older adults — lists first-generation antihistamines, including diphenhydramine BY NAME, as medications to **avoid for most or all older adults**. Specific reasons cited: diphenhydramine is highly anticholinergic, its clearance is reduced with advancing age, tolerance develops when used as a hypnotic (undermining long-term sleep-aid effectiveness even if short-term benefit exists), and it carries a real risk of confusion, dry mouth, constipation, and other anticholinergic toxicity. **Cumulative anticholinergic drug exposure (of which diphenhydramine is a common contributor) is associated with increased risk of falls, delirium, and dementia — an association reported even in younger adults, not only the elderly.** The Beers Criteria allow an exception for acute treatment of severe allergic reactions, but not for routine sleep-aid or combination-analgesic use — directly relevant to "PM" combination products, which are marketed for and commonly used by exactly the older-adult population this guideline flags.

## Contraindications
Per standard OTC label conventions: not for children under 12 (in "PM" combination products); avoid use with other diphenhydramine-containing products (additive anticholinergic/sedative risk, same overdose-mechanism logic documented for the topical route on [[topical-diphenhydramine]]); do not use with alcohol or other sedatives without consulting a doctor; do not use with warfarin without medical guidance.

## Special Population Flags
- **Older adults (the single most important flag for this ingredient):** AGS Beers Criteria recommends avoidance for most/all older adults, per the anticholinergic-burden/fall/delirium/dementia-association evidence above — in real tension with one systematic review's modest efficacy finding specifically in this same population, a genuine unresolved contradiction in the literature rather than a settled recommendation
- **Children:** not for use in "PM" combination products under age 12 per standard OTC labeling
- **Anyone taking another diphenhydramine-containing product** (oral or topical): additive absorption/sedation/anticholinergic risk

## Overdose / Toxicity
Diphenhydramine overdose produces an anticholinergic toxidrome: sedation progressing to agitation/delirium in some cases, dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, tachycardia, and in severe cases seizures or cardiac conduction abnormalities. No specific antidote for routine OTC-dose overdose; supportive care is standard. See [[topical-diphenhydramine]] for a documented pediatric fatality case from the topical route specifically (different exposure route, same underlying toxicology).
