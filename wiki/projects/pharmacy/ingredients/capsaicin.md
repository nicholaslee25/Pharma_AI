# Capsaicin

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Topical TRPV1 agonist (counterirritant/neuropathic-pain agent)

## Indication
OTC (0.1% cream): minor muscle/joint pain from arthritis, simple backache, strains, sprains, bruises. Broader systematic-review-supported uses (not all necessarily at OTC concentrations): neuropathic pain (nondiabetic and postherpetic neuralgia), osteoarthritis, chronic musculoskeletal pain, post-mastectomy pain syndrome, burning mouth syndrome, overactive bladder, pruritus/pruritus ani, postoperative sore throat, chemotherapy/radiotherapy-induced mucositis.

## Mechanism
TRPV1 (vanilloid receptor) agonist — activation floods calcium into nerve cells, causing mitochondrial dysfunction that renders nociceptive fibers non-functional. Also depletes substance P (a key pain-signaling neurotransmitter) from neurons with repeated application — this explains the classic capsaicin pattern of an initial burning sensation (from acute substance-P release/neurogenic inflammation) followed by longer-term reduced pain sensitivity as substance P is depleted. An additional proposed mechanism is calcium-induced death of TRPV1-bearing neurons, potentially prolonging the analgesic effect.

## Dosing (per Capzasin HP FDA OTC label)
Adults/children >18y: apply and massage into the affected area, no more than 3–4 times daily. **Do not bandage tightly or apply local heat** (e.g., heating pads) — this combination has been linked to serious burns. Note: a prescription-only **8% patch** exists for neuropathic pain, a substantially higher concentration than the OTC 0.1% cream, applied under medical supervision — these are genuinely different products, not just different strengths of the same routine use case.

## Contraindications
No absolute contraindications identified in the sources reviewed, though patients with asthma show heightened sensitivity to capsaicin. OTC label: do not apply to wounds or damaged/broken/irritated skin; avoid eyes and mucous membranes.

## Key Safety Data
- **Common/expected:** local erythema, local pain, local pruritus — largely expected given the drug's own mechanism (transient burning sensation typically resolves within several days of continued use)
- **Rare but real:** serious burns have been reported with this class of product, especially when combined with tight bandaging or local heat (explicitly warned against on the label)
- **Less common systemic:** nausea, hypertension
- **Rare:** neurological symptoms, throat irritation
- **Toxicity data:** animal LD50 >9 mg/kg (subcutaneous), lethal mechanism is respiratory paralysis at toxic doses; no human overdose cases documented. High-dose/accidental acute exposure (e.g., pepper-spray-level exposure) causes burning pain, eye damage (photophobia, blurry vision), and respiratory symptoms (dry coughing, wheezing) — this is a different exposure scenario than routine topical OTC cream use, included here for completeness rather than to suggest routine use carries this risk

## Special Population Flags
- **Asthma:** heightened sensitivity noted
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** OTC label says ask a health professional before use
- **Pediatric:** OTC label restricts to ages >18y — notably more restrictive than many other OTC topical analgesics in this database

## Overdose / Toxicity
See Key Safety Data above — no human overdose cases documented; animal toxicity data suggest a wide margin of safety at topical OTC concentrations, with the main real-world risk being local burns from misuse (tight bandaging, heat, application to broken skin) rather than systemic toxicity.
