# Dimenhydrinate

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** First-generation antihistamine (8-chlorotheophylline salt of diphenhydramine) with anticholinergic properties

## Indication
Prevention and treatment of motion sickness symptoms (nausea, dizziness, vomiting) — OTC label indication. StatPearls also lists vertigo-related nausea. Historically also used off-label for pediatric gastroenteritis-related vomiting in some countries (Canada, Germany) — see Efficacy Evidence below.

## Mechanism
Blocks H1 histamine receptors in the CNS, specifically the area postrema and the vomiting center in the vestibular nucleus; also has anticholinergic activity (muscarinic receptor blockade) at the same sites. Must cross the blood-brain barrier to work. Dimenhydrinate is chemically the 8-chlorotheophylline salt of diphenhydramine — the theophylline (xanthine) component is intended to offset some of diphenhydramine's sedation, though sedation remains a common effect in practice.

## Dosing
**Adult (StatPearls):** 50–100 mg PO every 6 hours as needed, max 400 mg/day.
**Pediatric (Dramamine For Kids label, dimenhydrinate 25mg chewable):**

| Age | Dose | Frequency | Daily Max |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 2 years | Do not give without doctor approval | — | — |
| 2 to under 6 years | 1/2 to 1 tablet | Every 6–8 hours | 3 tablets/24h |
| 6 to 12 years | 1 to 2 tablets | Every 6–8 hours | 6 tablets/24h |

First dose should be taken 1/2 to 1 hour before the activity expected to cause motion sickness.

## Efficacy Evidence

**Uhlig et al. 2009 (PMID 19752076, Pediatrics)** — prospective RCT, 243 children with gastroenteritis-related vomiting (mild/no dehydration), rectal dimenhydrinate vs. placebo, all receiving oral rehydration therapy:
- **Note: this trial studies gastroenteritis-related vomiting, not motion sickness specifically** — flagged explicitly since it is the strongest pediatric RCT with hard numbers located for this ingredient in this pass, not a perfect match to the page's core (motion sickness) indication
- Primary outcome (weight gain at 18–24h): **no difference** between groups
- Vomiting frequency: mean **0.64 episodes** (dimenhydrinate) vs. **1.36 episodes** (placebo)
- **69.6%** of the dimenhydrinate group vs. **47.4%** of the placebo group were completely vomiting-free in the follow-up window
- Adverse effects, hospital admission, fluid intake, and general well-being were similar between groups
- **Authors' own conclusion: overall benefit is LOW** because dimenhydrinate did not improve oral rehydration success or overall clinical outcome (weight gain), despite reducing vomiting frequency itself — a genuinely mixed, not strongly positive, verdict
- StatPearls separately notes dimenhydrinate/diphenhydramine carry efficacy "similar to dexamethasone and droperidol" for reducing postoperative nausea/vomiting (a different, non-motion-sickness use), though not typically first-line for that indication

No dedicated pediatric motion-sickness RCT with hard cure/prevention-rate numbers was located in this specific search pass — this is a real evidence gap worth flagging rather than papering over with the gastroenteritis trial's numbers as if they were motion-sickness-specific.

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity to dimenhydrinate. Lower respiratory tract disease (particularly asthma) warrants cautious use, especially under age 2 — per StatPearls, caution with all antihistamines under age 2 given respiratory depression risk documented with related agents (promethazine specifically).

## Key Safety Data
- Common anticholinergic effects: CNS depression/sedation, fatigue, blurred vision, dry mouth, constipation
- **Paradoxical excitation reported in some children** — the opposite of the expected sedation, an important counseling point since parents may not anticipate it
- Dimenhydrinate specifically can cause paradoxical vomiting and may worsen narrow-angle glaucoma
- Cardiac effects to monitor for in overdose/high-dose settings: tachycardia, prolonged QTc

## Drug Interactions
Synergistic CNS depression with alcohol, sedatives, hypnotics, tranquilizers — avoid combining. Label also directs asking a doctor before use if the patient takes sedatives or tranquilizers.

## Special Population Flags
- **Pediatric:** dose must be age-specific (see Dosing table); do not give under age 2 without doctor approval; paradoxical excitation is a specifically documented pediatric risk.
- **Pregnancy:** StatPearls notes doxylamine is generally the preferred antihistamine in pregnancy; diphenhydramine and loratadine have also been used safely — dimenhydrinate itself was not specifically addressed in the source.
- **Breastfeeding:** antihistamines generally should be avoided due to potential lactation-inhibiting effect; second-generation antihistamines are preferred where an antihistamine is needed.
- **Respiratory disease/glaucoma:** ask a doctor before use per OTC label (breathing problems like emphysema/chronic bronchitis, or glaucoma).
- **Monitoring flag:** use caution in patients with epilepsy, chronic alcohol use, or pre-existing heart disease (StatPearls).

## Overdose / Toxicity
Management: airway stabilization, supplemental oxygen, continuous monitoring, IV access, ECG. Sodium bicarbonate is an option for prolonged QRS/arrhythmias. Benzodiazepines for agitation/seizures. Physostigmine (an anticholinesterase) may be warranted for severe toxicity but requires toxicologist consultation and bedside atropine availability given its own risks. OTC label directs contacting Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) for suspected overdose.
