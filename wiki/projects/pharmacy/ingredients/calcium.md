# Calcium

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Mineral supplement (dietary supplement, not FDA-regulated as a drug except as an antacid — see [[antacids]])
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Moderate for correcting inadequate intake and bone health (with vitamin D — see [[vitamin-d]]); genuinely contested for cardiovascular safety, with a real, published, and disputed 2010 meta-analysis finding increased MI risk from calcium-alone supplementation
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/calcium/nih-ods-calcium-health-professional.md, raw/primary-sources/calcium/pubmed-bolland-2010-cv-risk.md

---

## Indication
Correction of inadequate dietary calcium intake; supports bone health/osteoporosis prevention (with vitamin D) in older adults — FDA has approved a health claim for calcium+vitamin D reducing osteoporosis risk. Also marketed for preeclampsia risk reduction in pregnancy (inadequate-intake populations).

## Mechanism
Structural component of bone/teeth; the small ionized calcium pool in blood/extracellular fluid/tissues mediates vascular contraction/dilation, muscle function, blood clotting, nerve transmission, hormonal secretion. Absorbed via active transport (dominant at lower intakes, vitamin-D-dependent) and passive diffusion (larger share at higher intakes).

## Dosing (NIH ODS RDA and Tolerable Upper Intake Level)

| Population | RDA | Upper Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Adults | 1,000–1,200mg | 2,000–2,500mg, varies by age |
| Infants/children/adolescents | 200–1,300mg, varies by age | 1,000–3,000mg, varies by age |

Absorption from supplements depends on the calcium salt used (commonly carbonate or citrate) and the total elemental calcium taken at one time — splitting doses generally improves absorption efficiency.

## Efficacy / Safety Evidence

**FDA-approved health claim:** calcium+vitamin D supplements may reduce osteoporosis risk in older adults — **however, NIH ODS itself notes not all clinical trials have found these supplements improve bone health or reduce fracture risk** in this population (see [[vitamin-d]] for the detailed Cochrane fracture-prevention data, which found vitamin D+calcium together, not calcium alone, produces the measurable fracture-risk reduction).

**Bolland et al. 2010 meta-analysis (PMID 20671013)** — patient-level and trial-level meta-analyses of RCTs of calcium supplements WITHOUT co-administered vitamin D:
- 15 trials; patient-level data (5 trials, 8,151 participants): 143 MIs (calcium) vs. 111 (placebo), HR **1.31** (95% CI 1.02–1.67, p=0.035)
- Trial-level data (11 trials, 11,921 participants): pooled RR **1.27** (95% CI 1.01–1.59, p=0.038)
- Non-significant increases in stroke (HR 1.20) and composite MI/stroke/sudden death (HR 1.18); no significant mortality increase (HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.96–1.23)
- **Authors' conclusion:** calcium supplements without vitamin D are associated with increased MI risk; given widespread use, this modest per-person risk could carry meaningful population-level impact; called for reassessing calcium's role in osteoporosis management
- **This finding was genuinely disputed** — at least 5 separate published rebuttal letters followed in BMJ, arguing the evidence was weak or non-generalizable. NIH ODS's own current framing is correspondingly hedged: "some prospective cohort studies and RCTs have found that calcium supplements might actually increase cardiovascular disease risk, while other studies have found no link... more research is needed."

**This vault presents both findings plainly rather than picking a side**: a real, peer-reviewed, disputed signal exists for calcium-alone supplementation and cardiovascular risk, and the scientific community has not reached consensus.

## Other NIH ODS Health-Outcome Summaries
- **Cancer:** observational link to lower colorectal cancer risk not always confirmed in supplementation trials; high dairy-calcium intake may be associated with HIGHER prostate cancer risk; mixed results for ovarian/breast cancer
- **Preeclampsia:** calcium supplements might reduce risk in pregnant women with inadequate baseline intake
- **Weight management:** unclear, inconsistent findings
- **Metabolic syndrome:** limited evidence suggests possible risk reduction, especially in women, but more research needed

## Pharmacokinetics
Absorbed via active transport (vitamin-D-dependent, dominant at lower intakes) and passive diffusion (larger share at higher intakes). Status best assessed via DXA bone mineral density (reflects cumulative lifetime status) — serum/plasma total calcium is NOT a good indicator of individual calcium status, since the body tightly regulates blood levels independent of tissue stores.

## Contraindications
Not itemized specifically in the sources reviewed beyond the excessive-intake risks below.

## Key Safety Data
- Higher supplemental calcium intake may increase **kidney stone** risk
- Potential increased **cardiovascular disease** risk from calcium-alone supplementation (see Bolland 2010 above) — a genuinely disputed finding
- Higher intake might increase **prostate cancer** risk
- **Deficiency** can cause osteoporosis (weak/fragile bones, fall/fracture risk) and contribute to rickets/osteomalacia (though these are more commonly vitamin-D-deficiency-driven)

## Drug Interactions
May interact with: **dolutegravir, levothyroxine, lithium, quinolone antibiotics** (typically via reduced absorption of the other drug when co-administered — calcium is a well-known chelator of several drug classes, though exact mechanism/direction was not detailed further in the source reviewed).

## Special Population Flags
- **Postmenopausal women:** at-risk group for inadequate intake, and the population most studied for both the bone-health benefit and the disputed cardiovascular risk signal
- **Dairy avoiders:** at-risk group for inadequate intake
- **Non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic Asian populations, people in poverty:** per NHANES data, more likely to have inadequate calcium intake
- **Pregnancy:** calcium supplementation may reduce preeclampsia risk specifically in those with inadequate baseline intake

## Overdose / Toxicity
See Key Safety Data above — Tolerable Upper Intake Level 2,000–2,500mg (adults) or 1,000–3,000mg (children/adolescents), varying by age; exceeding this chronically raises kidney stone and (per the disputed evidence above) possible cardiovascular risk.
