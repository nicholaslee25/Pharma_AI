# Witch Hazel (Hamamelis virginiana)

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Astringent (FDA OTC monograph — anorectal/skin protectant category)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Low-to-moderate — long-standing clinical use and professional-society (American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons) recognition for conservative hemorrhoid management, but no dedicated large-scale randomized controlled trial data was located specifically isolating witch hazel's effect; evidence is closer to accepted clinical practice/guideline-level than trial-level, a real distinction from more rigorously studied ingredients elsewhere in this database
**Last updated:** 2026-08-27
**Sources:** WebSearch (PMC review "Natural Products in Hemorrhoid Management," uspharmacist.com "Counseling Patients With Hemorrhoids," DailyMed Tucks label)

---

## Indication
FDA OTC monograph use as an astringent: temporary relief of external anal itching, burning, and irritation associated with hemorrhoids; also marketed more broadly for minor skin irritation. External use only.

## Mechanism
Tannins present in witch hazel extract produce a local protein-coagulant (astringent) effect on tissue surfaces, constricting blood vessels, reducing capillary permeability, and minimizing local irritation — a physical/chemical tissue-tightening action rather than a pharmacological receptor-based mechanism (contrast with [[rectal-phenylephrine]]'s alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction or [[hydrocortisone]]'s corticosteroid anti-inflammatory action).

## Efficacy Evidence
A 2025 narrative review ("Natural Products in Hemorrhoid Management: A Comprehensive Literature Review," PMC12128686) states that topical witch hazel preparations (medicated pads, ointments) "have demonstrated efficacy in reducing anal itching, mild bleeding, burning, and inflammation" in external or Grade I-II internal hemorrhoids, and notes it is recognized by the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons as a conservative-management option. **However, this review does not present specific randomized controlled trial data or quantitative outcome measures for witch hazel** — the evidence characterization is clinical-use/guideline-level, not RCT-level. By contrast, the same review notes that horse chestnut extract (a different botanical, working via the saponin aescin on venous tone — not used in any brand in this database) does have cited randomized trial evidence for hemorrhoid symptom improvement. This is a real, worth-noting evidence-strength gap between witch hazel's popularity/guideline-recognition and the depth of its own dedicated trial base, relative to some other ingredients in this database.

## Key Safety Data
No specific adverse-effect data beyond general astringent-class mild local irritation was located in this pass. External use only — not indicated for internal/intra-rectal application per OTC monograph convention.

## Contraindications
External use only; discontinue and consult a doctor if bleeding occurs, symptoms worsen, or no improvement within 7 days (standard OTC anorectal-product convention, see uspharmacist.com general guidance).

## Special Population Flags
No specific pregnancy/lactation/pediatric data was located in this pass beyond the standard OTC anorectal-product age/duration conventions applied across this category.

## Formulation Caveat
Witch hazel is most commonly sold as a pre-moistened topical pad (the format used by [[Tucks]]) rather than a cream/ointment — a real, distinct application format from the vasoconstrictor/anesthetic/corticosteroid products elsewhere in this category, which are almost universally cream, gel, or suppository formats.
