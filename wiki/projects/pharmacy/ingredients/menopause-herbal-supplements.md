# Menopause Herbal Supplements (Black Cohosh / Soy Isoflavones)

**Type:** Active Ingredient (class)
**Drug Class:** Phytoestrogen/herbal dietary supplements — black cohosh (Actaea racemosa) and soy isoflavones (daidzein/genistein), marketed for menopausal hot flash/night sweat relief. Not FDA-approved drugs; regulated as dietary supplements (DSHEA), no FDA premarket efficacy review.
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Mixed/Low-Moderate — individual trials and some meta-analyses show modest hot-flash reduction for both black cohosh and soy isoflavones, but Cochrane-level reviews describe evidence as inconsistent/low-quality, and mechanism (phytoestrogen vs. non-estrogenic for black cohosh) remains scientifically unsettled
**Last updated:** 2026-08-28
**Sources:** WebSearch (NCCIH black cohosh/soy isoflavone summaries, brand-label ingredient searches, PMC pooled-RCT analysis for succinate-based Amberen)

## Indication
Marketed (structure/function claims only, not FDA-approved disease claims) for relief of menopausal hot flashes, night sweats, mood changes, and related symptoms.

## Mechanism
**Soy isoflavones** (daidzein, genistein): structurally resemble estradiol and bind estrogen receptors (preferentially ER-beta) — a genuine phytoestrogen mechanism. **Black cohosh:** historically assumed to be a phytoestrogen, but NCCIH notes more recent research suggests it may act through non-estrogenic pathways (e.g., serotonergic) — the mechanism is NOT settled science despite widespread "phytoestrogen" marketing shorthand, worth flagging as a real gap between common marketing framing and current NCCIH-summarized science.

## Efficacy Evidence
- **Black cohosh:** NCCIH states evidence is mixed; some trials show modest reduction in hot-flash frequency/severity, others show no benefit over placebo. No consistent dose-response established across the trial base.
- **Soy isoflavones:** modest hot-flash reduction shown in some RCTs/meta-analyses, but effect sizes are generally smaller than hormone therapy and heterogeneous across trials (dose, isoflavone source, population).
- **Net assessment (this pass):** neither ingredient has Cochrane-level high-certainty efficacy evidence comparable to what this database documents for e.g. [[mood-health-supplements]]' St. John's Wort — both sit in a real, unresolved lower evidence tier despite substantial market share and decades of use.

## Key Safety Data
- Black cohosh: rare case reports of hepatotoxicity (liver injury) have prompted cautionary labeling in some markets; causality not definitively established, but flagged by regulatory bodies as a real, if uncommon, signal.
- Soy isoflavones: theoretical concern in estrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer given phytoestrogen activity — patients with a personal/family history of hormone-sensitive cancers should discuss with a physician before use (a real, sourced caution, not an established contraindication).

## Special Population Flags
- **Hormone-sensitive cancer history:** discuss with physician before using either ingredient given phytoestrogen mechanism (soy) or historically-assumed phytoestrogen mechanism (black cohosh).
- **Liver disease/hepatotoxicity risk:** black cohosh specifically; discontinue and seek care for signs of liver injury (jaundice, dark urine, abdominal pain).
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** not established as safe; not indicated for this population regardless (product is menopause-specific).

## Brand-Specific Divergence — Amberen Is Not a Phytoestrogen Product
**Real, significant finding:** [[Amberen Multi-Symptom Menopause Relief]] (15% share in this category) does NOT use black cohosh or soy isoflavones at all — its formula is ammonium succinate, calcium disuccinate, magnesium disuccinate, zinc difumarate, glycine, monosodium L-glutamate (MSG), and vitamin E, marketed as working through a claimed hypothalamic-mediated mechanism rather than direct phytoestrogen activity. This makes "Women's Health/Menopause Supplements" a category where the top three brands split across two genuinely different, non-overlapping proposed mechanisms (phytoestrogen vs. succinate-based) — not a case of one shared ingredient with different doses. See the Brand-Specific Alignment Check on [[Amberen Multi-Symptom Menopause Relief]] for the two-sided evidence picture (a real published pooled RCT analysis exists, alongside real independent criticism that MSG/proprietary-blend components are filler rather than active).
