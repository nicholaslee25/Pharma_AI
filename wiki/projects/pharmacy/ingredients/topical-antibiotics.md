# Topical Antibiotics (Bacitracin / Polymyxin B / Neomycin)

**Type:** Active Ingredient (class)
**Drug Class:** Topical antibiotic/antiseptic
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Not automatically superior to simple wound care — a real 922-patient RCT found plain petrolatum performs comparably on overall infection rate, while these antibiotics carry a well-documented, quantified contact-allergy risk that petrolatum does not
**Last updated:** 2026-08-26
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/topical-antibiotics/statpearls-bacitracin-topical.md, raw/primary-sources/topical-antibiotics/pubmed-dekoven-2018-nacdg-patch-test.md, raw/primary-sources/petrolatum/pubmed-smack-1996-jama-rct.md (shared cross-reference)

## Indication
Minor cuts, scrapes, and burns — bacitracin FDA-approved since 1948 for preventing/treating acute and chronic localized skin infections. Available as a single agent (bacitracin) or combined with neomycin and polymyxin B ("triple antibiotic," e.g., Neosporin).

## Mechanism
Bacitracin: cyclic polypeptide, bacteriostatic and bactericidal, inhibits mucopeptide transfer into bacterial cell walls, blocking cell wall synthesis. Effective against gram-positive bacteria; most gram-negative organisms are resistant — this is why triple-antibiotic combinations add polymyxin B/neomycin specifically for broader gram-negative coverage.

## Dosing
Apply sufficient ointment (bacitracin: 500 units/g) to affected area after cleansing with mild soap and water. **Do not use topically for more than 7 days unless directed by a physician** (StatPearls) — a real, specific duration limit, not an open-ended "use as needed" product.

## Efficacy Evidence — the "not automatically superior" claim, verified

**Smack et al. 1996 (PMID 8805732, JAMA)** — the pivotal RCT already documented in full on [[petrolatum]]: 922 patients, 1,249 wounds after dermatologic surgery, white petrolatum vs. bacitracin:
- **Overall infection rate: 2.0% (petrolatum) vs. 0.9% (bacitracin) — NOT statistically significant (P=0.37)**
- Staph-specific infections: 8 of 9 petrolatum-group infections were *S. aureus* vs. ZERO in the bacitracin group (P=0.004, this WAS significant) — bacitracin's antimicrobial action did prevent staph infections specifically, even without lowering overall infection risk
- **Allergic contact dermatitis: 0% (petrolatum) vs. 0.9% (bacitracin)** — a real, if modest, allergy-risk cost that plain petrolatum does not carry
- Conclusion: the two performed comparably on overall infection outcomes, directly supporting the "not automatically superior to petrolatum for uncomplicated wounds" framing rather than a default assumption that "antibiotic ointment = automatically better."

## Key Safety Data — Contact Allergy (real, quantified, and persistent over decades)
- **Bacitracin was designated "Contact Allergen of the Year" in 2003** by the American Contact Dermatitis Society (StatPearls).
- Bacitracin was the 6th most common patch-test allergen in 2005-2006 data.
- **NACDG 2015-2016 patch test results (PMID 30422882, DeKoven et al., Dermatitis 2018)** — 5,597 patients across 13 North American centers, standardized 70-allergen screening: neomycin and bacitracin both remained among the top-10 most frequent allergens, at approximately **7.0% and 6.9% prevalence** respectively among tested patients — a persistent, multi-decade signal, not a one-time finding.
- **Anaphylactic reactions to bacitracin have been reported**, and neomycin has been implicated in life-threatening contact urticaria and anaphylaxis in its own right, despite being chemically unrelated to bacitracin — simultaneous sensitization to both is well documented, likely due to coreactivity from combined-product exposure patterns rather than shared chemistry.
- Common reaction sites: legs/feet, particularly in patients using these products on stasis dermatitis or chronic leg ulcers — a population where repeated, prolonged exposure raises sensitization risk further.
- Common general adverse effects: fever, hives, itching, swelling, difficulty breathing, nausea, vomiting.

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity to bacitracin, neomycin, polymyxin B, or formulation components. Cross-sensitivity between bacitracin and neomycin is possible even though the two are chemically unrelated.

## Drug Interactions
Intramuscular (not topical) bacitracin should avoid concurrent use with streptomycin, kanamycin, polymyxin E, and neomycin due to nephrotoxicity risk — included for completeness though less directly relevant to OTC topical use.

## Formulation Caveat
Bacitracin, polymyxin B, and neomycin (alone or combined) are not necessarily interchangeable in either efficacy or allergy risk — neomycin in particular carries a well-documented, sometimes higher, contact-dermatitis and rare-anaphylaxis risk. A single-agent bacitracin product is not the same risk profile as a triple-antibiotic combination product, and evidence for one should not be assumed to transfer directly to the other.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Per this database's own subcategory file (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed): **Neosporin — 57%** (a triple-antibiotic combination — neomycin, polymyxin B, bacitracin), **Bacitraycin Plus — 10%**, **Polysporin — 10%** (bacitracin/polymyxin B, no neomycin) of Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics recommendations (1,546,639/month category volume), alongside Hibiclens 8% (chlorhexidine-based antiseptic, a different mechanism — see [[chlorhexidine]]), Dermoplast 4%, A&D 3%, Bactine 3%, Betadine 3% (iodine-based antiseptic, yet another mechanism), Alocane 2%. This is a pharmacist recommendation share, not proof that antibiotic ointment outperforms plain petrolatum — per the RCT evidence above, it generally does not for uncomplicated wounds, while carrying a real, quantified allergy-risk cost that petrolatum does not.

## Brands Using This Ingredient (in this database — formulation not individually verified per SKU)
**Neosporin** — confirmed triple-antibiotic (bacitracin zinc, neomycin sulfate, polymyxin B sulfate) per its own DailyMed label, pulled 2026-08-26 during brand-page research (see `drugs/Neosporin.md`). The same parent brand also sells **Simply Neosporin**, a neomycin-free 2-ingredient variant (bacitracin zinc + polymyxin B sulfate) explicitly marketed for neomycin-allergic users — "Neosporin" is not one fixed formulation. **Polysporin** — confirmed neomycin-free (bacitracin zinc + polymyxin B sulfate) per its own DailyMed label, pulled 2026-08-26 (see `drugs/Polysporin.md`); also confirmed hosted under the same corporate/Neosporin.com family as Neosporin. **Bacitraycin Plus** — confirmed bacitracin zinc 500 units + **pramoxine HCl 10mg** (external analgesic) per its own DailyMed label, pulled 2026-08-26 (see `drugs/Bacitraycin Plus.md`). **Pramoxine is a topical anesthetic in a chemical class distinct from both benzocaine and lidocaine, and has no dedicated evidence page in this database** — [[topical-anesthetics]] is explicitly scoped to benzocaine/lidocaine only; this is a real, flagged coverage gap, not an assumption of safety or risk either way. **Bactine MAX** (Burn Treatments/Topical Anesthetics/Liquid Bandages) — confirmed to span at least 3 different formulations under one name, one of which (the Ointment SKU) is lidocaine 40mg + bacitracin zinc 500 units + polymyxin B sulfate 10,000 units, i.e. a genuine member of this ingredient class alongside an anesthetic; see `drugs/Bactine MAX.md` for the full multi-formulation breakdown.

**Discrepancy flagged: [[A&D]] and [[A+D First Aid Ointment]]** are filed in this database under Burn Treatments and Topical Antibiotics/Antiseptics respectively, but their own DailyMed labels confirm active ingredients of **lanolin 15.5% + petrolatum 53.4%** — skin protectants, with NO antibiotic or antiseptic action of any kind. These are mechanism mismatches with the rest of this category, not antibiotic/antiseptic products at all — see [[petrolatum]] for their actual applicable evidence base.

**Additional confirmed antiseptic-class (non-antibiotic) brands in this category, each a genuinely different chemistry:** **Alocane** — benzalkonium chloride 4% (antiseptic, quaternary ammonium) + lidocaine HCl 4% (anesthetic); also appears in Burn Treatments and Sun Burn Relief. **Bactine** (plain) — benzalkonium chloride only (~0.13%), no anesthetic, no antibiotic — a simpler formula than the same-named Bactine MAX line. **Betadine** — povidone-iodine 10% (iodine-based broad-spectrum antiseptic). **Hibiclens** — chlorhexidine gluconate 4.0% w/v (biguanide antiseptic; see [[chlorhexidine]] for a flagged mismatch between that ingredient page's oral-rinse-only evidence base and Hibiclens' topical skin-antisepsis use case). None of these four is a peptide antibiotic (bacitracin/neomycin/polymyxin) — comparing their real-world performance to the Smack 1996 RCT evidence above (which tested bacitracin specifically) is not a like-for-like comparison.
