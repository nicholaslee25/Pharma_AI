# Loperamide

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Antidiarrheal — peripherally-restricted mu-opioid receptor agonist (phenylpiperidine opioid)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** High for therapeutic-dose symptomatic relief of acute diarrhea; the dominant real-world safety story is high-dose abuse cardiotoxicity, not therapeutic-dose risk
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/loperamide/statpearls-loperamide.md, raw/primary-sources/loperamide/dailymed-imodium-ad.md

---

## Indication
FDA-approved: traveler's diarrhea, IBS with chronic diarrhea, acute nonspecific diarrhea (≥2 years), reducing ileostomy output. Off-label: chemotherapy-related diarrhea (after excluding infection/colitis).

## Mechanism
Lipophilic synthetic opioid, mu-receptor agonist. At therapeutic doses acts locally on intestinal muscle mu-receptors to slow transit/inhibit peristalsis, with minimal CNS penetration because P-glycoprotein normally excludes it from the brain. At high (abuse) doses, P-glycoprotein becomes saturated, letting loperamide cross the blood-brain barrier and produce central opioid effects (euphoria) — and it also directly blocks cardiac Na+ channels (QRS widening) and the hERG channel (QTc prolongation), the mechanistic basis for its dangerous high-dose cardiotoxicity.

## Dosing (per Imodium A-D FDA OTC label)

| Population | Initial | Subsequent | Max/24h |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adults/children ≥12y | 2 caplets (4mg) | 1 caplet (2mg)/loose stool | 4 caplets (8mg) |
| Children 9–11y (60–95 lbs) | 1 caplet (2mg) | ½ caplet (1mg) | 3 caplets (6mg) |
| Children 6–8y (48–59 lbs) | 1 caplet (2mg) | ½ caplet (1mg) | 2 caplets (4mg) |
| Children 2–5y | Ask a doctor | — | — |
| Children <2y | Do not use | — | — |

StatPearls prescription-context dosing: chronic use 2mg twice daily; prescription max 16mg/day (vs. 8mg/day OTC max) — the OTC and prescription max doses are meaningfully different, worth not conflating.

## Pharmacokinetics
Peak plasma 4–5h. Oral bioavailability **<1%** due to extensive first-pass metabolism — at labeled doses almost no active drug reaches systemic/CNS circulation, which is the pharmacokinetic reason loperamide is safe as an OTC opioid at normal doses despite sharing a mechanism class with prescription opioids. Hepatic metabolism via CYP2C8/CYP3A4. Half-life ~9–14h (StatPearls gives slightly different ranges in different sections, 7–19h and 9.1–14.4h). Primarily fecal excretion.

## Contraindications
Age <2 years; acute ulcerative colitis; bloody diarrhea; diarrhea associated with bacterial infection; hypersensitivity to loperamide/excipients.

## Boxed Warning (FDA)
Torsades de pointes and sudden death when used at higher-than-recommended doses.

## Key Safety Data
- **Common:** dry mouth, flatulence, abdominal cramps, nausea, ileus, constipation, urinary retention, dizziness, drowsiness
- **Serious:** toxic megacolon, necrotizing enterocolitis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, QT/QTc prolongation, torsade de pointes, ventricular tachycardia ("VT storm" = ≥3 episodes/24h), cardiac arrest, syncope
- **Abuse/misuse trend:** 91% increase in reported loperamide exposures 2010–2015, including 8 deaths — driven by non-medical high-dose use for opioid-withdrawal self-management or euphoria-seeking. FDA responded in September 2019 with new package-size limits and unit-dose packaging specifically to curb high-dose access.

## Drug Interactions
CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein substrate. **Lonafarnib** (CYP3A4 inhibitor): raises loperamide levels — limit to 1mg/day initially if combined. **P-gp inducers** (reduce loperamide effect): carbamazepine, mitotane, phenytoin, rifampicin. **P-gp inhibitors** (raise loperamide levels/CNS penetration risk — use caution): amiodarone, carvedilol, ritonavir, quinidine, itraconazole, verapamil, clarithromycin, ranolazine.

## Special Population Flags
- **Pediatric:** contraindicated <2y; not recommended for infectious diarrhea in pediatric patients generally
- **Hepatic impairment:** use with caution, no specific dose adjustment given
- **Renal impairment:** no dose adjustment needed, mild-severe
- **Pregnancy:** limited data; oral rehydration/dietary changes first-line; lowest loperamide dose only if symptoms disabling
- **Breastfeeding:** manufacturers recommend against use, due to variable breast-milk concentrations
- **Elderly:** use cautiously — paralytic ileus risk

## Overdose / Toxicity
High-dose use produces opioid-like effects (euphoria, miosis, CNS/respiratory depression) plus potentially life-threatening dysrhythmias via cardiac sodium/potassium channel blockade. ECG: QRS widening, QTc prolongation (>450ms males, >470ms females). Risk factors: electrolyte abnormalities (Mg, K, phosphate). **Management:** supportive care primary; naloxone for respiratory depression with **≥24h observation after naloxone** (loperamide outlasts naloxone's half-life — a critical clinical point given loperamide is an opioid); sodium bicarbonate for QRS widening; isoproterenol or transcutaneous pacing for QTc prolongation; standard ACLS for cardiac arrest.
