# Rectal/Topical Phenylephrine (Hemorrhoid Vasoconstrictor)

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Alpha-1 adrenergic agonist (sympathomimetic), used topically/rectally as a vasoconstrictor — a distinct use context from [[oral-phenylephrine]]'s decongestant indication
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Low — an FDA-recognized OTC monograph active ingredient with a plausible, well-understood local mechanism, but no prospective randomized controlled trial evidence was located showing it reduces hemorrhoidal bleeding or prolapse; evidence is largely mechanistic/consensus rather than trial-based
**Last updated:** 2026-08-27
**Sources:** WebSearch (Medscape/WebMD/drugs.com phenylephrine rectal monographs, uspharmacist.com "Counseling Patients With Hemorrhoids"), DailyMed brand labels (Preparation H, RectiCare Advanced)

## Indication
FDA OTC monograph use: temporary relief of local itching and discomfort associated with hemorrhoids; temporarily shrinks hemorrhoidal tissue and reduces swelling via local vasoconstriction. This is a categorically different pharmacological context from [[oral-phenylephrine]]'s nasal-decongestant use — this page covers topical/rectal application only.

## Mechanism
Direct-acting alpha-1 adrenergic receptor agonist. Applied locally to hemorrhoidal/anorectal tissue, it produces vasoconstriction that restricts blood supply to swollen hemorrhoidal tissue, reducing swelling; also believed to relieve itching associated with local histamine release. **Important distinction from [[oral-phenylephrine]]:** oral phenylephrine's well-documented failure as a decongestant (FDA's 2023 non-GRASE determination) stems specifically from extensive first-pass gut/liver metabolism when swallowed — a pharmacokinetic problem that does not apply to direct topical/rectal application to the target tissue itself. This page's use case should not be assumed equally ineffective on that basis; the two are genuinely different delivery contexts, even though the underlying molecule and receptor mechanism are identical.

## Efficacy Evidence
FDA OTC monograph recognizes phenylephrine HCl (or another approved vasoconstrictor) at 0.25% as an effective amount for relieving hemorrhoid-associated swelling — this monograph status was established administratively (as with most OTC monograph actives) rather than through a modern large-scale RCT program. **No prospective randomized controlled trial was located in this pass demonstrating that topical phenylephrine reduces hemorrhoidal bleeding or prolapse** — per the sources reviewed, vasoconstrictors in this class "may improve local symptoms but do not treat the underlying disorder," and this specific evidence gap (no RCTs for bleeding/prolapse outcomes) is explicitly noted in more than one general reference source. This is a real, worth-flagging limitation for an ingredient combined into the majority (73% share) of this category's leading brand.

## Key Safety Data
Long-term use is discouraged due to local skin/mucosal irritation risk. Because phenylephrine is a systemic vasoconstrictor even when applied locally, absorption through irritated/broken anorectal tissue is a real concern distinct from application to intact skin.

## Contraindications
Per uspharmacist.com and drug-reference sources: should be avoided in patients with **heart disease, hypertension, thyroid disease, diabetes, or urination difficulty from an enlarged prostate**, and in those taking prescription medications for hypertension or depression (a genuine drug-interaction concern given phenylephrine's alpha-adrenergic systemic activity if absorbed). These are the same general categories of caution associated with sympathomimetic drugs used systemically, applied here out of caution for a topical/rectal product — not itemized every time on every brand's consumer-facing label, but a real underlying pharmacological consideration.

## Drug Interactions
Potential additive effect with other sympathomimetics; interaction concern with antihypertensive and antidepressant medications per the contraindication note above (mechanism not independently re-derived in this pass beyond the general sympathomimetic drug-interaction class).

## Special Population Flags
Not recommended for children under 12 without doctor consultation (consistent with the broader OTC anorectal-product convention, see brand pages). No specific pregnancy/lactation data was located in this pass.

## Formulation Caveat
FDA OTC hemorrhoid monograph products commonly combine phenylephrine with protectants (mineral oil, petrolatum, cocoa butter — see [[zinc-oxide]] and [[petrolatum]]) and, in some brands, a topical anesthetic (pramoxine or lidocaine, see [[topical-anesthetics]]) or hydrocortisone (see [[hydrocortisone]]) in the same product — a consumer's actual symptom relief in a combination product reflects several ingredients acting through different mechanisms, not phenylephrine alone.
