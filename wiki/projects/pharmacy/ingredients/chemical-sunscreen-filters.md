# Chemical Sunscreen Filters (Avobenzone, Octinoxate, Octisalate, Octocrylene, Oxybenzone, Homosalate)

**Type:** Active Ingredient (class)
**Drug Class:** Organic (chemical) UV filters — FDA OTC monograph sunscreen actives
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** High for general UV-protection/photodamage-reduction efficacy; Moderate for skin-cancer-prevention in humans specifically (limited RCT evidence, mostly for squamous cell carcinoma); the ingredients themselves carry an unusual, well-documented **regulatory-limbo status** — not declared unsafe, but not affirmed GRASE either — plus a real, quantified systemic-absorption finding
**Last updated:** 2026-08-27
**Sources:** FDA 2019 proposed rule (Federal Register, "Sunscreen Drug Products for Over-the-Counter Human Use"), Matta et al. 2020 JAMA RCT (systemic absorption), 2015 Cochrane systematic review (skin cancer prevention), Nambour Trial (melanoma RCT), Hawaii/Key West coral-reef legislation coverage

---

## Indication
FDA OTC monograph: prevention of sunburn and, with regular use alongside other sun-protection measures, decreased risk of skin cancer and early skin aging caused by the sun. This is the shared ingredient class behind the "Sunscreen" and "Sunburn Relief"-adjacent SPF products across many brands in this database's Topicals research (e.g., [[Aquaphor Lip Repair]], [[Sun Bum]], [[Nivea]], [[Cicatricure]], [[Banana Boat]]).

## Mechanism
These are organic ("chemical") UV filters: aromatic-ring chromophore molecules that absorb specific wavelengths of UV radiation and dissipate the energy as heat via a photochemical excitation/relaxation cycle, rather than physically blocking or reflecting light. Coverage varies by molecule and is why products combine several: **octinoxate, octisalate, homosalate** primarily absorb UVB; **avobenzone** is the only FDA-monograph filter providing meaningful UVA1 (long-UVA) coverage but is **photounstable** — it degrades on UV exposure unless paired with a stabilizer, most commonly **octocrylene** (which also independently absorbs UVB/short-UVA); **oxybenzone** absorbs across a broad UVB/UVA2 range. This is why "broad-spectrum" labeling on a chemical-filter product typically reflects a multi-ingredient combination rather than any single filter.

## FDA Regulatory Status — a real, well-documented limbo, not a safety verdict
The FDA's February 2019 proposed rule evaluated all 16 sunscreen active ingredients then marketed in the US and found:
- **Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide: GRASE** (Generally Recognized As Safe and Effective) up to 25% concentration — see [[mineral-sunscreen-filters]].
- **PABA and trolamine salicylate: NOT GRASE** (no longer used in modern US sunscreens as a result).
- **The remaining 12, including all six covered on this page (avobenzone, oxybenzone, octocrylene, octisalate, octinoxate, homosalate):** **insufficient safety data to make a positive GRASE determination** — this is explicitly **not** a finding of unsafe or ineffective, but a data gap the FDA asked industry to fill (additional systemic-absorption, carcinogenicity, and developmental/reproductive toxicity studies). These ingredients remain legally marketed under the existing (pre-1999) monograph while the rule remains proposed rather than final.

## Systemic Absorption Evidence
**Matta et al. 2020 (JAMA), FDA-funded randomized clinical trial**, maximal-use conditions, 4 commercial sunscreen products, 6 active ingredients (avobenzone, oxybenzone, octocrylene, homosalate, octisalate, octinoxate): **all six ingredients, in all four tested products, produced blood plasma concentrations exceeding the FDA's 0.5 ng/mL threshold** (the level above which the FDA requires additional nonclinical safety studies) after a single application. **Oxybenzone reached the highest levels** — maximum plasma concentrations of 258.1 ng/mL (lotion) and 180.1 ng/mL (aerosol spray) — with 100% of participants exceeding the threshold at 2 hours. Octinoxate reached lower but still threshold-exceeding levels (7.9 ng/mL nonaerosol spray, 5.2 ng/mL pump spray), with 75% of participants still exceeding threshold at 8 hours. **The study authors explicitly stated the clinical significance of these findings is undetermined** — exceeding the FDA's conservative safety-study trigger threshold is not the same as demonstrated harm, but it is a real, quantified data point that motivated the ongoing GRASE data request above.

## Skin Cancer Prevention Evidence
- **Nambour Trial (Australia), the only RCT of sunscreen specifically for melanoma prevention:** a nonstatistically-significant protective effect for all melanomas combined (11 in the sunscreen group vs. 22 in control), but a **statistically significant** protective effect for invasive melanoma specifically (3 vs. 11 cases) — real but based on small case counts.
- **2015 Cochrane systematic review:** identified evidence (from the same trial line) supporting a reduction in **squamous cell carcinoma** incidence with regular sunscreen use, and a lesser, less certain effect on basal cell carcinoma. A pooled meta-analysis of roughly a dozen case-control studies did **not** confirm the general assumption that sunscreen reduces melanoma risk at the population level — a genuinely mixed, not uniformly positive, human evidence picture for melanoma specifically (in contrast to strong experimental/mechanistic evidence that sunscreen reduces UV-induced skin damage generally).
- A Norwegian cohort analysis estimated SPF ≥15 use reduces melanoma risk by ~30% relative to lower-SPF use, with a larger effect in high-risk subgroups — real, but observational (not RCT) evidence.

**Net finding, stated plainly:** the mechanistic and photodamage-reduction case for sunscreen is strong, and there is real RCT-level evidence for invasive-melanoma and squamous-cell-carcinoma reduction — but the human evidence base for melanoma specifically is smaller and more mixed than commonly assumed, an important nuance for a "sunscreen prevents skin cancer" claim frequently treated as settled.

## Environmental/Reef-Safety Controversy (oxybenzone, octinoxate specifically)
**Hawaii banned the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate effective January 1, 2021** (legislation passed 2018), based on evidence that both compounds disrupt the photosynthesis of zooxanthellae (coral-symbiotic algae), contributing to coral bleaching and DNA damage. Key West, FL passed a similar ban (2019) that was subsequently overridden at the state level by Florida's governor and is not currently in effect. This is a real, enacted regulatory action in at least one US jurisdiction (Hawaii), distinct from and additional to the systemic-absorption/GRASE data-gap issue above. Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide (mineral filters) are not subject to either ban.

## Contraindications / Warnings
External use only; avoid eye contact; discontinue if irritation/rash develops (a real, if uncommon, contact-allergy risk exists for several of these filters, oxybenzone in particular). Standard FDA-mandated label language: consistent use with other sun-protection measures decreases skin cancer/early-aging risk; this product does not prevent all sun damage.
