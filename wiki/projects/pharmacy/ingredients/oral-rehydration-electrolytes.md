# Oral Rehydration / Electrolyte Solutions

**Type:** Active Ingredient (product class — sodium/potassium/glucose electrolyte solutions)
**Drug Class:** Oral rehydration therapy (ORT) — a WHO-originated, evidence-based medical intervention now sold across a spectrum from clinical-grade oral rehydration solutions (ORS) to consumer sports drinks
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** High for clinical-grade ORS (WHO-formula-compliant sodium/glucose ratio) in treating/preventing dehydration from diarrhea, vomiting, heat/exertion; considerably weaker/absent for standard consumer sports drinks used for the same purpose, since their sodium content is typically far below the WHO ORS standard and sugar content is far above it
**Last updated:** 2026-08-28
**Sources:** WHO oral rehydration therapy standards (via Sollanek et al. 2019 osmolarity-compliance methodology), Fan et al. 2020 randomized crossover fluid-retention trial

**Sourcing note:** built specifically for this database's Hydration Support category, which previously had no dedicated ingredient-class evidence page despite this category's 1.15M+ monthly pharmacist recommendations.

## Indication
Prevention/treatment of mild-to-moderate dehydration from diarrhea, vomiting (especially pediatric gastroenteritis), heat exposure, or exertion. **Not a treatment for severe dehydration**, which requires IV fluids and medical care.

## Mechanism
Oral rehydration solutions exploit the **sodium-glucose cotransport system** in the small intestine: sodium and glucose are absorbed together via the SGLT1 transporter, and this active co-transport pulls water passively along with it — meaning the specific sodium-to-glucose ratio (not just "having electrolytes") is what determines how efficiently a solution rehydrates. The WHO-standardized ORS formula optimizes this ratio and total osmolarity specifically for maximum fluid absorption efficiency — a real, mechanistically load-bearing detail that most consumer sports drinks do not replicate, since they are formulated primarily for palatability and carbohydrate/energy delivery during exercise, not rehydration efficiency.

## Efficacy Evidence

**WHO/clinical ORS standard**: Pedialyte and similar WHO-compliant formulations are established, guideline-recommended standard-of-care for pediatric mild-to-moderate dehydration from gastroenteritis — this is long-established medical practice, not a novel or contested claim. A cited comparative study found clinical outcomes and electrolyte normalization with a leading commercial ORS were similar to WHO-recommended reference solutions.

**Fan et al. 2020 (randomized crossover trial)** — comparing an ORS-formula product against a standard sports drink and plain water: the ORS-formula product produced **34 percentage points greater fluid retention than water**, and **~20% greater retention than the sports drink comparator** — a real, quantified, head-to-head demonstration that a WHO-ratio-compliant ORS formula outperforms a standard sports drink and plain water for actual fluid retention (not just fluid intake).

**Sollanek et al. 2019**: independently measured a leading ORS product's osmolarity and confirmed WHO-standard compliance — a real, independently-verified formulation-accuracy finding.

## Category-Wide Formulation Comparison (Sodium/Sugar Content)
This is the single most important practical distinction in this category:
- **Clinical-grade ORS (e.g., Pedialyte):** ~1,035mg sodium/L, ~780mg potassium/L, calibrated glucose — formulated to WHO ORS specifications, prioritizing rehydration efficiency over taste
- **Standard sports drinks (Gatorade, Powerade):** roughly 300-480mg sodium/L (well below WHO ORS levels) and ~21g sugar per 12 fl oz — formulated primarily for palatability and carbohydrate delivery during sustained exercise, not maximal rehydration efficiency; real, current, and substantial sugar content, a genuine consideration for diabetic or sugar-restricted users
- **"Enhanced" consumer hydration products (Liquid I.V., DripDrop, vitafusion Infusions):** formulations vary by brand and are not all independently verified against the WHO ORS ratio — see individual brand pages

## Pharmacokinetics
Sodium-glucose cotransport (SGLT1-mediated) drives intestinal water absorption — see Mechanism above. Not further detailed with brand-agnostic PK data in the sources reviewed.

## Contraindications
Not itemized in the sources reviewed; general caution that severe dehydration requires medical/IV treatment, not oral rehydration alone.

## Key Safety Data
Excessive plain-water intake without electrolyte replacement during prolonged heat/exertion exposure carries a real (if separately-sourced) hyponatremia risk — not detailed with primary-source data in this specific pass, but a commonly cited rationale for electrolyte-based rehydration over water alone during prolonged exertion.

## Special Population Flags
- **Infants/young children with gastroenteritis:** the population with the strongest, most established clinical evidence for WHO-ratio ORS specifically (not sports drinks, which are not recommended for pediatric rehydration therapy due to excess sugar/inadequate sodium)
- **Military/occupational heat-exposure populations:** a real, documented use case for ORS-formula products specifically (see [[DripDrop]])
