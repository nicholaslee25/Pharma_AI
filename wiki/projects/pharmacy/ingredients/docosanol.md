# Docosanol

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Topical antiviral (long-chain saturated fatty alcohol), OTC cold sore treatment
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Moderate — the pivotal registration RCT shows a real, statistically significant, but modest effect (~18 hours faster healing); the "aborted episode" outcome (arguably the most meaningful one to a patient) was NOT statistically significant
**Last updated:** 2026-08-26
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/docosanol/dailymed-abreva.md, raw/primary-sources/docosanol/pubmed-sacks-2001-rct.md

## Indication
OTC treatment of cold sores/fever blisters (herpes simplex labialis, HSL) on the face or lips — 10% cream concentration. Recurrent HSL affects an estimated 20–40% of the US population (Sacks et al. 2001); it is self-limiting in immunocompetent individuals, but patients seek treatment for discomfort and visible-lesion concerns.

## Mechanism
Docosanol is a 22-carbon saturated primary fatty alcohol. Its proposed antiviral mechanism is inhibition of fusion between the host cell plasma membrane and the viral envelope, blocking viral entry into the cell — mechanistically distinct from nucleoside analog antivirals (e.g., acyclovir), which act after viral entry. This is a general pharmacology characterization drawn from secondary medical references (e.g., clinical drug guides); no dedicated StatPearls or NCBI Bookshelf article specific to docosanol was located in this search pass, which is a real evidence gap for this page.

## Dosing
Per Abreva label: wash hands before and after applying. Apply to the affected area at the first sign of symptoms (tingle, itch, bump) — early application is explicitly the key variable per the label and the pivotal trial's design. Apply 5 times daily until healed, with gentle rubbing. Adults and children 12+; children under 12 require medical consultation first.

## Efficacy Evidence

**Sacks et al. 2001 (PMID 11464183, J Am Acad Dermatol)** — two identical multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs (21 sites total), 370 docosanol-treated vs. 367 placebo-treated adults with documented HSL history, treatment started during prodrome/erythema stage, applied 5x daily:
- **Median time to healing: 4.1 days with docosanol — 18 hours shorter than placebo (P=0.008, 95% CI 2–22 hours)**
- Reduced time to cessation of pain/itching/burning/tingling (P=0.002, 95% CI 3–16.5 hours)
- Reduced time to complete healing of classic lesions (P=0.023, 95% CI 1–24.5 hours)
- Reduced time to cessation of ulcer/soft-crust stage (P<0.001, 95% CI 8–25 hours)
- **Aborted episodes (lesion never progressing past an early stage) — arguably the outcome patients care most about: 40% docosanol vs. 34% placebo, P=0.109 (95% CI for OR 0.95–1.73) — NOT statistically significant.** This is a real limitation, not a minor footnote: the trial's positive findings are about accelerating healing of a lesion that still occurs, not about preventing the lesion in the first place.
- Adverse experiences with docosanol were mild and similar to placebo — a genuinely clean safety profile
- A published comment by Spruance (2002, PMID 12196766) raised critical questions about this trial's design/interpretation — flagged here as an existing point of scientific debate not independently adjudicated in this pass

**Bottom line:** docosanol's real, quantified benefit is on the order of one day or less of faster healing when started early — genuinely modest, consistent with this database's original "not a cure, not dramatic" framing, and it does not reliably stop a lesion from developing once triggered.

## Contraindications
Known hypersensitivity to docosanol or product components. Not for ophthalmic or intraoral use (external use only, avoid eyes and inside the mouth per label).

## Key Safety Data
- Adverse experiences in the pivotal RCT were mild and similar in frequency to placebo — no signal of a distinctive safety problem specific to docosanol
- Label allergy alert: may cause severe reactions (hives, facial swelling, difficulty breathing) in rare cases
- Label directs not sharing the product/applicator, to avoid spreading the infection to others
- Label directs discontinuing and consulting a doctor if the sore worsens or does not heal within 10 days — a real clinical safety-net threshold, not an arbitrary number

## Drug Interactions
Not addressed with specific interaction data in the sources gathered — consistent with docosanol's topical-only, non-systemically-absorbed mechanism.

## Special Population Flags
- **Pediatric:** children under 12 require medical consultation before use per label; not studied in the pivotal RCT population (adults only).
- No pregnancy/breastfeeding-specific data was located in the sources gathered for this pass.

## Overdose / Toxicity
Topical, external use only. Label directs keeping away from children and contacting Poison Control if swallowed; no specific ingestion case data was located in this pass.

## Brands Using This Ingredient (in this database — formulation not individually verified per SKU)
Abreva — confirmed docosanol 10% per the specific DailyMed label pulled above. **All nine other Cold Sore Treatments brands now individually researched (2026-08-26)** — genuinely different mechanisms confirmed across the category:
- **Blistex** — confirmed its own dedicated "Cold Sore Treatment" SKU ALSO uses docosanol 10%, directly matching Abreva's mechanism; but the same brand separately sells a lidocaine-based "Fast-Acting Pain Relief" gel and a camphor/menthol/phenol "Medicated" lip ointment — three different mechanisms under one name. See `drugs/Blistex.md`.
- **Anbesol Cold Sore Therapy** — confirmed allantoin + benzocaine + camphor + petrolatum (anesthetic/protectant combination, no antiviral action) — see `drugs/Anbesol Cold Sore Therapy.md` and [[topical-anesthetics]].
- **Campho-Phenique** — confirmed camphor + phenol (counterirritant/antiseptic, no antiviral action) — see `drugs/Campho-Phenique.md`.
- **Carmex** — confirmed its dedicated Cold Sore Treatment SKU uses benzocaine + white petrolatum (anesthetic), while its more general Classic Lip Balm SKU uses camphor + white petrolatum (protectant only, no anesthetic) — see `drugs/Carmex.md`.
- **Orajel** (4% share here) — its benzocaine/menthol/zinc-chloride "All Mouth Sores" gel is the plausible driver — see `drugs/Orajel.md` and [[topical-anesthetics]].
- **Lip Clear Lysine+** — **discrepancy:** despite the brand name, the FDA-recognized active ingredient is menthol 0.1%, not lysine (lysine is inactive-ingredient-only) — see `drugs/Lip Clear Lysine+.md`.
- **Zilactin** — confirmed benzyl alcohol 10% (a third anesthetic chemical class, distinct from both benzocaine and lidocaine; not covered by [[topical-anesthetics]]'s benzocaine/lidocaine scope — gap flagged there) — see `drugs/Zilactin.md`.
- **Curoxen** (and its Canker Sore Treatments sibling entry, Curoxen Mouth Sore Relief) — confirmed homeopathic (calendula/clove oil/olive extract), explicitly "not FDA evaluated" — see `drugs/Curoxen.md`.
- **Mederma** — **category-placement discrepancy:** its Cold Sore Treatments-category product is most plausibly the non-drug "Cold Sore Discreet Patch" (a hydrocolloid cosmetic patch with no confirmed active ingredient), while its dominant 56% Scar Treatments share is a genuine allantoin-based drug — two different regulatory statuses under one brand. See `drugs/Mederma.md`.

**Bottom line for this category:** Abreva and one Blistex SKU are the only two products confirmed to share docosanol's antiviral mechanism; every other researched brand relies on anesthetic, counterirritant, protectant, homeopathic, or (for Mederma) non-drug mechanisms — none targets the underlying virus.
