# Docusate

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Stool softener (surfactant/emollient laxative)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Low — has NO FDA-approved indication, and StatPearls states plainly that numerous systematic reviews (including a 2021 review of 7 RCTs) found no significant difference vs. placebo, despite docusate remaining one of the most frequently used laxatives
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/docusate/statpearls-docusate.md, raw/primary-sources/docusate/dailymed-colace.md

## Indication
**No FDA-approved indication exists for docusate** — a genuinely important fact for an ingredient this widely used. Per product monographs (not FDA approval), used when peristaltic (stimulant) laxatives are contraindicated, to ease difficult/painful defecation — potentially useful for patients with painful anorectal conditions or cardiac comorbidities where straining should be avoided. OTC label framing: relief of occasional constipation.

## Mechanism
Surfactant action — reduces the surface tension of the oil-water interface within stool, allowing water and lipids to penetrate the stool mass, producing a softer, easier-to-pass stool.

## Dosing (per Colace FDA OTC label and StatPearls)

| Population | Dose |
|---|---|
| Adults/children ≥12y | 1–3 capsules (100–300mg) daily |
| Children 2–<12y | 1 capsule (100mg) daily, or per StatPearls 50–150mg divided |
| Children <2y | Consult a doctor |
| Oral calcium salt (StatPearls) | 240mg once daily |
| Rectal enema | 283mg/5mL, 1–3x/day |

Expected onset: bowel movement generally within 12–72 hours (label).

## Efficacy Controversy — StatPearls' own framing
"Numerous systematic reviews examining the efficacy of docusate have yielded no significant evidence to support its use." A **2021 comprehensive review found no significant difference between docusate and placebo across 7 RCTs**. Studies specifically on opioid-induced constipation found docusate **has not been shown to be more effective than placebo**. Despite this consistent negative evidence, docusate remains one of the most frequently prescribed/used laxatives, and professional organizations have recommended discontinuing its routine use in favor of evidence-based alternatives (e.g. osmotic or stimulant laxatives with better trial support).

**This is a deliberately foregrounded honest finding:** docusate is extremely commonly used (61% of this database's Stool Softeners category recommendations go to Colace — see Pharmacy Times Context below) despite this weak trial evidence base, and this vault states that plainly rather than assuming high usage implies high efficacy.

## Pharmacokinetics
Significant first-pass hepatic metabolism, forming active and inactive metabolites; high plasma protein binding. No specific renal/hepatic dose adjustment given in the source reviewed.

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity to docusate/ingredients; nausea/vomiting (the drug's mechanism depends on adequate fluid intake, which is compromised by vomiting); intestinal obstruction, appendicitis symptoms, acute abdominal pain, fecal impaction; risk of dependency with use beyond 7 days; benzyl-alcohol-containing formulations contraindicated in neonates (gasping syndrome).

## Key Safety Data
- Anorexia, diarrhea, vomiting usually associated with excess doses
- Rare: abdominal cramping, rash
- Syrup/liquid formulations: throat irritation, bitter taste
- Chronic/excessive use: laxative dependency risk with potential hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis
- OTC label: stop use if rectal bleeding occurs, no bowel movement results, or a stool softener is needed for more than 1 week

## Drug Interactions
Use with caution alongside **mineral oil** — docusate may increase mineral oil absorption, potentially causing systemic lipid granulomas. Colace OTC label specifically: do not use with mineral oil unless directed by a doctor.

## Special Population Flags
- **Pregnancy:** limited data; one case report of symptomatic neonatal hypomagnesemia from chronic maternal use, but no broader evidence linking docusate to adverse pregnancy outcomes
- **Breastfeeding:** minimally absorbed from the GI tract, unlikely to be present in breast milk
- **Geriatric:** commonly prescribed despite limited evidence supporting effectiveness (consistent with the broader efficacy controversy above), though may help avoid straining post-surgery or post-myocardial-infarction — a rationale based on avoiding Valsalva strain, not proven laxative efficacy per se

## Overdose / Toxicity
Not addressed with a dedicated overdose section in the sources reviewed — chronic excessive use risk (electrolyte disturbance, dependency) is covered under Key Safety Data above.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Colace recommendation shares (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed): Stool Softeners category 61%, Colace 2-IN-1 (docusate + sennosides combination) 15% of Stimulant Laxatives category. These are pharmacist recommendation shares, not response/cure rates — and per the efficacy controversy detailed above, this high recommendation share should specifically NOT be read as evidence of strong clinical efficacy.

## Brands Using This Ingredient (in this database — formulation not individually verified per SKU)
Colace — confirmed docusate sodium 100mg per its own DailyMed label pulled above. **Colace 2-IN-1 confirmed 2026-08-27** as docusate sodium 50mg + sennosides 8.6mg — resolving the prior formulation hedge. See `drugs/Colace 2-IN-1.md`.

**Major finding (2026-08-27, Dulcolax brand-specific pass): "Dulcolax Stool Softener"/"Dulcolax Pink Stool Softener" confirmed via DailyMed as docusate sodium 100mg** — identical active ingredient/dose to Colace, sold under the Dulcolax brand family (whose core/best-known product is bisacodyl, a completely different stimulant-laxative mechanism per [[bisacodyl]]). This is a real, consequential one-brand-two-mechanisms finding: a consumer choosing "Dulcolax" without checking for the word "Stool Softener" could get either an FDA-approved stimulant laxative or a docusate product with no FDA-approved indication and a 2021-review-confirmed lack of placebo-superiority. See `drugs/Dulcolax.md`.
