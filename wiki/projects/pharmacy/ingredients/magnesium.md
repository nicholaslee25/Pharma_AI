# Magnesium

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Mineral supplement (dietary supplement; also present in some OTC laxatives/antacids as a drug ingredient)

## Indication
Correction of magnesium deficiency; migraine prevention (best-supported specific use — see Efficacy Evidence); also present as an active ingredient in some OTC laxatives (e.g. milk of magnesia) and antacids.

## Mechanism
Cofactor in >300 enzyme systems governing protein synthesis, muscle/nerve function, blood glucose control, and blood pressure regulation. Required for energy production, oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis; contributes to bone structural development; required for DNA/RNA/glutathione synthesis. Plays a role in active transport of calcium/potassium across cell membranes, important for nerve impulse conduction, muscle contraction, and normal heart rhythm.

## Dosing (NIH ODS RDA and Tolerable Upper Intake Level)

| Population | RDA | Upper Limit (supplemental only) |
|---|---|---|
| Adults | 310–420mg | 350mg |
| Infants/children/adolescents | 30–410mg, varies by age | 65–350mg, varies by age |

**Note:** the Tolerable Upper Intake Level applies to supplemental magnesium only, not magnesium from food — dietary magnesium alone poses low toxicity risk in healthy people even well above this number.

**Supplement form matters:** aspartate, citrate, lactate, and chloride forms have HIGHER bioavailability than magnesium oxide and magnesium sulfate — a real, sourced difference relevant to comparing products.

## Efficacy Evidence (by indication, per NIH ODS)

**Migraine prevention (strongest evidence):** low serum/tissue magnesium is associated with migraine occurrence; regular supplementation may reduce migraine frequency per some research. **The American Academy of Neurology and American Headache Society concluded magnesium is "probably effective" for migraine prevention** — a specific, named professional-society conclusion, though ODS notes the underlying data are limited.

**Hypertension/cardiovascular disease:** clinical trials show only a MARGINAL blood-pressure-lowering effect from supplementation. Observational studies link higher intake/serum levels to lower sudden cardiac death, ischemic heart disease, and stroke risk, but this has not been confirmed as a supplementation effect specifically — more research needed.

**Type 2 diabetes:** cohort-study meta-analyses link higher dietary magnesium to lower T2D risk, but only a few small trials tested supplementation for glycemic control, with conflicting results. **The American Diabetes Association states there is not enough evidence to recommend magnesium supplementation for glycemic control.**

**Osteoporosis:** essential for bone health generally; some studies link higher intake to higher bone mineral density, others find low serum magnesium in osteoporotic women — but ODS states more research is needed on whether supplementation specifically prevents or manages osteoporosis.

## Pharmacokinetics
~30–40% of dietary magnesium is absorbed. Supplement absorption varies by form — liquid-soluble forms and the aspartate/citrate/lactate/chloride salts absorb better than oxide or sulfate forms. Kidneys control homeostasis, normally excreting ~120mg/day in urine (reduced when status is low). Serum magnesium is a poor indicator of total body/tissue status since <1% of total body magnesium (~25g in an adult) is in blood serum.

## Contraindications
Not itemized specifically in the sources reviewed beyond the excessive-intake risks below.

## Key Safety Data
- **Deficiency signs (early):** loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, weakness. **Progression:** numbness, muscle contractions/cramps, seizures, abnormal heart rhythms, coronary spasms. **Severe:** hypocalcemia or hypokalemia. Symptomatic deficiency is uncommon in healthy people, usually arising from specific health conditions or medications rather than diet alone
- **Excess (supplement/medication doses):** diarrhea, nausea, abdominal cramping
- **Toxicity (very high doses):** hypotension, vomiting, difficulty breathing, irregular heartbeat, cardiac arrest; a few fatal hypermagnesemia cases have been reported

## Drug Interactions
May interfere with absorption of: **oral bisphosphonates, tetracyclines, quinolone antibiotics**. Conversely, some medications reduce magnesium status: **diuretics, proton pump inhibitors** (relevant given how commonly PPIs are used long-term — see [[proton-pump-inhibitors]] for the matching hypomagnesemia risk noted on that page).

## Special Population Flags
- **At risk for inadequacy:** people with GI diseases, type 2 diabetes, or alcohol dependence, and older adults
- **Highest risk of low intake specifically:** men ≥71 years old and adolescents of both sexes, per US intake survey data

## Overdose / Toxicity
See Key Safety Data above — Tolerable Upper Intake Level for supplemental magnesium is 350mg (adults) or 65–350mg (children/adolescents, by age); toxicity from food alone is very unlikely in healthy people.
