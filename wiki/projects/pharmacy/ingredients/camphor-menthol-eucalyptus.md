# Camphor / Menthol / Eucalyptus Oil (Topical Vapor Rub)

**Type:** Active Ingredient (combination, topical)
**Drug Class:** Cough suppressant / topical analgesic (camphor, menthol); cough suppressant (eucalyptus oil)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Low-to-moderate — one real pediatric RCT shows a symptomatic/sleep benefit over petrolatum and no-treatment, but the mechanism is understood as sensory/perceptual rather than a true decongestant or expectorant effect, and the trial was industry-funded
**Last updated:** 2026-08-27
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/brands/vicks-vaporub/dailymed-vicks-vaporub.md, WebSearch (Pediatrics 2010 Paul et al. trial summary, Poison Control, Drugs.com combination monograph)

## Indication
Temporary relief of cough due to minor throat and bronchial irritation associated with the common cold; topical/sensory relief of minor muscle and joint aches (camphor/menthol as topical analgesics). Fills the same "children's cough/cold" caregiver need as oral pediatric cough-cold combinations, via a topical route instead.

## Mechanism
Not a true decongestant or expectorant. **Menthol** activates cold-sensitive (TRPM8) skin/nasal receptors, producing a cooling sensation that is perceived as easier breathing without any actual measurable change in nasal airflow. **Camphor** penetrates skin as a mild counter-irritant/anesthetic, reducing the perception of muscle/throat discomfort, and (with menthol) may stimulate mucin secretion and tracheal mucus transport in inflamed airways. **Eucalyptus oil** contributes a similar aromatic/cooling sensory effect. Per Mayo Clinic's own framing: the product doesn't clear nasal congestion, but its strong odor "tricks the brain" into a sensation of unobstructed breathing — a real, useful clinical distinction between subjective symptom relief and objective decongestant action.

## Efficacy Evidence
**Paul et al. 2010 (Pediatrics, Dec 2010)** — the primary pediatric RCT behind this ingredient combination: 138 children ages 2–11 with upper respiratory infection, randomized to vapor rub (Vicks VapoRub), petrolatum placebo, or no treatment, applied to neck/chest 30 minutes before bedtime, partially double-blinded:
- Children in ALL THREE groups improved overnight — a real "even placebo/no-treatment helps" finding, common in self-limited pediatric URI symptom trials
- Parents in the vapor-rub group reported the GREATEST relief of cough, congestion, and sleep difficulty vs. both petrolatum and no-treatment
- **Funding disclosure:** unrestricted research grant from Procter & Gamble, Vicks' manufacturer — a real industry-funding conflict worth flagging rather than omitting, though the trial's basic design (3-arm, partially blinded) is not inherently invalidated by the funding source
- No comparable brand-specific trial was located for Mentholatum's version of this same three-ingredient combination

## Contraindications
Not for use in children under 2 years (both Vicks VapoRub and Mentholatum Children's Nighttime Vaporizing Rub labels specify "children under 2 years: ask a doctor," and Vicks' own label states the product is unsafe for any use under 2). Not for application inside the nostrils, near the face/mucous membranes, or on broken/damaged skin.

## Key Safety Data
**Camphor is a well-documented pediatric toxicity risk.** Per Poison Control and case-report literature: camphor is toxic even at low concentrations if ingested or absorbed in excess through mucous membranes/broken skin, with a toxicity progression from sweating/agitation to seizures, cardiac arrhythmias, and cardiopulmonary arrest in severe cases — a genuinely serious risk profile, not merely a mild-irritant caution, disproportionately relevant given this ingredient combination's marketing specifically toward infants/children. Never apply near or inside the nose; never ingest.

## Drug Interactions
Not systemically dosed at labeled topical use; not addressed in the sources reviewed.

## Special Population Flags
- **Children under 2:** contraindicated per FDA OTC labeling for all camphor/menthol/eucalyptus combination products identified in this database
- **Infants generally:** the camphor toxicity risk above is the basis for an entire sub-market of "natural"/"non-medicated" alternative chest rubs specifically marketed for babies under 2 — see Formulation Caveat below, since several of these have NO active ingredient at all rather than a lower-camphor formulation

## Formulation Caveat — a major, recurring category-level finding
**Of the five ranked brands in Children's Topical Cough Suppressants, Ointments (Pharmacy Times), only two are confirmed genuine FDA-regulated camphor/menthol/eucalyptus drugs — the other three are non-medicated cosmetic products with NO active ingredient at all, despite being ranked and pharmacist-recommended in the same drug category:**
- **Vicks VapoRub (64% share):** camphor 4.8%, eucalyptus oil 1.2%, menthol 2.63% — genuine FDA Drug Facts label
- **Mentholatum Children's Nighttime Vaporizing Rub (12% share):** camphor 5.3%, eucalyptus oil 1.3%, menthol 2.8% — genuine FDA Drug Facts label, and **notably HIGHER camphor concentration than Vicks VapoRub (5.3% vs. 4.8%) despite being the brand specifically marketed as the "Children's"/nighttime product** — a real, counterintuitive potency finding worth a caregiver's attention given camphor's toxicity profile above
- **Zarbee's Naturals Soothing Baby Chest Rub (16% share):** confirmed via manufacturer labeling to be **menthol-free and camphor-free** — eucalyptus + lavender essential oils in a beeswax/shea-butter/castor-oil balm, carrying an explicit "these statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration" disclaimer. Contains wintergreen leaf oil (methyl salicylate) in at least one labeled variant — a real, separate salicylate-toxicity consideration distinct from camphor, since methyl salicylate is also dangerous if ingested by young children. No FDA Drug Facts panel identified.
- **Maty's All Natural Baby Chest Rub (6% share):** confirmed **menthol-free, camphor-free, petroleum-free** — essential oils of chamomile, lavender, dill weed, patchouli, eucalyptus radiata, and coriander in a sunflower/castor/coconut oil base, also carrying a "not evaluated by the FDA" disclaimer. Contains coconut oil — a tree-nut allergen under US labeling law, the same allergen class already flagged for L'il Critters Gummy Vites in this database's Children's Multivitamins category.
- **Frida Baby No-Mess Vapor Rub (2% share):** explicitly marketed as "Non-Medicated" with **no active ingredients at all** — a water-based formula with eucalyptus/lavender fragrance and no Drug Facts panel identified.

**This means roughly 24% of this category's pharmacist-recommendation share (16% + 6% + 2%) goes to products with zero FDA-recognized active ingredient, filed in the same "drug" category and ranked alongside genuine camphor/menthol/eucalyptus OTC drugs** — the most extreme version of the mechanism/category-mismatch pattern already seen with A&D/A+D in Topical Antibiotics and Burn Treatments elsewhere in this database, because here it isn't a mismatched *active ingredient*, it's the *absence* of one. This is not necessarily a criticism of the products themselves (they are marketed and used as gentle sensory-comfort balms for infants too young for camphor, a real and reasonable caregiver need) but a caregiver comparing "recommendation share" across this category without checking each label would have no way to know which products are regulated drugs and which are cosmetic-classified essential-oil balms.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Per this database's own subcategory file (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed): Vicks VapoRub 64%, Zarbee's Naturals Soothing Baby Chest Rub 16%, Mentholatum Children's Nighttime Vaporizing Rub 12%, Maty's All Natural Baby Chest Rub 6%, Frida Baby No-Mess Vapor Rub 2% of Children's Topical Cough Suppressants, Ointments recommendations (1,109,244/month category volume). This is a pharmacist recommendation share, not an efficacy rate — and per the Formulation Caveat above, three of these five brands are not FDA-regulated drugs at all, a distinction this recommendation-share data does not itself surface.
