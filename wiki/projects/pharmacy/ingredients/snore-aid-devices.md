## What This Page Covers — three genuinely different mechanisms in one Pharmacy Times category
1. **External adhesive nasal strips** ([[Breathe Right]]): an adhesive strip worn on the outside of the nose bridge, spring-tension design physically pulls the nostrils open from outside.
2. **Internal nasal dilators** ([[Mute]], and the nasal-dilator SKU sold under the [[Sleepwell]] name): a small flexible piece inserted just inside each nostril to hold the nasal valve open from within — a mechanically different approach from an external strip, not just a smaller version of the same idea.
3. **Mandibular advancement devices/mouthpieces (MADs)** ([[SnoreRx]], and the separate mouthpiece SKU sold under the [[Sleepwell]] name and within [[Air Sleep-Snore]]'s dual-component kit): a boil-and-bite or custom-fit oral appliance that repositions the lower jaw forward during sleep, opening the airway at the throat/retroglossal level — mechanistically unrelated to the two nasal-based approaches above, and the device class with the most established RCT support in the broader sleep-medicine literature for actual OSA severity (AHI) reduction, not just snoring.
4. **Aromatic/essential-oil drops** (marketed alongside the mouthpiece in some kits, e.g. [[Air Sleep-Snore]]'s "AirSnore Drops" component, and infused into the [[Sleepwell]] nasal-strip SKU): lavender, chamomile, ylang ylang, eucalyptus, peppermint, Scots pine oils — a non-device, aromatherapy-level addition with no independent snoring-efficacy trial located in this pass. Marketed for a relaxation/decongestant feel, not itself mechanically responsible for any airway-opening effect.

## Efficacy Evidence

**External nasal strips — Camacho et al. 2016 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMC5187471)**, Breathe Right strips and NoZovent: 112 studies screened, 58 reviewed, 14 included, 147 OSA patients with AHI data:
- **AHI: 28.7 ± 24.0 → 27.4 ± 23.3 events/hr, p=0.64 — not a statistically significant change**
- No significant change in AHI, lowest oxygen saturation, or snoring index for OSA patients specifically
- **Subgroup finding: internal nasal dilators showed a slight apnea-index reduction (−4.87 events/hr) vs. external nasal dilators (+0.64 events/hr, i.e. essentially no benefit or slightly worse)** — a real mechanistic distinction between the two nasal-dilator subtypes in this category, not just a marketing difference
- Separate, smaller RCTs (chronic-rhinitis and habitual-snorer populations, not general OSA) found Breathe Right reduced snoring frequency and improved sleep efficiency/reduced arousal index, without significantly changing objective AHI — meaning strips may help subjective sleep quality/snoring sound in some non-OSA populations even without reducing the medical severity metric (AHI)

**Mandibular advancement devices — broader sleep-medicine literature (PMC4598931, RCT crossover)**: MADs are a well-established treatment for mild-to-moderate OSA in the peer-reviewed sleep-medicine literature, with actual RCT-level AHI reduction data — a materially stronger evidence base than either nasal-dilator subtype above. **Important labeling distinction:** OTC boil-and-bite MADs like [[SnoreRx]] are FDA-cleared (510(k) pathway) specifically for **snoring reduction, not for diagnosed sleep apnea treatment** — the RCT-level OSA evidence in the literature generally involves custom-fitted devices from a dentist/sleep specialist, not necessarily the exact OTC boil-and-bite product. A consumer should not assume an OTC MAD is a substitute for diagnosed-OSA treatment just because the device class has real OSA-relevant literature.

**Internal nasal dilator manufacturer trial data (Rhinomed/Mute):** company-sponsored trial (Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital) reported ~38% more nasal airflow vs. nasal strips; a manufacturer white paper claims 75% of users experienced reduced snoring and 73% of bed partners reported reduced snoring severity. These are manufacturer-reported figures, not independently replicated in the peer-reviewed literature reviewed in this pass — treat as a lower-confidence evidence tier than the Camacho meta-analysis above, though directionally consistent with that meta-analysis's internal-dilator subgroup finding.

**Bottom line:** this category spans a real evidence gradient — MADs > internal nasal dilators > external nasal strips, for objective apnea/breathing-severity metrics specifically. All three device types may still provide subjective snoring/sleep-quality benefit even without a strong AHI effect, which is a legitimate but different claim than "treats sleep apnea."

## Key Safety Data
No serious adverse events identified across the device types reviewed. MADs carry a real, separately-documented risk of dental/skeletal side effects with long-term use (per a cited systematic review/meta-analysis on oral-appliance side effects) — bite changes, tooth movement, jaw discomfort — a genuine long-term consideration not shared by the nasal-based devices. Nasal dilators (external or internal) carry minimal risk beyond local skin irritation (adhesive strips) or minor nasal discomfort (internal dilators).

## Contraindications
MADs: not appropriate for patients with significant dental issues, missing teeth, or TMJ disorders (general oral-appliance caution) — a dental/medical evaluation is advisable before regular use, especially for actual diagnosed OSA rather than simple snoring. Nasal strips/dilators: no major contraindications identified; adhesive strips may irritate sensitive skin.

## Special Population Flags
**Snoring vs. diagnosed OSA:** none of the OTC devices in this category are a substitute for diagnosed obstructive sleep apnea evaluation/treatment (CPAP, custom dental appliance, or other physician-directed therapy) — this applies across all three device mechanisms, most explicitly stated on SnoreRx's own FDA clearance scope.

## Overdose / Toxicity
Not applicable — mechanical devices and topical essential-oil drops, no systemic drug exposure.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Snore Aids (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed, 1,201,261/month volume): Breathe Right 86%, Sleepwell 5%, Air Sleep/Snore 4%, SnoreRx 3%, Mute 2%. Breathe Right's dominant share (86%) is concentrated in the device subtype (external nasal strips) with the weakest objective-AHI evidence per the Camacho meta-analysis above — though, as noted, that same meta-analysis and related trials found real subjective snoring/sleep-quality benefit in non-OSA populations, so this is not a claim the category leader "doesn't work" for its actual marketed purpose (snoring reduction), just that its evidence for the more rigorous AHI/OSA-severity metric is weak.
