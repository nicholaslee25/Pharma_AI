# Oral Phenylephrine

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Sympathomimetic (alpha-1 adrenergic agonist), oral decongestant
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Low/negative at the marketed OTC dose — a 2007 meta-analysis found the standard 10mg dose produced no significant objective decongestant effect, underlying the FDA's 2023 advisory-committee determination that oral phenylephrine is not GRASE (Generally Recognized As Safe and Effective) as a decongestant
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/oral-phenylephrine/statpearls-phenylephrine.md, raw/primary-sources/oral-phenylephrine/dailymed-sudafed-pe.md, raw/primary-sources/oral-phenylephrine/pubmed-hatton-2007-meta-analysis.md

## Indication
OTC label claim: temporary relief of sinus congestion/pressure and nasal congestion from the common cold, hay fever, or other upper respiratory allergies — see Efficacy Evidence below for why this claim is now contested by FDA's own advisory process.

## Mechanism
Direct-acting sympathomimetic amine, alpha-1 adrenergic agonist with minimal beta activity — produces vasoconstriction. Orally, phenylephrine undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism (sulfate conjugation predominantly in the intestinal wall, plus oxidative deamination via MAO-A/MAO-B, plus glucuronidation), which is the proposed mechanistic reason for its poor oral efficacy despite being pharmacologically active when given IV, intranasally, or ophthalmically.

## Dosing (per Sudafed PE FDA OTC label)
Adults/children ≥12y: 10mg (1 tablet) every 4 hours, max 60mg (6 tablets)/24h. Children <12y: ask a doctor.

## Efficacy Evidence — Hatton et al. 2007 meta-analysis (PMID 17264159)
Systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs measuring oral phenylephrine's effect on nasal airway resistance (NAR) vs. placebo:
- **Phenylephrine 10mg** (the standard marketed OTC dose; 8 studies, 138 patients): **did NOT significantly reduce NAR vs. placebo** — mean maximal relative-change difference 10.1% (95% CI −3.8% to 23.9%)
- **Phenylephrine 25mg** (2.5x the standard dose; 8 studies): DID significantly reduce NAR vs. placebo, 27.6% (95% CI 17.5–37.7%)
- Patient-reported (subjective) decongestion was not consistently better than placebo at any tested dose
- No consistent heart rate/BP effect at doses ≤25mg
- **Authors' conclusion:** insufficient evidence oral phenylephrine is effective as a nonprescription decongestant at marketed doses; recommended the FDA require additional studies

**Regulatory consequence:** this evidence base (and subsequent FDA-commissioned studies) led to the FDA's 2023 Nonprescription Drug Advisory Committee unanimously voting that oral phenylephrine, even at 10mg q4h and at higher doses, is not GRASE as a decongestant. **This determination applies specifically to ORAL phenylephrine — intranasal and ophthalmic phenylephrine formulations are unaffected and remain considered effective for their respective indications.** As of this research pass, oral phenylephrine products remain on US store shelves — an advisory committee vote is a recommendation to FDA, not itself an automatic market withdrawal, and this vault does not assert the products have been formally withdrawn.

**Update (2026-08-27, Pediatrics/Children's Cough-Cold Combinations research pass):** the FDA took a concrete regulatory step beyond the 2023 advisory vote — on 2024-11-08 it published a **proposed administrative order (OTC000036, Federal Register 89 FR 217)** to formally remove orally-administered phenylephrine HCl and phenylephrine bitartrate as nasal-decongestant active ingredients from OTC Monograph M012, on GRASE grounds. The public comment period closed 2025-05-07; multiple secondary sources reported a final order could plausibly arrive around May 2026, but **no confirmed final order/rule was located as of this research pass (2026-08-27)** — this remains a proposed, not final, order, and oral-phenylephrine products (including every pediatric combination product cross-referenced below) remain legally marketable in the interim. Flagged as a live, still-unresolved regulatory process, not a settled outcome in either direction.

## Pharmacokinetics
Well absorbed at the tissue level for non-oral routes; orally, extensive gut-wall/hepatic first-pass metabolism (sulfate conjugation, MAO-A/B oxidative deamination, glucuronidation) sharply limits systemic bioavailability — this is the proposed mechanism for its oral ineffectiveness. Volume of distribution 340L (exceeds total body water, reflecting extensive tissue distribution when it does reach systemic circulation via non-oral routes). Half-life ~2.5–3h. Primarily renal excretion, mostly unchanged.

## Contraindications
Per StatPearls: no definitive contraindications besides hypersensitivity to the drug/components (this applies to phenylephrine generally, across routes — IV formulation specifically also contains sodium metabisulfite, a distinct allergen). Per the OTC oral label: do not use with a prescription MAOI or within 2 weeks of stopping one.

## Key Safety Data
- Most common adverse effects (StatPearls, largely reflecting IV/systemic use data): nausea, vomiting, headache, nervousness
- Can cause baroreceptor-mediated reflex bradycardia
- In hypovolemic patients with cardiac dysfunction, may raise afterload more than preload, potentially worsening angina/heart failure/pulmonary hypertension — this is mechanistically-derived caution rather than oral-OTC-dose-specific incident data
- OTC label: ask a doctor before use if heart disease, high blood pressure, thyroid disease, diabetes, or trouble urinating due to enlarged prostate; stop use if nervousness, dizziness, or sleeplessness occur

## Drug Interactions
- **MAOIs:** contraindicated per OTC label (hypertensive crisis risk, consistent with other sympathomimetics)
- **Pressor-effect-increasing drugs** (StatPearls, general phenylephrine pharmacology): propranolol, clonidine, amitriptyline, atomoxetine, corticosteroids, ergot alkaloids
- **Pressor-effect-decreasing drugs:** phenothiazines, alpha-1 antagonists

## Special Population Flags
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** OTC label says ask a health professional before use; StatPearls notes systemic (non-oral) administration may reduce milk production
- **Elderly:** StatPearls notes limited effectiveness data specifically in patients ≥65y and recommends starting at the lower end of any dosing range given hepatic/renal/cardiac impairment and polypharmacy risk (this guidance is drawn from general phenylephrine pharmacology across routes, not oral-decongestant-specific elderly data)
- **Pediatric:** OTC label restricts unsupervised use <12y

## Overdose / Toxicity
Not addressed with oral-decongestant-specific data in the sources reviewed; StatPearls' toxicity/overdose discussion is IV-context-specific (no known antidote for IV phenylephrine; overdose hypertension typically transient given short half-life; managed by discontinuation plus chronotropic agents/vasodilators as needed). Not extrapolated to oral OTC overdose here since the pharmacokinetics differ substantially by route.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
Decongestants, Oral (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed): Sudafed PE (phenylephrine) — 4%, vs. Sudafed (pseudoephedrine) — 45% in the same category (see [[pseudoephedrine]]). This stark gap in pharmacist recommendation share is directionally consistent with the weak efficacy evidence for oral phenylephrine described above, though a recommendation share is not itself efficacy data and pseudoephedrine's behind-the-counter purchase friction (methamphetamine-precursor restrictions) likely also affects real-world brand share independent of efficacy.
