# Coal Tar

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Keratoplastic/cytostatic (antipsoriatic, antiseborrheic)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** FDA has an affirmative OTC monograph position (0.5-5% safe and effective for dandruff/seborrheic dermatitis/psoriasis) that is in real, documented tension with California's Proposition 65 carcinogen-warning requirement for the same concentration range — a genuinely unresolved regulatory split between two government bodies, not a settled "safe" or "unsafe" verdict.
**Last updated:** 2026-08-27
**Sources:** DailyMed (Neutrogena T/Gel, Medicasp, MG217 labels), FDA OTC monograph position (search-summarized), California Prop 65 coal-tar listing (search-summarized, SupplySideSJ/pharmacalway/psorsite sources). Brand-page research 2026-08-27.

## Indication
FDA-monograph OTC: dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, and psoriasis — confirmed via near-identical "Anti-dandruff, Anti-seborrheic dermatitis, Anti-psoriasis" purpose wording across every coal-tar label pulled this pass.

## Mechanism
Slows excessive epidermal cell turnover (keratoplastic/cytostatic effect), reducing the rapid skin-cell proliferation characteristic of psoriasis and the flaking of seborrheic dermatitis/dandruff. Per search-summarized general literature, coal tar is described as "the only non-prescription active ingredient that works by slowing down excessive cell reproduction" — a genuinely different mechanism from the antifungal actions of [[ketoconazole]], [[pyrithione-zinc]], and [[selenium-sulfide]] elsewhere in this category.

## Dosing
FDA-monograph allowable range: **0.5-5%** coal tar. Concentrations confirmed this pass: **0.5%** (Neutrogena T/Gel — labeled with a formulation-specific caveat, see Formulation Note below; also Medicasp, at exactly the Prop 65 warning threshold) and **3%** (MG217 Psoriasis Medicated Conditioning/Therapeutic Scalp Treatment coal tar shampoos). Directions uniform across brands: wet hair, massage into scalp, leave several minutes, rinse, repeat; use at least twice weekly or as directed.

**Formulation note (real labeling nuance):** the Neutrogena T/Gel label pulled this pass lists "Coal tar 0.5%" but also describes this as derived from "2% Neutar® Solubilized Coal Tar Extract" — two different numbers on the same label referring to the raw-extract concentration vs. the coal-tar-equivalent active concentration. This dual-numbering convention could confuse a consumer comparing "2%" marketing language against another brand's plain "3%" or "0.5%" figure; the FDA-relevant active concentration is the 0.5% figure, not the 2% extract figure.

## Efficacy Evidence
No large-scale independent RCT or Cochrane-style review specific to coal-tar shampoo efficacy was located in this pass — a genuine evidence gap. What was located is primarily FDA's own OTC-monograph safety/efficacy determination (that 0.5-5% coal tar is safe and effective for the labeled indications) rather than a specific quantified trial (response rate, RR, etc.) of the kind available for [[benzoyl-peroxide]] or [[salicylic-acid]] elsewhere in this database. This should be read as an evidence-documentation gap in this pass, not as evidence of ineffectiveness — coal tar's monograph status itself reflects an FDA safety/efficacy review, just not one with the same granular trial statistics gathered here for other ingredients.

## Regulatory Status — real, documented FDA-vs-state tension (the central finding for this ingredient)
The **FDA's OTC monograph position** is that coal tar at 0.5-5% is safe and effective for dandruff/seborrheic dermatitis/psoriasis, and that "there is no evidence that topical treatment of dermatological disorders with over-the-counter coal tar shampoo, soap, or ointment drug products increases the risk of skin cancers" (FDA determination, search-summarized).

**California's Proposition 65**, by contrast, lists coal tar as a chemical known to the state to cause cancer, and requires cancer-warning labels on any OTC coal-tar shampoo, lotion, or cream sold in California above 0.5% concentration. Search-summarized risk figures cited in this pass: an estimated 3.5-in-10-million lifetime skin-cancer risk from 1%-coal-tar shampoo use, and 1.7-in-1-million for 2.5%-coal-tar shampoo use — very small absolute risks, but nonzero, and the basis for Prop 65's differing standard from the FDA's "no evidence of increased risk" framing.

**Practical consequence for brands in this category:** Medicasp's confirmed 0.5% concentration sits exactly at the Prop 65 warning threshold, while MG217's confirmed 3% concentration is well above it — meaning MG217 coal-tar products sold in California should carry a Prop 65 cancer warning that Medicasp, at the boundary concentration, may or may not require depending on exact interpretation. This is a real, current regulatory split (federal "safe and effective, no cancer evidence" vs. state "warning required"), not an error on either side to be resolved — both standards are simultaneously in force.

## Contraindications
Known hypersensitivity to coal tar or product components.

## Key Safety Data
- **Photosensitivity**: every coal-tar label checked this pass warns that use may increase sunburn tendency for up to 24 hours after application — a consistent, real, cross-brand finding
- Do not use for prolonged periods without consulting a doctor; do not combine with other psoriasis therapies (UV/phototherapy or prescription drugs) unless directed by a doctor — a real drug-modality interaction caution given coal tar's photosensitizing effect combined with UV-based psoriasis treatments specifically
- Cosmetic effect: temporary discoloration of gray or tinted hair, per the Neutrogena T/Gel label
- See Regulatory Status above for the coal-tar-specific carcinogenicity tension, distinct from the acute-use warnings in this section

## Drug Interactions
UV/phototherapy and other psoriasis treatments — increased photosensitivity risk when combined, per label warnings across brands checked this pass.

## Special Population Flags
No specific pregnancy, breastfeeding, or pediatric restriction was located on any label checked this pass, beyond the general "ask a doctor before use if you have a condition that covers a large area of the body" caution seen on related dandruff-shampoo labels in this category.

## Overdose / Toxicity
Not addressed with specific overdose data in the sources gathered this pass; standard "for external use only" wording applies.
