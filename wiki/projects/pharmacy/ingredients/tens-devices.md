## What This Category Is
TENS units are battery-powered devices that deliver low-voltage electrical current through skin-surface electrodes, marketed for temporary relief of minor muscle/joint pain (back, neck, shoulder, arthritis-related joint pain). They are **devices, not drugs** — no active pharmaceutical ingredient, no systemic absorption, and evaluated on device/clinical-outcome evidence rather than pharmacokinetics.

## Mechanism (proposed, not definitively established)
Two commonly cited proposed mechanisms: (1) the "gate control" theory — electrical stimulation of large-diameter sensory nerve fibers may inhibit pain-signal transmission at the spinal dorsal horn; (2) stimulation-induced endogenous opioid/endorphin release at higher-frequency settings. Neither mechanism is definitively confirmed as the basis for any real-world clinical benefit — see Efficacy Evidence below.

## FDA Regulatory Status
Since 1977, TENS devices have been cleared for marketing via the FDA's 510(k) premarket notification pathway on the basis of substantial equivalence to devices already on the market before May 1976 — **510(k) clearance does not require clinical efficacy data**, only a safety/equivalence showing. Standard OTC TENS units for muscle/joint pain are generally Class II devices. (One specific device, Cefaly, received a distinct de novo FDA clearance in 2014 specifically for migraine prophylaxis — a different indication and regulatory pathway than the general OTC muscle/joint-pain TENS units tracked in this database's Pain and Inflammation category.) This FDA pathway is structurally similar to the Class I/510(k)-exempt status already documented for [[joint-support-braces]] in that neither pathway requires outcome-trial evidence before marketing — a "no RCT for this specific unit" finding is the expected norm for this device category, not a research gap unique to any one brand.

## Efficacy Evidence

**Gibson et al. 2019 Cochrane overview of reviews (CD011890, updated from an earlier version)** — overview of 9 eligible systematic reviews (7 specifically on TENS for pain/function across a variety of chronic pain conditions in adults), comparing TENS to sham TENS, usual care/no treatment, or TENS-plus-active-treatment vs. active treatment alone:
- **Conclusion: it was not possible to confidently state whether TENS is effective for relieving chronic pain**, in any of the three comparison types
- The 9 included reviews were themselves rated high-quality as reviews, but the underlying trial evidence within them was rated **very low certainty**
- No reliable evidence was found that effectiveness varies by delivery parameters (frequency, intensity, electrode placement) — meaning even among proponents of the modality, there's no confirmed "right way" to dose TENS for better outcomes
- This is consistent with the broader pattern already documented for other device categories in this database ([[joint-support-braces]]): low-risk, 510(k)/Class-I-adjacent devices frequently reach market and popularity without confirmatory outcome-trial evidence, since the regulatory pathway does not require it

**Bottom line:** TENS is a widely used, low-risk, patient-controlled pain-management device with a plausible but not confirmed physiological mechanism, and the best available evidence (a 2019 Cochrane overview of 9 reviews) explicitly declines to confirm effectiveness beyond placebo for chronic pain. This does not mean TENS provides no subjective benefit to individual users — sensory distraction, a sense of active self-management, and possible placebo response are all real, commonly reported experiences — but it does mean no brand's outcome claims in this category should be read as backed by confirmed superiority evidence.

## Key Safety Data
TENS units are generally considered low-risk for appropriate use (external electrical stimulation, no systemic drug exposure). Standard contraindications across the device class: not for use over the front/sides of the neck, over the eyes, transcerebrally (across the head), directly on the spine, on broken/irritated skin, or by individuals with a pacemaker/implanted electronic device without physician guidance; not for undiagnosed pain of unknown cause; not for use while driving or operating machinery, or in the bath/shower.
