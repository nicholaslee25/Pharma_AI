# Vitamin C

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Water-soluble vitamin/antioxidant (dietary supplement, not FDA-regulated as a drug)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Low for general cold prevention (a 29-trial Cochrane meta-analysis found essentially no incidence reduction in the general population); moderate for a specific benefit in people under heavy physical/cold stress, and for modest duration/severity reduction with regular (not after-onset) use
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/vitamin-c/nih-ods-vitamin-c-health-professional.md, raw/primary-sources/vitamin-c/cochrane-hemila-2013-common-cold.md

## Indication
Prevention/treatment of vitamin C deficiency (scurvy). Widely marketed for immune support and cold prevention/treatment — evidence for this specific use case is nuanced (see Efficacy Evidence).

## Mechanism
Essential water-soluble nutrient (humans cannot synthesize it endogenously). Required for collagen, L-carnitine, and neurotransmitter biosynthesis; involved in protein metabolism. Important physiological antioxidant that also regenerates vitamin E. Plays a role in immune function and improves absorption of nonheme (plant-based) dietary iron.

## Dosing (NIH ODS RDA and Tolerable Upper Intake Level)

| Population | RDA | Upper Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Adults | 90–120mg | 400–2,000mg, varies by age |
| Infants/children/adolescents | 15–115mg, varies by age | 400–2,000mg, varies by age |
| Smokers | RDA + 35mg/day extra | Same upper limits |

Intestinal absorption is dose-dependent and saturable — higher doses have progressively lower absorption efficiency than moderate doses, with excess excreted in urine.

## Efficacy Evidence — Hemilä & Chalker 2013 Cochrane review (PMID 23440782)
29 trial comparisons, 11,306 participants, regular (preventive) vitamin C supplementation vs. placebo:
- **General population cold incidence:** pooled RR 0.97 (95% CI 0.94–1.00) — **essentially no reduction** in ordinary adults
- **People under heavy physical/cold stress** (marathon runners, skiers, subarctic soldiers; 5 trials, 598 participants): pooled RR **0.48** (95% CI 0.35–0.64) — cold incidence roughly **halved**, a genuinely large and distinct effect in this specific population
- **Duration** (31 comparisons, 9,745 episodes): reduced 8% in adults (95% CI 3–12%), 14% in children (95% CI 7–21%); 1–2g/day in children shortened colds by 18%; severity also reduced with regular use
- **Therapeutic use** (started at symptom onset, 7 comparisons, 3,249 episodes): **no consistent effect** on duration or severity — the regular-supplementation benefit did not replicate for after-onset dosing

**Authors' conclusion:** routine vitamin C supplementation for cold prevention is not justified in the general population, but may help people exposed to brief severe physical/environmental stress; regular (not post-onset) supplementation modestly shortens/softens colds when they do occur. This directly contradicts the common "take vitamin C when a cold starts" marketing pattern — the evidence supports ongoing regular intake, not reactive dosing.

## NIH ODS Additional Health-Outcome Summaries
- **Cancer prevention:** observational associations exist, but most RCTs found no effect of supplementation on cancer risk
- **Cardiovascular disease:** plausible mechanisms, but clinical trials have not confirmed protective benefit
- **AMD/cataracts:** limited trial data do not show prevention benefit from vitamin C alone or with other antioxidants, though people who already have AMD and take antioxidant combinations including vitamin C have a lower risk of progressing to advanced AMD (a treatment-adjacent, not prevention, finding)

## Pharmacokinetics
Intestinal absorption via a dose-dependent active transporter — absorption efficiency declines as dose increases; unmetabolized excess is excreted in urine. Status typically assessed via plasma vitamin C levels.

## Contraindications
Not itemized specifically in the sources reviewed beyond the excessive-intake effects below.

## Key Safety Data
- Generally low toxicity; high intake does not typically cause serious adverse effects
- Can cause diarrhea, nausea, abdominal cramps, other GI disturbance at high doses
- Some concern regarding kidney stones and excess iron absorption at high intakes, though not generally considered a risk in healthy individuals
- **Deficiency (scurvy):** signs can appear within 1 month of consuming <~10mg/day; causes petechiae, ecchymoses, purpura, joint pain, poor wound healing, hyperkeratosis, corkscrew hairs; fatal if untreated (rare in developed countries today)

## Drug Interactions
May interact with radiation therapy and medications including chemotherapeutic agents and statins (per NIH ODS; specific mechanism/direction not detailed in the source reviewed).

## Special Population Flags
- **Smokers/secondhand smoke exposure:** require 35mg/day more than non-smokers, and are a specific at-risk group for inadequacy
- **Infants fed evaporated/boiled milk:** at-risk group for inadequacy
- **Limited dietary variety, malabsorption, chronic disease:** at-risk groups for inadequacy

## Overdose / Toxicity
See Key Safety Data above — Tolerable Upper Intake Level 400–2,000mg depending on age; low overall toxicity even above this level, with GI symptoms being the main concern rather than organ damage in healthy individuals.

## Pharmacy Times Context (recommendation share — NOT efficacy)
High-Potency Vitamin C Supplements (Pharmacy Times 2026 OTC Guide, ~1,700 pharmacists surveyed, 1,434,454 monthly recommendations): Emergen-C 52%, Nature Made 18%, Airborne Vitamin C 10%, Nature's Bounty 8%, Ester-C 6%, Solaray 3%, Sundown 2%, Vitafusion 1%. These are pharmacist recommendation shares, not response/cure rates — and per the Cochrane review above, the dominant real-world use pattern (taking a high-potency vitamin C product at the first sign of a cold) is specifically the therapeutic/after-onset use case that showed no consistent benefit in the trial evidence.
