## What This Page Is
"Joint Support/Braces" spans a wide device range from simple elastic bandages (ACE wraps) to neoprene compression sleeves (Copper Fit, generic knee sleeves) to semi-rigid adjustable braces (Mueller) to custom hinged functional braces (DonJoy's ACL/ligament-instability line). These are **devices, not drugs** — most are FDA Class I (general controls, 510(k)-exempt) under the limb-orthosis regulation (21 CFR 890.3475), which explicitly lists "a whole limb and joint brace, a hand splint, an elastic stocking, a knee cage" as Class I examples. Custom-fit, ligament-specific functional braces marketed with a biomechanical claim (e.g. "reduces ACL strain") sit in a different regulatory and evidence tier than an OTC neoprene sleeve, even though both are colloquially "knee braces." A brace producing no measurable clinical-outcome benefit in a trial does not mean it is useless or mis-marketed — comfort, proprioceptive feedback, warmth, and subjective support are real, commonly reported benefits that simply aren't the same thing as a demonstrated reduction in pain/swelling/recovery time on a validated outcome measure.

## Efficacy Evidence

**Cochrane review, knee bracing for osteoarthritis (Duivenvoorden et al. 2015, PMID 25773267, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD004020.pub3):** evaluated valgus/unloader knee braces and lateral-wedge foot orthoses for medial compartment knee OA. **Conclusion: evidence was inconclusive** for bracing's effect on pain, stiffness, function, or quality of life. Low-quality evidence found no clear difference between valgus knee braces and laterally wedged insoles. Long-term (>12 month) data was especially sparse.

**Systematic review, compression wrapping for acute joint injuries (PMID 32688372):** 8 studies of elastic-bandage compression for acute ankle sprains. **No difference found** vs. no compression on pain, swelling, ankle function, range of motion, or recovery time. All evidence rated low to very low certainty.

**Bottom line:** the two best-available systematic reviews for this device category — one for chronic OA bracing, one for acute-injury elastic wrapping — both land on the same conclusion: no clearly demonstrated clinical-outcome benefit, with evidence quality too low to rule benefit in or out either way. This is consistent across both device sub-types (rigid/semi-rigid brace and elastic wrap), not an artifact of one weak study.

## What's Different About Functional/Custom Ligament Braces
DonJoy-style custom hinged braces (e.g. for post-ACL-reconstruction) are marketed on **biomechanical strain-reduction data** (cadaveric/in-vivo strain-gauge studies showing reduced ACL graft strain under load with certain hinge designs), a different evidence type from the patient-outcome trials above. Manufacturer marketing citing "50% ACL strain reduction" reflects real biomechanics research, but strain reduction in a lab measurement is not the same claim as reduced re-injury rate in real-world use — no independent re-injury-rate RCT for any single hinged-brace design was located in this pass. Treat biomechanical and clinical-outcome evidence as two different, non-interchangeable evidence tiers for this sub-category.

## FDA Classification
- Elastic bandages: 21 CFR 880.5075, **Class I**, 510(k)-exempt.
- Knee braces / limb orthoses generally (including neoprene sleeves, hinged adjustable braces): 21 CFR 890.3475, **Class I**, 510(k)-exempt — this covers the large majority of retail joint-support products in this category, including all five brands tracked below.
- Custom-fabricated functional ligament braces are typically still marketed under this same Class I framework in the US, though with a substantially more elaborate fitting/biomechanical-design process than an off-the-shelf sleeve.

## Special Notes
- "No RCT exists for this specific knee sleeve" is the norm for this category, not a gap in this vault's research — Class I 510(k)-exempt status means manufacturers are not required to generate outcome-trial evidence to market these devices, unlike drugs or Class II/III devices.
- **Discrepancy flagged: [[Copper Fit]]'s copper-infusion marketing claim is not supported by therapeutic evidence found in this pass.** Copper has documented antimicrobial/antifungal properties relevant to hospital-surface infection control, but no clinical trial isolating a copper-specific joint-pain or inflammation benefit (vs. compression alone) was located. Independent reviewers (MedicalNewsToday, HighYa) attribute Copper Fit's real-world benefit to compression, treating the copper content as primarily an odor-control/marketing feature. See [[Copper Fit]] for full detail — flagged on both pages per this project's discrepancy convention.

## Brands Using This Category (in this database)
**ACE** (53%, 3M) — elastic bandages and basic braces, the Class I products the FDA limb-orthosis regulation was written around. **Futuro** (20%, 3M) — compression sleeves/braces, same corporate family and device class as ACE. **Copper Fit** (13%) — neoprene compression sleeves with a copper-infusion marketing claim not supported by therapeutic evidence beyond the compression itself. **Mueller** (9%) — neoprene/adjustable braces with double-buttress/patella-opening design features, same Class I category. **DonJoy** (5%, DJO/Enovis) — the outlier in this list: a custom hinged functional-brace line marketed on biomechanical ACL-strain-reduction data, a materially different product and evidence tier from the other four.
