# Insect Repellents (DEET / Picaridin / Other EPA-Registered Actives)

**Type:** Active Ingredient (class) — NOT an FDA drug ingredient
**Drug Class:** EPA-regulated pesticide/repellent under FIFRA (Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act) — a fundamentally different regulatory pathway from every FDA Drug Facts-labeled ingredient elsewhere in this database
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Good for comparative repellent efficacy (large field/lab study base, 140+ studies reviewed in one comparison) — DEET and picaridin perform comparably at equivalent concentrations; genuinely mixed/contested for DEET pediatric neurotoxicity specifically, where real case reports exist alongside an EPA safety determination that could not confirm causation
**Last updated:** 2026-08-27
**Sources:** WebSearch (2018 comparative science review of 140+ DEET/picaridin field and lab studies per journals.sagepub.com summary; CDC MMWR "Seizures Temporally Associated with Use of DEET Insect Repellent"; PubMed case-analysis review of DEET toxic encephalopathy in children; EPA 1998/2014 DEET re-registration reviews per existing `drugs/Off.md` research)

## Indication
Prevention (not treatment) of insect bites/stings from mosquitoes, ticks, and other biting arthropods — a fundamentally different use case from every FDA-regulated topical drug elsewhere in this database's Insect Bite and Sting Management category, which treat symptoms of a bite that has already occurred.

## Mechanism
DEET (N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide) and picaridin (also known as icaridin) both work by interfering with insects' olfactory detection of human/animal skin odorants (lactic acid, carbon dioxide, other attractants), making treated skin effectively undetectable to host-seeking insects rather than killing or repelling them through irritation. Other EPA-registered actives include IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus/PMD (a plant-derived synthetic analog of the natural oil), 2-undecanone, and various essential oils (citronella, geranium, cedarwood, etc., generally shorter-acting and less rigorously EPA-reviewed than the "conventional" repellent class).

## Efficacy Evidence
**2018 comparative science review** (per journals.sagepub.com summary, covering 140+ field and laboratory studies): **no significant difference in performance between DEET and picaridin at equivalent concentrations** — e.g., picaridin 20% performs comparably to DEET 20%. A Cambodia field study found 97.4% overall 5-hour protection against early- and outdoor-biting malaria/arbovirus mosquito vectors. **EPA registration data on protection duration:** picaridin 20% is effective 8-14 hours against mosquitoes/ticks; picaridin 10% is effective 5-12 hours; DEET 30% is effective roughly 8-10 hours — duration scales with concentration for both actives, and neither is dramatically superior to the other at matched strength. Both are characterized by EPA as "conventional" (longer-lasting, well-studied) repellents, distinct from the shorter-duration essential-oil-based products also sold under some of the same brand names.

## Key Safety Data — a genuinely contested area, held plainly rather than resolved
**Real, documented DEET pediatric neurotoxicity case reports exist:** a case-analysis review identified **17 reports of DEET-associated toxic encephalopathy in children**, with seizures the most prominent symptom (72% of cases), more frequent with skin (vs. spray-then-avoid) application; one documented case involved a mild (17.6%) DEET concentration in an 18-month-old who developed seizures and respiratory distress after brief exposure. CDC's own MMWR published a report specifically titled "Seizures Temporally Associated with Use of DEET Insect Repellent." **However, EPA's own 1998 and 2014 re-registration reviews (the same reviews cited on `drugs/Off.md`) explicitly state EPA "was not able to conclude whether reports of seizures were directly related to DEET exposure"** — and EPA's overall safety determination is that DEET "does not present a health concern" when used as directed, with no age restriction for children. Poison Control Center data separately did not find children more likely than adults to develop adverse effects from DEET exposure. **Both facts are preserved here rather than one overriding the other:** real seizure case reports exist and are documented in peer-reviewed/CDC literature, AND the responsible federal regulator's own most recent safety reviews did not establish DEET caused them and concluded labeled use is not a health concern.

Picaridin does not carry the same documented neurotoxicity case-report history as DEET, though it has a shorter real-world usage history overall (less post-market surveillance data accumulated).

## Contraindications
Do not apply over cuts, wounds, or irritated skin; avoid application near children's eyes, mouth, and hands; use only enough to cover exposed skin/clothing; wash treated skin with soap and water after returning indoors; for sprays, apply to hands first then rub onto face rather than spraying directly on the face (standard EPA label guidance across DEET-containing products).

## Special Population Flags
- **Pregnancy:** two studies of DEET-using pregnant women found no birth abnormalities attributable to DEET exposure — a real, if limited, reassuring data point.
- **Children:** EPA places no age restriction on DEET or picaridin use, but the real seizure case-report history above (skewing toward pediatric cases) is a genuine, documented safety signal worth a caregiver's awareness even though causation was not established by EPA's review.
- **Concentration matters:** higher DEET/picaridin concentrations extend protection duration but do not meaningfully change the underlying mechanism-of-action safety profile per the sources reviewed — concentration should be chosen based on needed protection duration, not assumed to scale risk linearly in either direction based on the data located in this pass.

## Formulation Caveat
This is an ingredient CLASS page covering an entire regulatory category (EPA pesticide/repellent), not a single FDA-monograph active — brands in this space commonly span multiple distinct actives (DEET, picaridin, PMD, essential oils) across their own product lines, and percentage/duration claims vary substantially by specific SKU. See `drugs/Off.md` for a detailed single-brand example of this multi-active-ingredient spread.
