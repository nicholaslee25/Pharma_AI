# Burn Treatment (Minor Thermal Burns, General Protocol)

## Indication
First-degree (superficial, epidermis-only) thermal burns — outpatient-manageable in the large majority of cases per StatPearls.

## The Protocol
1. Cool the burn with cool running water — specifically **20 minutes, started within 3 hours of injury**, per the strongest evidence located (see below)
2. Remove constrictive objects (rings, tight clothing) near the burn site before swelling progresses
3. Clean the area (remove superficial stripping skin layer, per StatPearls) and protect the wound with a non-adhesive dressing
4. Avoid applying ice directly to burned tissue (can worsen tissue injury via vasoconstriction/cold injury)
5. Avoid butter, grease, or other irritating household folk remedies — not supported by evidence and can worsen outcomes or complicate later wound assessment

## Efficacy Evidence — real, quantified data for the cooling step specifically

**Wood et al. 2016 (PMID 26808839, PLoS One)** — prospective bi-national cohort study, Burn Registry of Australia and New Zealand (BRANZ), 2009–2012, propensity-score-adjusted regression:
- Pre-admission cooling was applied in **68%** of patients; only **46%** received the recommended ≥20-minute duration
- **Graft surgery probability fell from 0.537 to 0.467 — a 13% relative reduction (p=0.014)**
- **ICU admission probability fell from 0.175 to 0.091 — a 48% relative reduction (p<0.001)**
- **Hospital length of stay fell from 12.9 to 10.6 days — an 18% relative reduction (p=0.001)**
- All outcomes except death showed a dose-response relationship with cooling duration — more time cooling (up to the guideline amount) tracked with better outcomes, a real quantitative signal, not just a correlation

**Griffin et al. 2022 (PMID 35688782, Australas Emerg Care)** — systematic review/meta-analysis, 323 records screened, 7 studies included (67% from Australia/NZ, quality rated fair-good): 20 minutes of cool running water within 3 hours of injury **significantly decreased the odds of requiring skin grafting and surgical intervention** (pooled odds ratio not stated in the abstract text obtained). Authors note that despite this evidence, guideline-writers still rely partly on expert consensus for the "optimal" duration because high-quality RCT data remains limited, and international guidelines vary in their recommended duration.

**Bottom line:** the cooling step of the protocol — not any specific branded topical product — has the strongest, most quantified evidence base of anything on this page.

## Additional StatPearls Management Notes (First-Degree Burn)
Pain relief with oral analgesics plus topical agents (StatPearls names Silvadene, 3% bismuth tribromophenate, or petroleum gauze as examples — not an endorsement of any single OTC brand). Clean to remove the superficial stripping skin layer. Non-adhesive dressings, especially for children in flexure regions, changed three times weekly absent infection. If infection develops: daily wound exam/dressing changes, with a 7-day course of flucloxacillin as first-line antibiotic therapy (erythromycin as an alternative) — this is prescription-level care, outside OTC scope, included here for completeness.

## Key Safety Data
- Complications of first-degree burns are typically limited to pain and hypothermia risk (especially in children and the elderly, due to higher body-surface-area-to-mass ratio); once pain resolves, first-degree burns rarely have adverse sequelae (StatPearls).
- **Special population flag (StatPearls):** clinicians are advised to suspect non-accidental trauma (abuse) in children presenting with burns on the hands, feet, buttocks, or perineum — a real clinical red-flag pattern, not a general statement about burn care.
- Ice applied directly to burned tissue is specifically discouraged — can worsen tissue injury.
- Butter, grease, and other household substances are explicitly discouraged despite being persistent folk remedies.

## Special Population Flags
- **Pediatric:** higher hypothermia risk during burn first aid/wound care due to body-surface-area-to-mass ratio; non-accidental trauma should be considered for burns in the specific body locations noted above.
- **Elderly:** also at elevated hypothermia risk during burn care per StatPearls.

## Formulation Caveat
Topical burn products (ointments, gels, sprays) remain secondary to the cooling/protection protocol above. No source gathered in this pass identifies a specific OTC topical product with demonstrated superiority over the basic protocol — the quantified outcome data above is specifically for the cooling first-aid step itself, not for any branded product.
