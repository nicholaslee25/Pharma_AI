# Lactase

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Digestive enzyme (dietary supplement, not an FDA-regulated drug)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** Moderate but genuinely uncertain — a rigorous 2010 AHRQ-sponsored systematic review found evidence for lactase/lactose-reduced products insufficient to draw strong efficacy conclusions, while also finding most "lactose intolerant" people can tolerate 12–15g lactose without any product at all
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/lactase/statpearls-lactose-intolerance.md, raw/primary-sources/lactase/pubmed-shaukat-2010-systematic-review.md

## Indication
Symptomatic management of lactose intolerance (bloating, diarrhea, abdominal pain, flatulence, nausea after lactose ingestion) in people with primary, secondary, developmental, or congenital lactase deficiency. Not an FDA-approved drug — marketed as a dietary supplement/food enzyme.

## Mechanism
Lactase enzyme (extracted from yeasts/molds in commercial supplements) hydrolyzes dietary lactose into glucose and galactose before or during digestion, mimicking the endogenous brush-border enzyme that is deficient in lactose-intolerant individuals — preventing undigested lactose from reaching the colon, where bacterial fermentation would otherwise cause the classic osmotic-diarrhea and gas-production symptoms.

## Dosing
StatPearls gives administration timing rather than a specific numeric dose: tablets/capsules taken just before eating a high-lactose food, or with the first bite; liquid drops added to milk should be shaken/stirred and refrigerated 24 hours before drinking to allow lactose breakdown in advance. No standardized unit dose is given in the sources reviewed — commercial products (e.g. Lactaid) set their own per-tablet enzyme unit counts, not independently verified here.

## Efficacy Evidence — Shaukat et al. 2010 AHRQ-sponsored systematic review (PMID 20404262)
36 RCTs (26 on lactase/lactose-hydrolyzed milk supplements or lactose-reduced milk or tolerable lactose doses; 7 on probiotics; 2 on colonic adaptation; 1 other agent):
- **Moderate-quality evidence:** 12–15g of lactose (about 1 cup of milk) is well tolerated by MOST adults — meaning a substantial share of people who identify as lactose intolerant can handle a real amount of lactose without any product
- **Evidence was INSUFFICIENT** that lactose-reduced products (0–2g lactose) outperform higher-lactose (>12g) products for symptom reduction
- **Evidence for probiotics, colonic adaptation, and other agents was also insufficient**
- Authors' conclusion: additional studies are needed to determine the effectiveness of lactose-intolerance treatments generally

**Practical implication for this page:** this is a genuinely humbling finding for a widely-used supplement category — the rigorous evidence base does not clearly establish that lactase supplementation (or lactose-reduced products) outperforms simply consuming a modest amount of regular dairy, though this does not mean lactase supplements don't work for any individual, only that the trial evidence as of 2010 was insufficient to make a strong general claim either way.

## Pharmacokinetics
Not covered with systemic PK data in the sources reviewed — lactase acts locally/exogenously in the gut lumen or pre-digested in milk, not systemically absorbed in a pharmacologically relevant way.

## Contraindications
Not itemized in the sources reviewed — flagged as a gap. Congenital lactase deficiency and other genetic galactose-handling disorders are the underlying conditions this ingredient addresses, not contraindications to it.

No safety signal was identified in this pass; flagged as a gap rather than assumed benign without a source.

## Drug Interactions

## Special Population Flags
- **Ethnic variation:** lactose intolerance prevalence varies hugely by ancestry (50–100% in African American, Hispanic/Latino, and Asian populations; nearly complete in American Indians; 2–15% in Northern Europeans/North Americans) — relevant to who is likely to seek out and benefit from this supplement
- **Secondary lactase deficiency:** from GI infections, celiac disease, Crohn disease, chemotherapy, antibiotics, small intestinal surgery, or radiation — treating the underlying cause may resolve the intolerance without needing ongoing lactase supplementation
- **Bone health:** untreated lactose malabsorption (if it leads to dairy avoidance) may contribute to osteopenia — calcium/vitamin D supplementation is recommended alongside dietary management, independent of whether lactase supplements are used

## Overdose / Toxicity
Not applicable/not addressed in the sources reviewed — a digestive enzyme supplement with no identified toxicity mechanism in this pass.
