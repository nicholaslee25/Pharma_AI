# Acetaminophen

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Analgesic/antipyretic (central-acting; mechanism distinct from peripheral NSAIDs — see Mechanism)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** High for general analgesia/fever reduction (well-established FDA indication); low/insufficient specifically for common-cold symptom relief per the one Cochrane review located
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25

## Indication
FDA-approved: mild-to-moderate pain (monotherapy); severe pain (combined with opioids); fever reduction. OTC (Tylenol label): common cold, headache, backache, minor arthritis pain, toothache, muscular aches, menstrual cramps, fever.

## Mechanism
Precise mechanism unclear — unlike NSAIDs, acetaminophen may inhibit the COX pathway centrally (CNS) but not peripherally, and does not bind directly to COX-1/COX-2 active sites. Possible COX-3 (a COX-1 splice variant) inhibition, unverified in humans. Analgesia may partly involve descending serotonergic pathway stimulation; a metabolite (AM404) may activate cannabinoid systems and modulate glutamatergic transmission via spinal TRPV1 receptors. This distinct, non-NSAID mechanism is why acetaminophen lacks meaningful peripheral anti-inflammatory effect and the GI/antiplatelet risks of NSAIDs, but carries its own distinct (hepatic) risk profile instead.

## Dosing

| Population | Dose | Max |
|---|---|---|
| Adults/adolescents ≥13y, ≥50kg | 1000mg q6h or 650mg q4h (max single dose 1000mg) | 4000 mg/day |
| Adults/adolescents <50kg | 12.5 mg/kg q4h or 15 mg/kg q6h | 75 mg/kg/day (≤3750mg) |
| Pediatric 2–12y | 12.5 mg/kg q4h or 15 mg/kg q6h | 75 mg/kg/day |
| Neonates (≥32wk GA or <28 days old) | 12.5 mg/kg q6h | 50 mg/kg/day |
| Infants 29 days–2y | 15 mg/kg q6h | 60 mg/kg/day |
| Elderly (AGS) | 325–500mg q4h or 500–1000mg q6h | 4g/day typical; reduce 50–75% with hepatic impairment/alcohol misuse history |

OTC label (Tylenol Extra Strength, 500mg): adults/children ≥12y, 2 caplets q6h, max 6 caplets/24h without doctor approval, not beyond 10 days without medical direction. **Do not use with any other acetaminophen-containing product** — combination-product overlap is a leading real-world cause of unintentional overdose.

## Efficacy Evidence — Cochrane, acetaminophen for common cold in adults (Li et al. 2013, PMID 23818046)
4 RCTs, 758 participants (data not pooled due to heterogeneity; 3 of 4 were short 4–6h trials):
- Nasal obstruction: significantly improved with acetaminophen in 2 of 4 studies
- Rhinorrhea: one study found acetaminophen superior to placebo for severity, but **not** for sneezing/coughing
- Sore throat, malaise: **not** improved in 2 of 4 studies
- Headache/achiness: improved more with acetaminophen in 2 studies; no difference in 1 study
- **No included study reported on duration of cold symptoms**
- Minor side effects (GI events, dizziness, dry mouth, somnolence, sweating) in 2 of 4 studies
- **Authors' conclusion: evidence is insufficient to inform practice** for this specific indication — 2 of 4 studies were small, allocation concealment unclear in all four

This is a deliberately honest inclusion: acetaminophen's general analgesic/antipyretic efficacy is well-established via its FDA approval and decades of clinical use, but the specific "relieves cold symptoms" claim common on OTC combination-product packaging is not strongly supported by the one dedicated Cochrane review located in this pass.

## Pharmacokinetics
Oral: peak plasma 30–60min. Low protein binding (10–25%). Hepatic metabolism (glucuronide conjugation primary, sulfate conjugation, CYP2E1 oxidation) produces toxic intermediate NAPQI, normally neutralized by glutathione at therapeutic doses. >90% eliminated within 24h; <5% excreted unchanged.

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity to acetaminophen; severe hepatic impairment or severe active hepatic disease.

## Key Safety Data
- **Toxicity epidemiology:** ~500 US deaths/year and ~50,000 ED admissions/year linked to acetaminophen; >80,000 US poison control cases in 2021; most prevalent drug-related cause of acute liver failure in the US
- **Toxicity threshold:** rare below 200 mg/kg (child) or 150 mg/kg (adult)
- **Common adverse effects:** skin rash, hypersensitivity reactions, nephrotoxicity (elevated BUN/creatinine), hematologic abnormalities (anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, pancytopenia), electrolyte/metabolic imbalances
- **Rare but serious:** anaphylaxis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis, drug-induced pneumonitis
- **Blood pressure:** PATH-BP trial (cited in StatPearls) found 4g/day acetaminophen raised daytime systolic BP by 5 mmHg in a hypertensive/elderly-relevant population — potential cardiovascular risk implication at high chronic dose
- **Regulatory response:** 2011 FDA mandate limiting acetaminophen to 325mg/tablet in opioid-combination products was associated with a significant decrease in hospitalizations and acute-liver-failure cases from acetaminophen/opioid toxicity

## Drug Interactions
- **Warfarin:** prolonged use at 4000mg/day associated with elevated INR — increase INR monitoring frequency
- **Chronic alcohol misuse:** significantly raises toxicity risk (induces CYP2E1, depletes glutathione, impairs NAPQI detoxification, decreases glucuronidation, disrupts hepatocyte membranes, reduces biliary excretion)

## Special Population Flags
- **Pregnancy — significant regulatory update, 2026-08-27 pass:** ACOG's long-standing position considers acetaminophen safe in pregnancy and notes it remains the only OTC antipyretic/analgesic approved for pregnancy use, since NSAIDs/aspirin carry well-documented fetal risks (see [[ibuprofen]], [[aspirin]]). Observational studies (e.g. Alemany et al., 73,881 mother-child pairs) have associated *prenatal* exposure with 19% higher ASD risk and 21% higher ADHD risk; *postnatal* exposure showed no such association. **On 2025-09-22, the FDA announced it had initiated the process for an acetaminophen label change** for pregnancy use, citing additional large studies (Nurses' Health Study II, Boston Birth Cohort) showing an association with later ASD/ADHD diagnosis, and sent a physician notice — but the FDA's own announcement explicitly states **"a causal relationship has not been established"** and acknowledges **"contrary studies in the scientific literature."** Medical societies (including ACOG) have pushed back, maintaining that high-quality evidence does not support a causal link, and Tylenol's manufacturer (Kenvue) has publicly urged the FDA to reject the label change. Separately, a federal multidistrict litigation (MDL No. 3043, S.D.N.Y.) consolidating Tylenol-autism/ADHD injury claims was dismissed in August 2024 but **revived on 2026-07-13 when the U.S. Second Circuit vacated that dismissal**, reinstating roughly 550 cases on the grounds that plaintiffs' scientific experts were wrongly excluded. **Bottom line: this is a live, genuinely contested regulatory and scientific question, not a settled causal finding in either direction** — the honest framing is "association reported in some large observational studies; FDA-initiated label-change process; causation explicitly not established per FDA's own language; disputed by major medical societies and the manufacturer."
- **Breastfeeding:** considered suitable; breast milk levels significantly lower than typical infant doses
- **Hepatic impairment:** severe/active disease contraindicated; mild impairment — reduce dose, monitor LFTs; may be relatively safer than NSAIDs in alcoholic liver disease specifically
- **Renal impairment:** severe (CrCl ≤30 mL/min) — extend intervals, reduce total daily dose
- **Elderly:** dosing per table above; caution with renal/hepatic disease, G6PD deficiency, severe hypovolemia

## Overdose / Toxicity
Mechanism: therapeutic doses are 60–90% cleared via conjugation; overdose depletes Phase II pathways, shifts metabolism toward CYP2E1, increases toxic NAPQI formation (normally only 5–15% of dose), depletes glutathione, and NAPQI attacks hepatocyte macromolecules → centrilobular (zone 3) liver injury, potential nephrotoxicity. Clinical course: early (0–24h) often asymptomatic; delayed (24–72h+) nausea/vomiting/jaundice/abdominal pain/hypotension, potentially progressing to cerebral edema. Monitoring: plot serum level on Rumack-Matthew nomogram if <8h post-ingestion; assess transaminases if non-acute. **Antidote: N-acetylcysteine (NAC)** — restores glutathione to neutralize NAPQI; 20-hour IV or 72-hour oral protocol, minimum 300 mg/kg over initial 20–24h. Massive toxicity (>900 μg/mL with acidosis/altered consciousness): hemodialysis + NAC.
