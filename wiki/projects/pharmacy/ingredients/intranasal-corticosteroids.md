# Intranasal Corticosteroids

**Type:** Active Ingredient (class)
**Drug Class:** Anti-inflammatory nasal glucocorticoids (examples: fluticasone propionate, fluticasone furoate, triamcinolone acetonide, mometasone furoate, budesonide, beclomethasone)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician
**Evidence Grade:** High — recommended first-line for allergic rhinitis, with a large (151-study) meta-analysis confirming most agents outperform placebo; fluticasone propionate specifically is among the top-performing single agents for nasal symptoms
**Last updated:** 2026-08-25

## Indication
First-line treatment for allergic rhinitis (seasonal/perennial): nasal congestion, itchy nose, runny nose, itchy/watery eyes, sneezing.

## Mechanism
Topical anti-inflammatory glucocorticoid action at the nasal mucosa, reducing local inflammatory cell activity and mucosal pathology. Per StatPearls, intranasal corticosteroids are superior to antihistamines specifically at reducing nasal inflammation and improving mucosal pathology, and are more effective than antihistamines at relieving sneezing, rhinorrhea, and nasal pruritus/blockage.

## Dosing (per Flonase FDA OTC label)

| Population | Dose |
|---|---|
| Adults/children ≥12y, week 1 | 2 sprays/nostril once daily |
| Adults/children ≥12y, weeks 2 through 6 months | 1–2 sprays/nostril once daily as needed |
| Adults/children ≥12y, beyond 6 months | Ask a doctor |
| Children 4–11y | 1 spray/nostril once daily, adult-supervised; growth monitoring recommended if used >2 months/year |
| Children <4y | Do not use |

**Important onset note (StatPearls):** these medications should be used regularly, since peak effect may take multiple days to develop — this is not a same-dose, same-hour rescue product like an oral decongestant or antihistamine. The Flonase label itself notes relief may begin day 1 but full effect takes several days.

## Efficacy Evidence — Sousa-Pinto et al. 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis (PMID 38685482)
151 primary studies (mostly seasonal allergic rhinitis; most at unclear/high risk of bias), intranasal corticosteroids and antihistamines vs. placebo:
- Most assessed treatments outperformed placebo in both perennial and seasonal AR
- **In seasonal AR: azelastine-fluticasone (combination), fluticasone furoate, and fluticasone propionate** had the highest probability of moderate-to-large improvement in Total Nasal Symptom Score and Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality-of-Life Questionnaire
- Azelastine-fluticasone (combination antihistamine+steroid) had the highest probability of moderate-large Total Ocular Symptom Score improvement — i.e., outperforming fluticasone propionate alone for the eye-symptom domain specifically
- **Evidence certainty:** high in only 6/46 analyses, moderate in 23/46, low/very low in 17/46 — meaningful uncertainty remains for many specific agent/outcome combinations
- **Authors' conclusion:** most intranasal medications are effective for symptoms/QoL, but evidence certainty varies meaningfully by specific agent and outcome

## Pharmacokinetics
Not covered with agent-specific systemic PK data in the sources reviewed — these are topical nasal-mucosa products with generally low systemic absorption at labeled OTC doses, though see Key Safety Data below for the pediatric growth-rate caution, which implies some systemic glucocorticoid exposure is possible.

## Contraindications
Do not use in children <4y; do not use to treat asthma (per Flonase label — this product class is not a substitute for asthma controller therapy); do not use with an unhealed nasal injury or recent nasal surgery; do not use if allergic to the product/ingredients.

## Key Safety Data
- Most common adverse effects (StatPearls): nasal irritation, followed by epistaxis (nosebleed) — largely preventable with correct technique (spray away from the nasal septum)
- Flonase label: child growth rate may slow with pediatric use — growth monitoring specifically recommended if used beyond 2 months per year in children 4–11y
- Ask a doctor before use if glaucoma or cataract history (ocular glucocorticoid-related caution)
- Stop use and see a doctor for: exposure to chickenpox/measles/tuberculosis (immunosuppressive-adjacent caution for a topical steroid), symptoms not improving within 7 days, a constant nasal whistling sound, allergic reaction, vision changes, or severe/frequent nosebleeds

## Drug Interactions
Per Flonase label: ask a doctor/pharmacist before use if taking HIV medicines (e.g. ritonavir), other steroid medicines for asthma/allergies/skin rash, or ketoconazole pills for fungal infection — these are all agents that can raise systemic corticosteroid exposure via CYP3A4 interaction or additive steroid effect (mechanism not further detailed on the OTC label itself).

## Special Population Flags
- **Pediatric:** not for <4y; growth-rate monitoring specifically recommended for children 4–11y using the product beyond 2 months/year
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** ask a health professional before use
- **Ocular history (glaucoma/cataracts):** ask a doctor before use
- **Immunocompromised-adjacent exposure:** seek medical advice if exposed to chickenpox, measles, or tuberculosis while using this product class

## Overdose / Toxicity
Not addressed with product-specific data in the sources reviewed in this pass — topical nasal glucocorticoid, low acute-toxicity product class; flagged as a gap rather than filled with an estimate.
