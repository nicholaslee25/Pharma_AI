# Iron

**Type:** Active Ingredient
**Drug Class:** Mineral supplement (dietary supplement, not FDA-regulated as a drug)
**Verification Status:** AI-compiled from primary sources, unverified — not yet reviewed by a pharmacist/physician

**Last updated:** 2026-08-25
**Sources:** raw/primary-sources/iron/nih-ods-iron-health-professional.md

## Indication
Prevention/treatment of iron deficiency and iron deficiency anemia (IDA) — particularly in pregnancy, infants/young children, women with heavy menstrual bleeding, and frequent blood donors. Also relevant (controversially) in anemia of chronic disease.

## Mechanism
Essential component of hemoglobin (oxygen transport, lungs to tissues) and myoglobin (muscle oxygen supply). Needed for physical growth, neurological development, cellular function, and synthesis of some hormones. Dietary iron exists as heme (meat/seafood/poultry, higher bioavailability) and nonheme (plants, fortified foods, lower bioavailability, affected by dietary enhancers like vitamin C and inhibitors like phytate). Hepcidin, a circulating peptide hormone, is the master regulator of iron absorption and body distribution.

## Dosing (NIH ODS RDA and Tolerable Upper Intake Level)

| Population | RDA | Upper Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Adults | 8–27mg, varies by sex/life stage | 45mg |
| Infants/children/adolescents | 0.27–27mg, varies by age | 40–45mg, varies by age |

**Vegetarians/vegans need MORE than the standard RDA** due to nonheme iron's lower bioavailability. Experts recommend taking calcium and iron supplements at **different times of day** to avoid the two minerals interfering with each other's absorption.

## Efficacy Evidence (per NIH ODS, by population)

**Pregnancy:** iron supplementation reduces IDA risk in pregnant women and certain adverse effects in their infants. However, **not all professional/public health bodies agree the evidence justifies routine screening and supplementation in asymptomatic pregnant women** — a real, stated disagreement between guideline-issuing organizations, not a settled recommendation.

**Infants/toddlers:** IDA can cause potentially irreversible adverse cognitive/psychological effects in this population. Supplementation or food fortification reduces deficiency risk, but **guidelines vary across professional societies** on specifics for this age group.

**Anemia of chronic disease:** usually managed by treating the underlying inflammatory/infectious/neoplastic disease rather than iron itself; iron supplementation or erythropoiesis-stimulating agents are used in some cases, but **using iron supplementation for this specific condition remains controversial given infection and cardiovascular event risk** — a genuinely disputed use case, not a straightforward "iron helps anemia" claim.

## Pharmacokinetics
Absorption regulated by hepcidin; heme iron absorbs better than nonheme. Iron losses are normally small (urine, feces, sweat, shed skin), but substantially greater in menstruating women — directly relevant to why this population has a higher RDA and higher deficiency risk.

## Contraindications
Not itemized specifically in the sources reviewed, though hereditary hemochromatosis (a genetic iron-overload condition) is a clear reason to avoid supplementation absent a diagnosed deficiency.

## Key Safety Data
- **Overload risk from food alone is low** in adults with normal intestinal function
- **High-dose supplements cause GI effects**; extremely high doses can cause corrosive intestinal necrosis, multisystem organ failure, and death — iron overdose (especially pediatric accidental ingestion of adult-strength iron tablets) is a recognized serious poisoning risk, more so than most vitamin/mineral supplements
- **Hereditary hemochromatosis** patients are at risk of iron overload even without supplementing

## Drug Interactions
May interact with: **levodopa, levothyroxine**. **Proton pump inhibitors can reduce iron absorption** — relevant given how commonly PPIs are used long-term (see [[proton-pump-inhibitors]]).

## Special Population Flags
- **Pregnancy:** at-risk group for inadequacy; supplementation reduces IDA and some infant adverse effects, though universal screening/supplementation for asymptomatic pregnant women is not universally endorsed across guideline bodies
- **Infants/young children:** at-risk group; IDA here carries potentially irreversible cognitive/psychological consequences
- **Women with heavy menstrual bleeding, frequent blood donors:** at-risk groups for inadequacy due to blood loss
- **Vegetarians/vegans:** need higher intake given nonheme iron's lower bioavailability
- **Cancer, GI disorders, heart failure:** associated with inadequate iron intake/status

## Overdose / Toxicity
Tolerable Upper Intake Level 45mg (adults) or 40–45mg (children/adolescents, by age). High-dose supplement overdose is a genuine medical emergency risk (notably in accidental pediatric ingestion) — can progress to corrosive intestinal necrosis, multisystem organ failure, and death at extreme doses. This is a meaningfully sharper toxicity profile than most other vitamin/mineral supplements covered in this database.
