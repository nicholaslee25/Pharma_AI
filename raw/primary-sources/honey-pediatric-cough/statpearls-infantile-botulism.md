## Mechanism
*Clostridium botulinum* spores colonize an infant's GI tract; the bacterium produces botulinum toxin, which inhibits acetylcholine release at neuromuscular junctions, causing flaccid paralysis. Even minimal toxin exposure (as low as 1 mcg/kg) can be lethal.

## Epidemiology
Represents 70% of all new botulism cases annually in the U.S.; incidence 1.9 per 100,000 live births (~77 new cases/year). Equal sex distribution.

## Honey/Spore Connection
Honey exposure accounts for roughly 20% of documented cases (most cases lack an identified source). Environmental exposure (dust, soil, construction sites) is another significant pathway.

## Age-Related Risk (the core safety fact for this ingredient page)
**Infantile botulism occurs exclusively in children younger than 12 months.** Older children have sufficient gastric acidity to prevent C. botulinum spore germination, making them resistant to infection via this route — this is the direct pharmacologic/physiologic basis for the "no honey under 12 months" rule, not an arbitrary cutoff.

## Clinical Symptoms
Initial: constipation, poor feeding, weak crying, lethargy. Progression: generalized hypotonia ("floppy" infant). Advanced: descending paralysis, ptosis, drooling, respiratory compromise.

## Diagnosis
Stool culture + direct toxin assay (toxin assay results in 24h; stool culture takes 1-4 weeks). Most reliable test: mouse inoculation test, performed by the CDC.

## Treatment
Supportive care with airway management; ~50% of cases require intubation. Human BIG-IV (botulism immune globulin) reduces hospital stay and mechanical ventilation duration when given IV over 30 minutes. Antibiotic therapy is CONTRAINDICATED; aminoglycosides must specifically be avoided.

## Outcomes
Most infants recover fully within several months to a year. Modern mortality <15%, vs. ~90% a century ago.
