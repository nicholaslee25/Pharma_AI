# PubMed — Buchanan/Eaglstein et al. 2002, Liquid Adhesive Bandage RCT

**PMID:** 11896780
**Journal:** Dermatologic Surgery, 2002
**Retrieved via:** WebSearch secondary summary (full-text/abstract paywalled), 2026-08-26

## Study Design
Randomized controlled trial. 162 volunteers with recent minor cuts or abrasions, randomized to: (1) liquid adhesive bandage (LAB, octyl-2-cyanoacrylate-based) or (2) control — standard Band-Aid brand adhesive bandage, sheer, 2.5cm.

## Endpoints & Results
- **Primary endpoint — complete healing at day 12:** NO statistically significant difference between LAB and standard bandage groups.
- **Secondary endpoints:** ability of patients to properly apply LAB; ability of LAB to stop bleeding (hemostasis); ability of LAB to reduce pain; ability of LAB to remain on the wound.
- **Only the LAB group showed significant hemostasis and pain relief** — a real, specific advantage over the standard adhesive bandage on these secondary measures, even though overall healing rates were equivalent.

## Authors' Conclusion
The liquid adhesive bandage was as effective as a standard adhesive bandage for promoting healing (day-12 complete healing), with the added secondary benefits of stopping bleeding and reducing pain that the standard bandage did not provide.

## Background (general cyanoacrylate liquid bandage mechanism)
Octyl-2-cyanoacrylate is FDA-approved for closure of incisions/lacerations; a more flexible formulation is used in liquid-bandage products as an occlusive protective film over minor cuts/scrapes. Designed to adhere and dry within 1-2 minutes of application, sloughing off naturally within 1-3 days as underlying skin heals.

## Adjacent safety literature — cyanoacrylate/eye exposure (general class, not liquid-bandage-specific)
"A sticky sight: cyanoacrylate 'superglue' injuries of the eye" (PMC3027638) documents accidental ocular cyanoacrylate instillation, historically most associated with superglue repackaged in dropper-style bottles mistaken for eye drops — three patient groups: visually impaired individuals, careless adults, and children playing with glue bottles. Clinical presentation: eyelid margins adhered shut, corneal abrasion on removal. This literature concerns cyanoacrylate adhesives generally (including non-medical superglue), not FDA-regulated liquid-bandage brush-applicator products specifically — flagged as adjacent-class context, not a documented liquid-bandage-brand-specific incident.
