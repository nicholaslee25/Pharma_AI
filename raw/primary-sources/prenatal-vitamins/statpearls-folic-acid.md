## Mechanism of Action
Folic acid (synthetic folate) is converted to tetrahydrofolate (THF) via dihydrofolate reductase, then to 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate — used for DNA synthesis (deoxythymidine monophosphate formation) and methionine synthesis (homocysteine reduction, requiring vitamin B12 as a cofactor via methionine synthase).

## Indications
**FDA-approved:** neural tube defect prevention in pregnancy; macrocytic megaloblastic anemia from folate deficiency.
**Other cited uses (not FDA-approved for these):** protection against neoplasia in ulcerative colitis, cervical dysplasia prevention, vitiligo treatment.
**Off-label:** combined B12+folate supplementation showed cognitive improvements superior to either alone in some research.

## Dosing
- Standard adult: 400 mcg/day
- WHO neural tube defect prevention: 400–800 mcg/day
- Macrocytic anemia: 1–5mg once daily orally; IV doses ≤5mg
- Prenatal vitamins typically contain **1mg of folate**
- **Pregnancy timing note:** a pregnant woman taking 4mg/day folic acid may require **20 weeks** to reach optimal folate levels — supplementation should begin **5–6 months before conception** for maximum protective effect
- Pediatric: 1–11 months, 30–45mcg/day (starting at 15mcg/kg/dose); 1–10y and ≥11y, 0.1–4mg orally daily, max 5mg/day
- Hemodialysis patients: 1–5mg/day
- Renal/hepatic impairment: dose adjustments undefined in available data

## Pharmacokinetics
Concentrated mainly in the liver, with small kidney reserves. Water-soluble — NOT stored in the body long-term (relevant to why ongoing daily intake matters more than for fat-soluble vitamins).

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity to folic acid/formulation is a potential contraindication, though the article notes hypersensitivity reactions are not definitively established in the literature.

## Adverse Effects/Safety
Daily intake below the 1,000mcg Tolerable Upper Intake Level has not been shown to cause adverse health outcomes; the National Toxicology Program concluded no definitive evidence of adverse effects at that level. Rare GI upset reported. One case report described fatal poisoning, though authors themselves noted it may be a unique/atypical case.

**Critical clinical concern:** folic acid may improve the anemia of B12 deficiency while MASKING/worsening the neurocognitive effects of that same B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration) — B12 status should be assessed before treating with folic acid alone.

## Neural Tube Defect Prevention Evidence
Period of greatest vulnerability: the 4th week of development — often before a woman knows she's pregnant, which is the core rationale for pre-conception (not just post-confirmation) supplementation. Proposed mechanisms: effects on neural-tube-closure gene ubiquitination, and folate's homocysteine-lowering effect. Adequate folic acid intake is also associated with decreased preterm birth risk.

## Special Population Considerations
**Antiepileptic drug users:** folate deficiency detected in up to 16% of patients on antiepileptic drugs (gabapentin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, primidone).
**Methotrexate therapy:** patients should be prescribed folic acid supplements to reduce methotrexate's adverse effects.

## Care Coordination
Clinicians should verify B12 levels before folic acid administration to avoid masking subacute combined degeneration; multidisciplinary coordination (clinicians, APCs, nurses, pharmacists, dietitians) is emphasized for optimal outcomes.
