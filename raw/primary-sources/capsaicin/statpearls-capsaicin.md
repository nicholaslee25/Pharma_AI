## Mechanism of Action
Agonist at vanilloid receptor subtype 1 (TRPV1), an ion channel responsive to temperature, pH, and physical stress. Activation floods calcium into nerve cells, causing mitochondrial dysfunction and rendering nociceptive fibers non-functional. Initially depletes substance P (a key pain mediator) via neurogenic inflammation on first application; with repeated application, the neuron becomes depleted of substance P — this is why capsaicin often causes an initial burning sensation followed by longer-term reduced pain sensitivity. An alternative/additional mechanism involves calcium-induced cell death of TRPV1-bearing neurons, potentially offering more prolonged analgesia.

## Indications (per systematic reviews cited)
Neuropathic pain (nondiabetic and postherpetic neuralgia), osteoarthritis, chronic musculoskeletal pain, post-mastectomy pain syndrome, burning mouth syndrome, overactive bladder, pruritus/pruritus ani, postoperative sore throat, chemotherapy/radiotherapy-induced mucositis. Note: evidence for weight-loss or blood-sugar-reduction claims (sometimes marketed) is limited/not well supported.

## Administration Forms
Low-concentration creams/lotions/patches (OTC range), intradermal injections, oral formulations, and a prescription-only **8% patch** (a much higher, medically-supervised-application concentration distinct from OTC creams). Specific OTC concentration thresholds are not detailed in this StatPearls article — see the DailyMed label for the actual marketed OTC concentration.

## Adverse Effects
**Topical:** local erythema, local pain, local pruritus (all common and often expected/transient). **Systemic (less common):** nausea, hypertension. **Rare:** neurological symptoms, throat irritation.

## Contraindications/Toxicity
No absolute contraindications identified, though patients with asthma show heightened sensitivity. Animal LD50 data: >9 mg/kg (subcutaneous), with respiratory paralysis as the lethal mechanism at toxic doses — no human overdose cases documented. Acute high-dose/accidental exposure (e.g., pepper spray context) causes burning pain, eye damage (photophobia, blurry vision), and respiratory symptoms (dry coughing spells, wheezing).
