## Mechanism of Action
Competitively inhibits COX-1 and COX-2, slightly more selective for COX-1, blocking arachidonate binding and conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandin G. Analgesic/anti-inflammatory via reduced prostaglandin E/F production (these sensitize pain receptors).

## FDA-Approved Indications
Acute gout, ankylosing spondylitis, bursitis, polyarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis, osteoarthritis, tendonitis, rheumatoid arthritis, pain, primary dysmenorrhea. Off-label: acute migraine treatment, migraine prophylaxis. Approved prescription-only in 1976; OTC approval 1994.

## Adult Dosing
- Mild-moderate arthritis (OA/RA/ankylosing spondylitis): 220–550 mg PO q12h; max 1650 mg/day for up to 6 months
- Acute gouty arthritis: 825 mg initial, then 275 mg PO q8h until resolution
- Acute severe headache/migraine: naproxen sodium 550 mg q12h, may increase to 825 mg; max 1375 mg/day

## Pediatric Dosing
- ≥12y: 20 mg/kg/day PO, not exceeding 1000 mg/day (prescription); 660 mg/day max non-prescription/OTC
- 2–12y: 20 mg/kg/day PO, not exceeding 1000 mg/day; non-prescription use not recommended
- <2y: safety/efficacy not established
- General pediatric NSAID principle: lowest effective dose, shortest duration

## Pharmacokinetics
Extensive hepatic metabolism; ~95% excreted in urine; half-life 12–17h. Naproxen sodium absorbed faster than naproxen base. Can be taken with food/milk/antacids/PPI/misoprostol to reduce GI effects. Topical formulation also exists.

## Special Populations
- **Renal:** no adjustment needed if CrCl ≥30 mL/min; not recommended if CrCl <30 mL/min
- **Hepatic:** no specific dosing guideline; use lowest doses with caution
- **Pregnancy:** avoid in 1st trimester; contraindicated at 30 weeks gestation
- **Elderly:** start with lower doses — increased risk from CV disease, CKD, GI ulcer/bleed history

## Contraindications
**Absolute:** documented NSAID hypersensitivity; NSAID/aspirin-induced asthma; pregnancy ≥30 weeks; perioperative CABG surgery.
**Relative/caution:** recent MI/heart disease, hypertension, CHF, fluid retention/edema, dehydration, GI history (PUD/GERD), bleeding/coagulopathy, hepatic/renal disease, asthma, sodium restriction, chronic alcohol/tobacco use, elderly, women actively attempting conception.

## Adverse Effects
**Common:** dyspepsia, nausea, dizziness, elevated LFTs, increased BP, diminished renal function, rash, increased bleeding risk, GI ulcers.
**Serious but rare:** blood dyscrasias, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, MI, stroke, heart failure, anaphylaxis.
**GI mechanism:** COX inhibition reduces gastric mucosal prostaglandin/protective effect; COX-1 more important for mucosal integrity than COX-2.
**Renal mechanism:** prostaglandins regulate renal hemodynamics/electrolyte reabsorption; NSAIDs can significantly decrease renal blood flow → AKI/renal failure when perfusion is prostaglandin-dependent; sodium/water retention can raise BP in hypertensive patients.
**Platelet effects:** COX-1 inhibition in platelets decreases thromboxane A2, dose-dependently increases bleeding time; reversible on discontinuation; clinical bleeding-risk studies show mixed results.
No specific incidence percentages given in this article.

## Monitoring
Pain relief efficacy; blood pressure; kidney function; GI symptoms (GERD, abdominal pain, melena); periodic CBC (anemia) and chemistry panel (renal/hepatic) for chronic therapy.

## Toxicity/Overdose
Naproxen overdose common (OTC availability) but usually mild. No specific antidote — supportive care, vital sign monitoring. Activated charcoal benefit uncertain. Hemodialysis generally not recommended (high protein binding) but may help selected cases with renal impairment/acid-base disturbance. Large overdoses can cause seizures and metabolic acidosis, potentially leading to renal failure.
