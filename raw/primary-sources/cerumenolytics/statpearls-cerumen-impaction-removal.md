## Epidemiology
Most common ear complaint presented to US clinicians. Affects up to 6% of the general population, 10% of children, >30% of elderly/cognitively impaired individuals. ~12 million patient visits and ~8 million removal procedures annually in the US.

## Risk Factors
Routine hearing aid/earplug use; external ear canal exostoses or anatomic abnormalities; immunosuppressive conditions (HIV, diabetes, transplant recipients); chronic anticoagulation; cognitive impairment.

## Pathophysiology
Cerumen ≈60% keratin from shed skin cells, remainder lipids/peptides from sebaceous/ceruminous glands (lateral third of external auditory canal). Impaction occurs when natural jaw-movement self-cleaning fails. Impacted cerumen shows increased bacterial/fungal colonization — every sampled recurrent-impaction case showed fungal growth (most commonly *Aspergillus terreus*); when bacteria present, *Staphylococcus aureus* most common.

## Clinical Presentation / Treatment Indications
Symptoms: hearing loss, ear fullness, pruritus, dizziness, tinnitus, otalgia. Treatment indicated when symptomatic or when cerumen blocks otoscopic exam/audiometric testing/chronic-condition surveillance. Asymptomatic impactions may not need treatment — cerumen has bactericidal, protective, emollient properties.

## Treatment Options

**Cerumenolytic agents** — liquid solutions to thin/soften/break up/dissolve wax; water- or oil-based.
- **Water-based:** hydrogen peroxide, acetic acid, docusate sodium, sodium bicarbonate. "Docusate sodium appears to be the most effective, particularly when used as pre-treatment before irrigation."
- **Oil-based:** peanut, olive, almond oils (OTC).
- **Carbamide peroxide:** commonly prescribed/OTC; 5–10 drops twice daily up to 4 days; releases oxygen to soften/encourage spontaneous extrusion, weak antibacterial effect. Typical general dosing: up to 5 drops, 1–2x/day, 3–7 days.
- **Contraindications:** perforated tympanic membrane, history of ear surgery (incl. tympanostomy tubes).

**Irrigation:** requires confirmed intact tympanic membrane first. Methods: syringe irrigation (warm water or 50/50 water-H2O2), oral jet irrigators, electronic irrigators. Water temperature should match body temperature — over/under-warm fluid can cause caloric vertigo via semicircular canal convection currents. **Efficacy note: less effective than manual removal under visualization in patients >70 years.**

**Manual removal:** AAO-recommended method; binocular microscope, handheld specula, metal/plastic loop/spoon/curette/alligator forceps. Advantage: no moisture exposure (lower infection risk). Disadvantage: perforation/local trauma risk, especially with uncooperative patients or inexperienced clinicians.

**Recurrence prevention:** for >1 impaction/year — mineral oil applied to external canal 10–20 min weekly; hearing-aid users should remove devices 8h/day to reduce buildup.

## Contraindications
No absolute contraindications for removal in general, but caution warranted: immunosuppressed patients, chronic anticoagulation, anatomical canal narrowing. Diabetic patients have higher cerumen pH, making superimposed bacterial infection more common.

## Complications
Manual removal: tympanic membrane perforation, external auditory canal trauma. Avoid cotton swabs (can worsen impaction/perforate TM) and ear candling (not effective in scientific studies, carries injury risk). Cerumenolytic use risks: local irritation, rash, superinfection with prolonged application.

## Clinical Significance
Post-removal, persistent symptoms should prompt evaluation for Eustachian tube dysfunction, otitis media, otosclerosis, sensorineural hearing loss, TMJ syndrome, URI.

## Personnel
Simple removal: emergency physicians, primary care providers, nurses, technicians. Complex anatomy: otolaryngologist. Pediatric/cognitively impaired: may need sedation/general anesthesia, though smaller children may tolerate papoose restraint instead.
