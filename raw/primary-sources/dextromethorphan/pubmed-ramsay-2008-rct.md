# PubMed Abstract — Ramsay et al. 2008 RCT (condensed)

**Citation:** Ramsay J, et al. Assessment of antitussive efficacy of dextromethorphan in smoking related cough: objective vs. subjective measures. Br J Clin Pharmacol. 2008 May. PMID: 18279476. Free PMC article.

## Design
Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover trial in adults with smoker's cough (an established antitussive research model). Dextromethorphan 22mg (0.8mL⁻¹) pregastric delivery vs. matched placebo. Objective measure: citric acid dose-response cough challenge (concentration eliciting 2 coughs/inhalation, "C2"). Subjective measures: daily cough-symptom diary, Leicester Cough Questionnaire, manual cough counter.

## Results
- **Objective (C2, citric acid cough threshold):** dextromethorphan significantly increased the threshold vs. placebo at both 1h post-dose (0.49 mM vs. 0.24 mM increase, 95% CI 0.05–0.45 for the difference, p<0.05) and 2h post-dose (0.57 mM vs. 0.34 mM, 95% CI 0.01–0.43, p<0.05) — i.e., objectively reduced cough reflex sensitivity
- **Subjective (diary, quality-of-life questionnaire):** highly significant improvement vs. baseline in BOTH arms, but **no significant difference between dextromethorphan and placebo**
- No correlation seen between objective cough-sensitivity threshold and actual recorded cough counts/symptoms
- Marked "placebo effect" evident in both subjective and objective data vs. screening baseline

## Authors' Conclusion
Dextromethorphan objectively diminishes cough reflex sensitivity (citric acid challenge), but this does NOT translate into a subjective symptom benefit over placebo. The paper explicitly frames prior literature the same way: "efficacy in altering cough reflex sensitivity has been shown in healthy volunteers [but] evidence for an effect on clinically important cough is poor." The authors question whether currently-used objective antitussive tests actually predict real-world symptom benefit.

## Relevance
This is a deliberately included honest/mixed result: it shows a real, measurable pharmacologic effect of dextromethorphan on the cough reflex, while also showing that effect does not reliably translate into patient-perceived symptom relief beyond placebo — directly analogous to the pattern seen with pseudoephedrine (objective decongestant effect not always matching subjective symptom scores) and guaifenesin (StatPearls' own "weak evidence" characterization).
