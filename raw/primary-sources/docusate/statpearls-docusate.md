## Mechanism of Action
Emollient stool softener, a surfactant laxative. Reduces surface tension of the oil-water interface of stool, allowing water and lipids to enter the stool mass, producing softer consistency and easier intestinal passage.

## Clinical Background
Constipation prevalence: 16% of all adults, 33% of adults >60y. Docusate has been used in the US since the 1950s.

## FDA Status
**Docusate has NO FDA-approved indications.** Per product monographs, used when peristaltic stimulants are contraindicated, to alleviate difficult/painful defecation — potentially beneficial for patients with painful anorectal conditions or cardiac comorbidities (avoiding straining).

## Dosing
- Oral sodium: 100mg twice daily (typical), up to max 300mg/day
- Oral calcium: 240mg once daily
- Rectal enema: 283mg/5mL, 1–3x/day
- Pediatric 2–12y: 50–150mg single or divided doses
- Cerumenolytic use (off-label): intra-aural liquid via syringe

## Pharmacokinetics
Significant first-pass hepatic metabolism, forming active and inactive metabolites; high plasma protein binding. No specific renal/hepatic dose adjustments given.

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity to docusate/ingredients; nausea/vomiting (requires significant fluid intake to work, problematic if vomiting); intestinal obstruction, appendicitis symptoms, acute abdominal pain, fecal impaction; risk of dependency with excessive use beyond 7 days; benzyl alcohol-containing formulations contraindicated in neonates (gasping syndrome risk).

## Adverse Effects
Anorexia, diarrhea, vomiting usually associated with excess doses. Rare: abdominal cramping, rash. Syrup/liquid formulations may cause throat irritation and bitter taste. Excessive/chronic use creates laxative dependency risk with potential hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.

## Drug Interactions
Use with caution alongside mineral oil — docusate may increase mineral oil absorption, potentially causing systemic lipid granulomas.

## Special Populations
- **Pregnancy:** limited data; one case report of symptomatic neonatal hypomagnesemia from chronic maternal use, but no evidence overall suggests docusate use is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes
- **Breastfeeding:** minimally absorbed from the GI tract, unlikely to be present in breast milk
- **Geriatric:** commonly prescribed despite limited evidence supporting effectiveness, though may help avoid straining post-surgery or post-myocardial-infarction

## Efficacy Controversy (StatPearls' own framing)
"Numerous systematic reviews examining the efficacy of docusate have yielded no significant evidence to support its use." A 2021 comprehensive review found **no significant difference** between docusate and placebo across 7 RCTs. Studies specifically on opioid-induced constipation show docusate **has not been shown to be more effective than placebo**. Despite this, docusate remains one of the most frequently prescribed laxatives — leading professional organizations to recommend discontinuing its routine use in favor of evidence-based alternatives.
