## Epidemiology
Affects ~65–70% of the global population (varies greatly by ancestry). Rare in children <5y. Ethnic distribution: highest (50–100%) in African Americans, Hispanics/Latinos, Asian populations, nearly complete in American Indians; lowest (2–15%) in Northern Europeans/North Americans. Onset timing varies: Chinese/Japanese populations lose 80–90% of lactase activity within 3–4 years post-weaning; Northern Europeans may take 18–20 years to reach lowest lactase levels.

## Pathophysiology
Lactase (small intestinal brush border) hydrolyzes lactose into glucose and galactose, absorbed via SGLT1/Glut-2. In deficiency, undigested lactose reaches the colon, fermented by bacteria into hydrogen, methane, CO2, hydrogen sulfide, short-chain fatty acids. Two symptom mechanisms: (1) osmotic — unabsorbed lactose raises intraluminal osmotic pressure, causing fluid influx/osmotic diarrhea; (2) gas production from bacterial fermentation causing bloating/flatulence.

**Primary lactase deficiency (most common):** ancestral/normal Mendelian pattern, gradual enzyme decline with age, symptoms in adolescence/early adulthood. C/T-13910 polymorphism: TT = lactase persistence, C/T = partial deficiency, CC = complete deficiency.

**Secondary lactase deficiency:** from intestinal mucosal injury (GI infections like rotavirus/Giardia, celiac disease, Crohn disease, chemotherapy, antibiotics, small intestinal surgery, radiation) — resolving the underlying cause may resolve the lactose intolerance.

**Developmental:** premature infants (28–37 weeks gestation), improves with intestinal maturation.

**Congenital:** rare autosomal recessive LCT gene mutation, ~40 cases reported worldwide, symptoms shortly after birth with milk ingestion.

## Clinical Presentation
Symptoms 30min–2h after lactose ingestion: diarrhea, abdominal bloating/pain, nausea/vomiting, fullness, flatulence. Less common: headache, muscle/joint pain, mouth ulcers, urinary symptoms, impaired concentration.

## Diagnosis
Hydrogen breath test (most common): >20ppm rise above baseline indicates malabsorption. Stool acidity test: detects low pH from lactic acid fermentation. Milk tolerance test: <9 mg/dL glucose rise after 500mL milk suggests malabsorption. Lactose tolerance test: 50g lactose, glucose at 0/60/120min, <20 mg/dL rise suggests intolerance (75% sensitivity, 96% specificity; false negatives possible with diabetes/SIBO). Genotyping emerging but not widely adopted outside Germany/Nordic countries.

## Treatment and Management

**Dietary modification:** most tolerate up to 15g lactose/day, especially with other foods — complete elimination rarely necessary. Greek yogurt has lowest lactose content among yogurts; live yogurt cultures produce beta-galactosidase that may aid in vivo digestion. Plant-based milk alternatives (almond, coconut, soy) are lactose-free options.

**Probiotic supplementation:** Lactobacillus acidophilus DDS-1 strain has shown potential for symptom alleviation.

**Nutritional support:** calcium and vitamin D supplementation recommended to maintain bone health, since untreated malabsorption may contribute to osteopenia.

**Lactase enzyme supplements:** extracted from yeasts/molds, available as tablets or drops. Tablets/capsules: take just before eating a high-lactose product or with the first bite. Liquid drops added to milk: shake/stir and refrigerate 24h before drinking to allow lactose breakdown. **No specific dosing recommendation given in this article** — only administration timing.

**Emerging:** fungal beta-galactosidases (from Aspergillus carbonarius) show greater gastric-condition stability than current commercial lactase preparations; nutrigenomics for personalized intervention.

## Complications/Prognosis
Excellent prognosis overall with dietary modification. Untreated malabsorption may contribute to osteopenia/osteoporosis, malnutrition, weight loss, rickets/growth failure in children. Vitamin D deficiency linked to the LCT-13910C>T variant among Whites. Lactose ingestion causes only reversible symptoms, no permanent GI damage (unlike celiac disease).
