## Mechanism of Action
Precise mechanism unclear. May inhibit COX pathway in the CNS but not peripheral tissues (unlike NSAIDs); does not bind directly to COX-1/COX-2 active sites. May inhibit COX-3 (a COX-1 splice variant), unverified in humans. Analgesia may involve stimulation of descending serotonergic pathways. Metabolite AM404 may activate cannabinoid systems and modulate glutamatergic transmission via TRPV1 receptor activation in the spinal dorsal horn.

## FDA-Approved Indications
Mild-to-moderate pain (monotherapy); severe pain (combined with opioids); fever reduction.

## Pharmacokinetics
- Oral: peak plasma 30–60 min
- IV: immediate, higher peak concentrations
- Rectal: preferred in unconscious patients/children, bypasses first-pass metabolism
- Plasma protein binding low: 10–25%
- Extensive distribution excluding fat tissue
- Hepatic metabolism, first-order kinetics: glucuronide conjugation (primary), sulfate conjugation, CYP450 oxidation (mainly CYP2E1) → toxic intermediate NAPQI, normally neutralized by glutathione at therapeutic doses
- >90% eliminated within 24h; most metabolites renal; <5% unconjugated drug excreted unchanged

## Dosing

| Population | Dose | Max |
|---|---|---|
| Adults/adolescents ≥13y, ≥50kg | 1000mg q6h or 650mg q4h (max single dose 1000mg, min interval 4h) | 4000 mg/day |
| Adults/adolescents <50kg | 12.5 mg/kg q4h or 15 mg/kg q6h (max single dose 15mg/kg) | 75 mg/kg/day (up to 3750mg) |
| Pediatric 2–12y | 12.5 mg/kg q4h or 15 mg/kg q6h (max single dose 15mg/kg) | 75 mg/kg/day |
| Neonates (≥32wk gestational age or <28 days old) | 12.5 mg/kg q6h | 50 mg/kg/day |
| Infants 29 days–2y | 15 mg/kg q6h | 60 mg/kg/day |
| Elderly (AGS) | 325–500mg q4h or 500–1000mg q6h | 4g/day typical; reduce by 50–75% with hepatic impairment or alcohol misuse history |

Routes: oral (tablet/capsule/syrup/solution/suspension), rectal (suppository), IV infusion. Note: literature review found a lack of evidence that IV acetaminophen outperforms oral/rectal across surgical procedures.

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity to acetaminophen; severe hepatic impairment or severe active hepatic disease.

## Adverse Effects
**Oral/rectal:** skin rash, hypersensitivity reactions, nephrotoxicity (elevated BUN/creatinine), hematologic abnormalities (anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, pancytopenia), metabolic/electrolyte imbalance (decreased bicarbonate, reduced sodium/calcium, hyperammonemia, hyperchloremia, hyperuricemia, elevated glucose/bilirubin/alk phos).
**IV:** nausea, vomiting, constipation, pruritus, abdominal pain.
**Rare but serious:** hypersensitivity/anaphylactic reactions; severe skin reactions (toxic epidermal necrolysis, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome); drug-induced pneumonitis/lung injury.

## Toxicity/Overdose

**Epidemiology:** ~500 fatalities and 50,000 ED admissions/year in the US linked to acetaminophen; >80,000 US poison control cases in 2021; most prevalent drug-related cause of acute liver failure in the US.

**Mechanism:** At therapeutic doses 60–90% undergoes conjugation. In overdose, Phase II pathways deplete, CYP2E1 metabolism increases, NAPQI formation rises (normally only 5–15%), glutathione depletes, NAPQI attacks cellular macromolecules → centrilobular (zone 3) hepatic injury/hepatocellular death, potential nephrotoxicity.

**Toxicity threshold:** rare below 200 mg/kg (child) or 150 mg/kg (adult).

**Clinical course:** Early (0–24h) often asymptomatic, abdominal pain/nausea only. Delayed (24–72h+): nausea, vomiting, jaundice, abdominal pain, hypotension; may progress to cerebral edema requiring airway management/IV fluids/vasopressors.

**Monitoring:** acute ingestion <8h — plot serum APAP on Rumack-Matthew nomogram; non-acute — assess APAP concentration + transaminases; monitor AST/ALT during NAC treatment.

**Antidote — N-acetylcysteine (NAC):** precursor to glutathione synthesis, restores intracellular glutathione to neutralize/inactivate NAPQI. Oral or IV (IV preferred if vomiting). Protocols: 20-hour IV or 72-hour oral; minimum 300 mg/kg over initial 20–24h. High-risk ingestion (≥30g or above high-risk nomogram line): activated charcoal may remain effective beyond 4h for extended-release products; NAC initiation window 4–24h post-ingestion. Stopping criteria: APAP <10 μg/dL, INR <2, normal AST/ALT, or AST/ALT decreased 25–50% if clinically stable. Massive toxicity (>900 μg/mL with acidosis/altered consciousness): hemodialysis + NAC recommended.

## Drug Interactions
- **Warfarin:** prolonged use at 4000mg/day associated with elevated INR — increased INR monitoring frequency advisable
- **Chronic alcohol misuse:** significantly increases toxicity risk — induces CYP2E1, reduces hepatic glutathione, impairs NAPQI detoxification, decreases glucuronidation, enhances oxidation, disrupts hepatocyte membranes, reduces biliary excretion

## Special Populations

**Hepatic impairment:** severe/active liver disease — contraindicated. Mild impairment — reduce total daily dose, monitor LFTs regularly. May be safe in alcoholic liver disease specifically (per cited evidence).

**Renal impairment:** severe (CrCl ≤30 mL/min) — extend dosing intervals, reduce total daily dose.

**Pregnancy:** ACOG considers acetaminophen safe; current evidence does not conclusively link use to fetal developmental issues; no elevated teratogenic effect at normal doses, though caution advised early in pregnancy pending further research. Observational studies have associated prenatal exposure with cryptorchidism, ADHD, and autism spectrum disorder — one cited study (Alemany et al., 73,881 mother-child pairs) found prenatal exposure associated with 19% elevated ASC risk and 21% elevated ADHD risk; postnatal exposure showed no such association.

**Breastfeeding:** suitable for pain/fever relief in breastfeeding mothers; breast milk levels significantly lower than typical infant doses; adverse effects in nursing infants infrequently reported.

**Elderly:** dosing per AGS above; caution with renal/hepatic disease, G6PD deficiency, severe hypovolemia.

**Blood pressure (elderly/hypertensive):** PATH-BP trial (MacIntyre et al.) found 4g/day acetaminophen caused a 5 mmHg increase in daytime systolic BP — high-dose use in hypertensive patients could potentially elevate cardiovascular disease risk.

## Regulatory Note
2011 FDA mandate limited acetaminophen to 325mg/tablet in opioid-combination products — associated with a significant decrease in hospitalizations and the proportion of acute liver failure cases caused by acetaminophen/opioid toxicity.
