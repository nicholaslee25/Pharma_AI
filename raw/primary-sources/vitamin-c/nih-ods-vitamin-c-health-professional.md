## Introduction / Mechanism
Water-soluble vitamin (L-ascorbic acid); humans cannot synthesize it endogenously (unlike most animals) — an essential dietary nutrient. Required for collagen, L-carnitine, and certain neurotransmitter biosynthesis; involved in protein metabolism. Collagen is essential to connective tissue and wound healing. Important physiological antioxidant; regenerates other antioxidants including vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol). Plays a role in immune function and improves absorption of nonheme (plant-based) iron. Deficiency causes scurvy: fatigue/lassitude, widespread connective tissue weakness, capillary fragility.

Intestinal absorption regulated by a dose-dependent active transporter — higher doses have progressively lower absorption than moderate doses; excess absorbed ascorbic acid is excreted in urine.

## Recommended Intakes
90–120mg for adults; 15–115mg for infants/children/adolescents (varies by age/sex/life stage). **Smokers require 35mg MORE per day than non-smokers.**

## Sources
Best food sources: citrus fruits, red/green peppers, kiwifruit, broccoli, strawberries, Brussels sprouts; also orange/grapefruit/tomato juice, fortified cereals. Heat-sensitive — cooking reduces content. Supplement ascorbic acid has bioavailability equivalent to naturally-occurring food ascorbic acid.

## Deficiency
Scurvy is rare in developed countries. Signs/symptoms can appear within 1 month of consuming <~10mg/day. Impaired collagen synthesis → petechiae, ecchymoses, purpura, joint pain, poor wound healing, hyperkeratosis, corkscrew hairs. Fatal if untreated.

## Groups at Risk of Inadequacy
Smokers and secondhand-smoke-exposed individuals, infants fed evaporated/boiled milk, people with limited dietary variety, malabsorption or certain chronic diseases. (Inadequacy = below RDA but still above 10mg/day, i.e., not yet frank scurvy.)

## Vitamin C and Health

**Cancer prevention:** case-control studies show high dietary vitamin C associated with lower cancer risk and cancer patients have lower plasma vitamin C — but evidence from other study types is inconsistent; most RCTs found vitamin C supplementation (alone or with other nutrients) does NOT affect cancer risk.

**Cancer treatment:** mixed results for IV or oral vitamin C in cancer patients; IV administration produces far higher plasma concentrations than oral — more research needed.

**Cardiovascular disease:** plausible mechanisms (reducing vascular smooth muscle apoptosis/plaque instability) but prospective studies show conflicting results, and most clinical trials found no conclusive CVD protection from supplementation.

**Age-related macular degeneration/cataracts:** some studies suggest high intake may reduce risk, but limited clinical trial data do not show vitamin C alone or with other antioxidants affects development risk. However, people who ALREADY have AMD and take antioxidant supplements including vitamin C have a lower risk of progressing to advanced AMD (a treatment-adjacent finding, not a prevention finding).

**Common cold:** regular intake ≥200mg/day may reduce cold incidence specifically in people who exercise intensely, are exposed to cold environments, or have marginal vitamin C status. In the GENERAL population, prophylactic vitamin C does NOT appear to reduce cold risk, though it does modestly reduce cold duration and severity. Taking vitamin C AFTER cold symptoms start does NOT appear to affect duration or severity.

## Health Risks from Excessive Vitamin C
Generally low toxicity; high intake doesn't cause serious adverse effects, but can cause diarrhea, nausea, abdominal cramps, other GI disturbance. Some concern re: kidney stones and excess iron absorption at high doses, but not generally considered a risk in healthy individuals. **Tolerable Upper Intake Level: 400–2,000mg, depending on age.**

## Interactions with Medications
May interact with radiation therapy and medications including chemotherapeutic agents and statins.
