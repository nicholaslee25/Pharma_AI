## Introduction / Mechanism
Essential component of hemoglobin (oxygen transport from lungs to tissues) and myoglobin (muscle oxygen supply, connective tissue health). Necessary for physical growth, neurological development, cellular functioning, and synthesis of some hormones. Two dietary forms: heme (meat/seafood/poultry, better bioavailability) and nonheme (plants, iron-fortified foods; also present in meat alongside heme iron). Heme iron contributes ~10–15% of total intake in Western populations despite better absorption.

Adults contain 3–4g elemental iron, mostly in hemoglobin; remainder stored as ferritin/hemosiderin (liver, spleen, bone marrow) or in muscle myoglobin. Transferrin transports iron in blood. Iron losses are normally small (urine, feces, sweat, shed skin) but greater in menstruating women. **Hepcidin** (circulating peptide hormone) is the key regulator of iron absorption and distribution.

## Assessing Status
Hemoglobin and hematocrit are most common screening tests, though neither sensitive nor specific. Serum ferritin reflects iron stores but is confounded by inflammation. Often multiple measures plus dietary/supplemental intake history are combined for diagnosis.

## Recommended Intakes
8–27mg for adults; 0.27–27mg for infants/children/adolescents, varies by age/sex/life stage. **Vegetarians need more iron** than those eating animal products, due to nonheme iron's lower bioavailability.

## Sources
Food: lean meat/seafood (heme iron, richest source); nuts, beans, vegetables (nonheme); fortified wheat/flour products. Heme iron has better bioavailability than nonheme; ascorbic acid enhances and phytate inhibits nonheme iron absorption. Supplements: ferrous and ferric iron salts most common; different forms contain different elemental-iron amounts and vary in GI side-effect likelihood at high doses. **Experts recommend taking calcium and iron supplements at different times** to avoid absorption interference between them.

## Deficiency
Progressive stages: depletion → deficiency → iron deficiency anemia (when stores exhausted, hematocrit/hemoglobin decline). Symptoms: GI disturbances, weakness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating; can impair cognitive function, immune function, body temperature regulation. Often co-occurs with other nutrient deficiencies.

## Groups at Risk of Inadequacy
Pregnant women, infants/young children, women with heavy menstrual bleeding, frequent blood donors; also people with cancer, GI disorders, or heart failure.

## Iron and Health

**Iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy:** supplementation reduces IDA risk in pregnant women and certain adverse infant effects. Some professional societies/public health bodies recommend routine screening + supplementation; **not all groups agree the evidence is sufficient to recommend routine screening/supplementation for asymptomatic pregnant women** — a real, stated guideline disagreement.

**Iron deficiency anemia in infants/toddlers:** can cause potentially irreversible adverse cognitive/psychological effects. Supplementation or food fortification reduces deficiency risk, but **guidelines vary across professional societies** for this population.

**Anemia of chronic disease:** caused by inflammatory/infectious/neoplastic diseases disrupting iron homeostasis. Usually treated by addressing the underlying disease; iron supplementation or erythropoiesis-stimulating agents used in some cases, but **iron supplementation for this specific condition remains controversial due to infection and cardiovascular event risk.**

## Health Risks from Excessive Iron
Low overload risk from food alone in adults with normal intestinal function. High-dose supplements can cause GI effects; extremely high doses can cause corrosive intestinal necrosis, multisystem organ failure, death. **Tolerable Upper Intake Level: 45mg for adults; 40–45mg for infants/children/adolescents**, varies by age. (Hereditary hemochromatosis patients are separately at risk of iron overload even without supplementation.)

## Interactions with Medications
May interact with: **levodopa, levothyroxine**. **Proton pump inhibitors can reduce iron absorption** (relevant cross-reference given how commonly PPIs are used — see [[proton-pump-inhibitors]]).
