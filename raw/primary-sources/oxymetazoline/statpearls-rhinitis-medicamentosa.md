## Definition
Rhinitis medicamentosa (RM), "rebound congestion" — drug-induced nonallergic nasal inflammation from prolonged topical nasal decongestant overuse.

## Epidemiology
~14% of nonallergic rhinitis cases in the general population (varies by study). ~49% of self-medicating patients with persistent rhinitis overuse intranasal decongestants despite awareness of recommended duration limits. Risk factors: smoking, anxiety disorders, underlying nasal inflammation; possibly more common in younger patients; no clear racial/gender predilection.

## Pathophysiology / Why Oxymetazoline Causes Rebound
Nasal mucosa resistance vessels (arteries/arterioles/AV anastomoses) and capacitance vessels (venous plexuses) are adrenergically regulated. Four proposed mechanisms for rebound:
1. **Ischemic theory:** chronic vasoconstriction → mucosal ischemia → predisposes to interstitial edema
2. **Tachyphylaxis/fatigue:** constrictor mechanisms fatigue → reactive hyperemia; adrenoreceptors become refractory, requiring higher doses, correlating with reduced sensitivity to endogenous catecholamines
3. **Vascular permeability:** vasomotor tone changes → increased permeability/edema
4. **Beta-adrenergic rebound:** beta-receptor activity outlasts alpha effects → rebound vasodilation
Also: prolonged use may suppress endogenous sympathetic norepinephrine release via negative feedback, leaving nerves unable to maintain vasoconstriction on discontinuation.

## Causative Medications
**Imidazolines (more potent, longer-acting — includes oxymetazoline):** oxymetazoline HCl, xylometazoline HCl (onset ~5min, lasts up to 10h), naphazoline HCl — work primarily via alpha-2 agonism, affecting both capacitance and resistance vessels more than phenylethylamines.
**Beta-phenylethylamine derivatives (lower potency):** ephedrine HCl, phenylephrine HCl (onset 15–20min, lasts 2–4h).
**Contributing factor:** benzalkonium chloride (BKC, a preservative in many decongestant sprays) may worsen RM via increased mucosal swelling, though clinical significance evidence is mixed.
**Systemic drugs causing drug-induced rhinitis (different mechanism):** oral beta-blockers, antipsychotics, oral contraceptives, antihypertensives (ACE inhibitors, CCBs), central sympatholytics (clonidine, methyldopa, reserpine), NSAIDs/aspirin, psychotropics (amitriptyline, chlorpromazine, risperidone).

## Time Course
Typically develops after **5–7 days** of topical decongestant use. Older ephedrine-based decongestants showed rebound as early as 3 days, as late as 4–6 weeks. Imidazolines (oxymetazoline) carry lower but still clinically significant risk with prolonged/inappropriate use.

## Clinical Presentation
Recurrent/persistent nasal congestion, typically without rhinorrhea, from prolonged/frequent decongestant use. Also: rhinorrhea (less common), sneezing, nasal itching, postnasal drip, oral breathing/dry mouth/snoring (severe obstruction). Dependence symptoms: headaches, restlessness, anxiety, especially after stopping. Exam: swollen/red/granular (or pale/swollen) nasal mucosa; late-stage atrophic/crusted membrane. RM is a diagnosis of exclusion — no biochemical/imaging test confirms it.

## Treatment
**Primary:** discontinuation. Counsel that congestion may temporarily worsen after stopping — this is not treatment failure.
**Adjuncts:** intranasal corticosteroids (shown to alleviate rebound in animal studies and small human trials — subjective improvement within 48h combined with decongestant discontinuation, vs. >1 week with placebo alone); short oral corticosteroid course (e.g. prednisone 0.5mg/kg/day x5 days); oral antihistamines; turbinate corticosteroid injection; oral adenosine; mast-cell stabilizers; nasal saline irrigation (adjunctive, may reduce medication use); oral pseudoephedrine (limited evidence, may worsen symptoms, has its own CV contraindications).
**Weaning strategies:** discontinue one nostril at a time; dilute with saline before slow withdrawal — reduces abrupt rebound.

## Recovery Timeline
Subjective symptoms may resolve within 48h with intranasal corticosteroids, vs. >1 week without. Objective mucosal abnormalities (edema/inflammation) often take 7–14 days to resolve.

## Prevention / Patient Education
Standard clinical practice: limit use to 3–5 days maximum (implied by the 5–7 day onset threshold). Key messages: OTC availability creates false perception of harmlessness; even brief reuse after prolonged discontinuation can trigger recurrence; patients should be warned against reusing topical decongestants even briefly.

## Complications
Structural/mucosal: mucosal hyperplasia, chronic rhinosinusitis (± polyps), atrophic rhinitis, septal perforation, increased intraoperative bleeding. Systemic: hypertension, other CV effects. Psychological: dependency, withdrawal anxiety.

## Prognosis
Favorable with appropriate management, particularly discontinuation — but reuse (even briefly) risks rapid recurrence.
