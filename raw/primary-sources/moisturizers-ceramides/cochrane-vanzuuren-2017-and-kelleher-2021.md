# Efficacy Evidence — Moisturizers for Eczema: Treatment vs. Prevention (condensed facts, not full text)

## Source 1: van Zuuren et al. 2017 (TREATING existing eczema)
van Zuuren EJ, Fedorowicz Z, Christensen R, et al. Emollients and moisturizers for eczema: abridged Cochrane systematic review including GRADE assessments. Br J Dermatol. 2017 Nov. PMID: 28432721 (abridged version; full Cochrane review PMID 28166390).
**Retrieved:** 2026-08-26 (abstract viewed via PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28432721/)
**License note:** Journal article, copyrighted. Condensed facts from the free PubMed abstract, no full text reproduced.

### Condensed facts
- 77 RCTs, 6,603 participants. Risk of bias: 7 studies (9%) low risk, 34 (44%) unclear, 36 (47%) high risk — a real quality caveat even in this large review.
- **Moisturizer vs. no moisturizer, SCORAD (Scoring Atopic Dermatitis) severity:** mean difference **-2.42 (95% CI -4.55 to -0.28)** — statistically significant improvement, BUT this did NOT meet the pre-specified minimal important difference of 8.7. I.e., real but not necessarily clinically meaningful on this specific severity scale.
- **Fewer flares with moisturizer:** risk ratio **0.40 (95% CI 0.23–0.70)** — a substantial, real reduction in flare risk.
- **Flare rate reduced:** hazard ratio 3.74 (95% CI 1.86–7.50) (interpreted as time-to-flare being meaningfully delayed).
- **Topical corticosteroid use reduced:** mean difference -9.30g over 6-8 weeks (95% CI -15.3 to -3.27) in groups also applying moisturizer — a real steroid-sparing effect.
- Glycyrrhetinic acid-, urea-, and glycerol-containing creams outperformed their controls (vehicle/placebo/no moisturizer) per both patient and physician assessment.
- **More flares with moisturizer ALONE than moisturizer + twice-weekly fluticasone propionate:** RR 2.17 (95% CI 1.55–3.11) — i.e., moisturizer is not a substitute for anti-inflammatory treatment when one is indicated; combining the two beats moisturizer alone.
- Adding moisturizer to topical anti-inflammatory treatment was more effective than anti-inflammatory treatment alone, with fewer flares.

## Source 2: Kelleher et al. 2021 (PREVENTING eczema onset in infants — a different question entirely)
Kelleher MM, Cro S, Van Vogt E, et al. Skincare interventions in infants for preventing eczema and food allergy: A Cochrane systematic review and individual participant data meta-analysis. Clin Exp Allergy. 2021 Mar. PMID: 33550675 (IPD meta-analysis paper; full Cochrane review PMID 33545739).
**Retrieved:** 2026-08-26 (abstract viewed via PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33550675/)
**License note:** Journal article, copyrighted. Condensed facts from the free PubMed abstract, no full text reproduced.

### Condensed facts
- Prospectively planned individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis within a Cochrane review. RCTs of infants <1 year WITH HEALTHY SKIN (i.e., no pre-existing eczema), comparing a skincare/emollient intervention to control, for PREVENTING eczema/food allergy by ages 1-3.
- 33 trials identified; 17 (5,823 participants) had usable outcome data; 10 (5,154 participants) contributed to IPD meta-analysis. Interventions mostly emollients applied for the first 3-12 months of life.
- **Skincare/emollient interventions probably do NOT change eczema risk by age 1-3: RR 1.03 (95% CI 0.81–1.31, I²=41%, MODERATE certainty, 3,075 participants, 7 trials).**
- Food allergy risk: unclear whether increased — RR 2.53 (95% CI 0.99–6.47, VERY LOW certainty, 996 participants, 1 trial) — wide CI, low confidence, but notably the point estimate suggests possible harm, not benefit.
- **Skincare interventions probably INCREASE local skin infection risk: RR 1.34 (95% CI 1.02–1.77, I²=0%, MODERATE certainty, 2,728 participants, 6 trials).**
- **Authors' conclusion: regular emollients during infancy probably do NOT prevent eczema and probably INCREASE local skin infections.**

## Synthesis note — a genuinely important distinction this page must preserve
These two reviews answer DIFFERENT questions and should never be collapsed into one "moisturizers work/don't work" statement:
1. **In infants without eczema, using emollients to try to PREVENT eczema from ever developing:** probably doesn't work, and probably raises skin-infection risk (Kelleher 2021).
2. **In people who already HAVE eczema, using moisturizer as part of TREATMENT:** real, moderate benefit — fewer flares, reduced steroid use, though the severity-score improvement alone doesn't clear the bar for "clinically important" on the SCORAD scale (van Zuuren 2017).
This database's OTC context (adults/families buying CeraVe, Eucerin, etc. for existing dry skin/eczema) matches the SECOND scenario far more than the first — but the first finding is directly relevant if a parent is asking whether daily infant moisturizing prevents eczema.
