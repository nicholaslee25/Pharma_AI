## Mechanism of Action
Azo dye acting as a urinary analgesic via LOCAL effects on the urinary tract — inhibits nerve fibers in the bladder that respond to mechanical stimuli, and blocks kinases involved in pain signaling. Not a systemic analgesic in the conventional sense.

## Indications
FDA-approved: symptomatic relief of dysuria, urgency, frequency associated with lower UTIs — **adjuvant therapy only, not a cure**, used alongside antibiotics for comfort during infection treatment. Additional uses: pain after catheter insertion/cystoscopy, radiation-induced cystitis (safe up to 2 months per source).

## Dosing
- Adults: 100-200mg three times daily with meals (prescription strength); OTC formulation 50-99.5mg tablets, typically 2 tablets 3x/day
- Max duration: 2 days with antibiotics (OTC label)
- Children 6-12: 12 mg/kg/day divided into 3 doses; no commercial pediatric formulation exists (requires compounding)
- Renal impairment: every 8-16h if GFR >50; CONTRAINDICATED if GFR <50 mL/min

## Pharmacokinetics
Rapid GI absorption, peak plasma 2-3h. Half-life ~9.4h (StatPearls notes inconsistent data across sources). Metabolized to aniline and triaminopyridine (linked to adverse hematologic/renal effects). 41-65% excreted unchanged renally.

## Contraindications
Known hypersensitivity. Severe renal insufficiency (GFR <50). Severe hepatitis. G6PD deficiency — precaution, avoid use (hemolysis risk).

## Adverse Effects
**Common:** headache; nausea/vomiting/diarrhea; rash, pruritus, discoloration, ulceration (dermatologic).
**Serious/life-threatening:**
- **Methemoglobinemia:** fewer than 50 cases documented since 1951, though a 2020 National Poison Data System study linked phenazopyridine to "a notable number" of reported cases — a real discrepancy between historical rarity and more recent surveillance data.
- **Hemolytic anemia:** particularly in G6PD-deficient patients; characterized by Heinz bodies and degmacytes ("bite cells").
- **Acute interstitial nephritis:** reported even at THERAPEUTIC doses in patients with normal renal function — not just an overdose phenomenon.
- **Hepatotoxicity:** associated with hypersensitivity reactions, dose-dependent.
- Other: sulfhemoglobinemia, aseptic meningitis, visual disturbances, jaundice, renal calculi.

## Drug Interactions
No confirmed drug-drug or drug-food interactions. Theoretical increased methemoglobinemia risk with local anesthetics (e.g., benzocaine), but confirmed clinical evidence is lacking — has been safely used with lidocaine gel in practice.

## Special Populations
**Pregnancy:** FDA Category B; crosses placenta; use only if indicated.
**Breastfeeding:** avoid, especially if infant <1 month old or has G6PD deficiency.
**Pediatric:** 12 mg/kg/day divided TID; no commercial pediatric formulation, requires compounding.
**Older adults:** consult renal dosing guidance.

## Monitoring & Patient Education
Key counseling points: symptomatic relief ONLY, not an antibiotic; urine turns reddish-orange (normal, not blood); may stain clothing/contact lenses; extended use causes skin/scleral yellowing; report worsening symptoms.
**Patient knowledge-gap data (real, quantified):** research shows **71% of OTC phenazopyridine consumers were unaware that a bladder infection** was causing their symptoms, and **38% reported purchasing the drug as a substitute for medical care** — this is a striking, directly relevant finding for an OTC-safety-focused database, showing the "does not treat UTI" warning is not landing with a large share of actual users.

## Toxicity & Overdose
**Methemoglobinemia treatment:** first-line IV methylene blue (1% solution, 1-2 mg/kg) — CONTRAINDICATED in G6PD deficiency or with concurrent antidepressants (serotonin syndrome risk). Alternative: high-dose IV ascorbic acid.
**Overdose effects:** renal toxicity, acute renal failure, hepatic impairment, life-threatening hemolytic anemia. The triaminopyridine metabolite may damage distal renal tubules.

## Clinical Evidence (StatPearls-cited)
Limited rigorous studies exist (drug predates modern regulatory requirements). One cited study: symptomatic relief rates of dysuria 95.3%, burning 93.6%, frequency 85.6%, nocturia 83.7%. Separately, only 31% of men with prostatitis reported satisfactory symptom relief — a much lower rate than for uncomplicated UTI symptoms, and a real limitation on generalizing the higher percentages above to all urinary-symptom populations.
