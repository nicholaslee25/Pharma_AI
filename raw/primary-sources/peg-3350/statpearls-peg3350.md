## Mechanism of Action
Multiple ethylene glycol monomer units; minimal GI absorption. Forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules, preventing water reabsorption in stool — causes water retention in stool and increases osmotic pressure, producing softer stools and increased bowel movement frequency.

## Indications
**FDA-approved:** constipation treatment in patients ≥17 years. Preferred agent for chronic constipation and disimpaction due to limited adverse effects and better taste than other osmotic agents.
**Non-FDA-approved:** colonoscopy preparation, fecal impaction, pediatric constipation.

## Key Trial Evidence (cited in this StatPearls article)
Randomized, placebo-controlled, multicenter, double-blind trial, 203 patients, 7 days: PEG group reported less hardened stools and decreased exertion (**P<0.0001**), plus better quality of life, reduced pain, less bloating, and constipation symptom relief. (Note: this trial is cited by StatPearls without a specific PMID given in the extracted text — not independently re-verified against a PubMed record in this pass; presented here as StatPearls' own summary.)

## Adult Dosing
Standard: 17g mixed in 8 oz liquid (water, juice, tea, coffee, soda), once daily. OTC use: max 2 weeks without physician guidance; up to 6 months if medically indicated. Onset: 2–4 days for initial bowel movement. Colonoscopy prep: 238g in 2L sports drink day before procedure, split-dosing (1L each day) recommended for afternoon procedures.

## Pediatric Dosing (non-FDA-approved use)
Fecal impaction (>2y): 1–1.5 g/kg/day for 3–6 days, max 6 consecutive days. Chronic constipation: initial 0.4 mg/kg/day [likely a unit typo in source for g/kg, common dosing convention is g/kg — presented as sourced], maintenance 0.2–0.8 mg/kg/day adjusted to response; continue minimum 2 months, taper gradually after ≥1 month of symptom resolution.

## Pharmacokinetics
Minimal absorption after oral administration, poor systemic bioavailability — this is why it's considered suitable even for pregnant/breastfeeding populations.

## Special Populations
- **Pregnancy:** former Category C; despite limited robust research, AAFP considers it the drug of choice for chronic constipation in pregnancy
- **Breastfeeding:** poor systemic absorption means low clinically significant breast milk excretion expected
- **Hepatic/renal impairment, geriatric:** no dose adjustment guidance in manufacturer labeling

## Adverse Effects
**Common:** flatulence, nausea, stomach cramps, diarrhea, abdominal swelling, rectal hemorrhage.
**Topical:** urticaria documented after PEG electrolyte lavage solution use (bowel-prep context, not the plain OTC product).
**Serious but rare:** metabolic acidosis (primarily IV administration context), renal proximal tubular necrosis with oliguria/azotemia/renal failure.
**Pediatric neuropsychiatric concerns (prolonged use) — FDA-reported:** seizures, tremors, obsessive-compulsive behaviors (including repetitive chewing/sucking), paranoia, mood swings, tics, headache, sedation, aggression, anxiety, lethargy, rages. This is a real FDA signal specifically flagged for pediatric long-term use — presented plainly as reported, not exaggerated or minimized.

## Contraindications
Known/suspected bowel obstruction, appendicitis, inflammatory bowel disease, perforated bowel, hypersensitivity to PEG.

## Drug Interactions
**Thickening agents:** preliminary data suggests PEG interacts with starch-based thickeners (risk of viscosity loss) differently than xanthan-gum-based thickeners; xanthan gum recommended as the safer alternative for patients requiring thickened liquids for dysphagia.

## Monitoring
Pre-treatment assessment for bowel obstruction signs; monitor therapeutic efficacy (decreased abdominal pain, BM within 2–4 days); periodic electrolyte assessment with prolonged/frequent/excessive use; pediatric long-term users specifically require monitoring for the neuropsychiatric events listed above; discontinue if loose stools develop; taper chronically-treated patients rather than stopping abruptly.

## Toxicity/Overdose
Exposure/toxicity uncommon and rare overall. Overdose primarily causes diarrhea, potentially leading to dehydration; complications can include confusion and potentially seizures. High/intermediate molecular weight PEG is considered nontoxic given minimal oral absorption. **No antidote exists for PEG overdose.**
