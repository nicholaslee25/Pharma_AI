# PubMed Abstract — Methemoglobinemia Related to Local Anesthetics, 242-Episode Review (condensed facts, not full text)

**Source:** Guay J. Methemoglobinemia related to local anesthetics: a summary of 242 episodes. Anesth Analg. 2009 Mar;108(3):837-845. PMID: 19224791.
**Retrieved:** 2026-08-26 (abstract viewed via PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19224791/)

## Condensed facts
- Literature review: search of PubMed for "local anesthetic" AND "methemoglobinemia" across all published case reports/series.
- **242 episodes identified** (40.1% published in year 2000 or later).
- Per secondary reporting of this review's full data breakdown (not fully itemized in the abstract text extracted, noted here for transparency): approximately 159 cases involved benzocaine, 68 involved prilocaine, and 12 involved lidocaine — i.e., benzocaine was implicated in roughly two-thirds of cases and lidocaine in only a small minority.
- Chocolate-colored blood suggests methemoglobinemia, but other colors may appear too.
- Discrepancy between pulse oximeter saturation (≤90%) and arterial oxygen partial pressure (≥70 mmHg) was present in **91.8% of episodes** — a real, useful clinical clue (the "saturation gap").
- Prilocaine: symptomatic methemoglobinemia can occur in children >6 months at doses >2.5 mg/kg; adult dose should stay under 5.0 mg/kg (reduced to 3.2 mg/kg with renal insufficiency, 1.3 mg/kg with concurrent other oxidizing drugs).
- **A SINGLE spray of benzocaine may induce methemoglobinemia** — not necessarily an overdose/misuse phenomenon.
- Clinical symptoms (including coma) observed at relatively LOW methemoglobin values: coma reported at 32.2% (children) and 29.1% (adults) methemoglobin — lower than some textbook severity thresholds might suggest.
- **Rebound methemoglobinemia (from benzocaine on mucous membranes) can occur up to 18 hours AFTER methylene blue treatment**, with methemoglobin values as high as 59.9% — a critical monitoring point often missed if a patient is discharged too early after apparent treatment success.
- Complications: hypoxic encephalopathy, myocardial infarction, death.
- **Author's conclusion: "Benzocaine should no longer be used."** Prilocaine should not be used in children under 6 months, pregnant women, or patients on other oxidizing drugs, with dose limited to 2.5 mg/kg. This is an unusually strong, direct recommendation from a peer-reviewed anesthesiology journal review — not a hedge.
- This 2009 publication prompted published commentary/debate (Weinberg 2009, PMID 19224770; Bourke 2010, PMID 20023189) about the practicality of a full benzocaine ban — flagged here as evidence the conclusion was notable enough to generate discussion, not universally adopted into practice.
