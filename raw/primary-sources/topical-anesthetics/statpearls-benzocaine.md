## Mechanism of Action
Reversibly binds to and inhibits sodium channels in the neuronal cell membrane — enters cells nonionized, becomes ionized after crossing the membrane, binds the alpha subunit to block voltage-gated sodium channels. pKa 2.6 → relatively fast onset, pH-independent.

## Indications
FDA-approved: dental procedures, infiltrative-anesthesia preparation, minor trauma. Topical gels/liquids/tablets approved; spray formulations lack FDA authorization (20% of sprays were recalled for benzene contamination). OTC oral products NOT approved for children under 2.

## Dosing
5%, 10%, or 20% concentrations (solutions, tablets, sprays, aerosols, creams, gels). Topical spray: ages 12+, max 4x/day. Onset ~30 seconds at 20% concentration; adequate depth in 2-3 minutes.

## Pharmacokinetics
Lipid-soluble weak base. Ester-class drug, rapid plasma hydrolysis via pseudocholinesterase → para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). Primarily renal excretion, minor fecal.

## Critical Adverse Effect: Methemoglobinemia
Benzocaine metabolizes to nitrobenzene, oxidizing hemoglobin iron (Fe2+ → Fe3+), reducing oxygen-carrying capacity. Severity by methemoglobin level:
- **>10%:** unexplained cyanosis
- **15-35%:** headache, tachycardia, dyspnea, tachypnea
- **>35%:** CNS effects (confusion, seizures, coma, apnea)
- **>70%:** fatality
Other effects: hypotension, bradycardia, cardiac arrest, convulsions, dizziness, edema, allergic reactions.
**Risk increases ~20-fold if a patient was exposed to benzocaine within the previous week** — a real, quantified re-exposure risk multiplier.

## Contraindications
Absolute: severe ester-anesthetic allergy; PABA allergy; children under 2 years (methemoglobinemia risk).
Precautions: cardiac arrhythmias, prior methemoglobinemia history, G6PD deficiency, decreased lung function, COPD/emphysema/CAD, significant skin trauma or infection.

## Drug Interactions
Dapsone and nitrate derivatives both increase methemoglobinemia risk when combined with benzocaine.

## Special Populations
**Pregnancy:** FDA Category C; lidocaine/prilocaine (Category B) are preferred alternatives.
**Breastfeeding:** minimal impact if applied away from the breast; avoid direct nipple/breast application (infant ingestion risk if <2 years).
**Pediatric:** retrospective analysis 2004-2014 documented exposures primarily in children under 4, some requiring methylene blue and critical care admission. **Benzocaine appears on the Key Potentially Inappropriate Drugs in Pediatrics (KIDs) List** specifically due to methemoglobinemia association — pediatric use for teething or pharyngitis should be avoided.
**Older adults:** higher complication risk, especially with bronchitis/asthma/emphysema/heart disease.

## Monitoring (per World Federation of Societies of Anesthesiologists)
Continuous ECG, blood pressure/pulse oximetry, CO2 assessment, intermittent urine output, routine CBC if methemoglobinemia suspected.

## Toxicity Management
**Methemoglobinemia:** supplemental oxygen; IV methylene blue 1% solution (repeatable after 1h if no improvement) — CAUTION: doses >7 mg/kg may worsen symptoms; ascorbic acid as slower alternative.
**Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST):** prompt 20% lipid emulsion therapy for suspected seizures/cardiovascular events; airway control, seizure management, hemodynamic stabilization.

## Additional Notes
A documented case: vulvar changes/labial enlargement attributed to chronic topical benzocaine use in a 14-year-old — an off-label-use risk example.
