## Mechanism of Action
Blocks H1 receptors in the CNS — specifically the area postrema and the vomiting center in the vestibular nucleus. Many antihistamines (including dimenhydrinate) also have anticholinergic activity, blocking muscarinic receptors at the same sites. Must cross the blood-brain barrier to exert antiemetic effect. (Dimenhydrinate is the 8-chlorotheophylline salt of diphenhydramine — combining a first-generation antihistamine with a xanthine intended to offset diphenhydramine's sedation, per the separate "Antihistamines" StatPearls article.)

## Indications
Motion sickness prevention, vertigo-related nausea. Antihistamines (diphenhydramine, dimenhydrinate) carry efficacy similar to dexamethasone and droperidol for reducing postoperative nausea/vomiting, though not typically first-line for that use.

## Dosing
**Adult:** dimenhydrinate 50–100 mg PO every 6 hours as needed (max 400 mg/day per the separate general Antihistamines StatPearls article).
**Pediatric:** not specifically given for dimenhydrinate in this article; general caution urged with all antihistamines in very young children (article notes promethazine specifically carries increased respiratory depression risk under age 2).

## Contraindications
Hypersensitivity; lower respiratory tract disease (particularly asthma) warrants cautious use, especially under age 2.

## Adverse Effects
CNS depression, fatigue, blurred vision, dry mouth, constipation (anticholinergic effects). Dimenhydrinate specifically can cause paradoxical vomiting and may worsen narrow-angle glaucoma.

## Drug Interactions
Synergistic CNS depression with alcohol and other depressants — avoid combining with alcohol, sedatives, hypnotics, tranquilizers.

## Special Populations
**Pregnancy:** doxylamine is the generally preferred antihistamine in pregnancy; diphenhydramine and loratadine have also been used safely.
**Breastfeeding:** antihistamines in general should be avoided due to potential lactation inhibition; second-generation antihistamines preferred if needed.

## Monitoring
Watch for anticholinergic effects, particularly in patients with epilepsy, chronic alcohol use, or pre-existing heart disease; watch for cardiac effects (tachycardia, prolonged QTc).

## Toxicity/Overdose
Airway stabilization, supplemental oxygen, continuous monitoring, IV access, ECG. Sodium bicarbonate an option for prolonged QRS/arrhythmias. Benzodiazepines for agitation/seizures. Physostigmine (anticholinesterase) may be warranted for severe toxicity — requires toxicologist consultation and bedside atropine availability (physostigmine itself can cause complications).
