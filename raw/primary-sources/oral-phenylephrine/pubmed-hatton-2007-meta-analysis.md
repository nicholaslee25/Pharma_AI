# PubMed Abstract — Hatton et al. 2007 Meta-Analysis (condensed)

**Citation:** Hatton RC, et al. Efficacy and safety of oral phenylephrine: systematic review and meta-analysis. Ann Pharmacother. 2007 Mar. PMID: 17264159.
**Retrieved via:** PubMed abstract (browser), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17264159/

## Design
Systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs measuring oral phenylephrine's effect on nasal airway resistance (NAR) vs. placebo in patients with nasal congestion. Searched MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, EMBASE, International Pharmaceutical Abstracts, Federal Register through Jan 2007, supplemented by personal files/hand searches/citation searching. Combination-product studies excluded. Random-effects meta-analysis for NAR and cardiovascular effects.

## Results
- **Phenylephrine 10mg** (8 unpublished studies, 138 patients): did NOT affect NAR more than placebo — mean maximal relative-change difference 10.1% (95% CI −3.8% to 23.9%, i.e. not statistically significant)
- **Phenylephrine 25mg** (8 unpublished studies): significant NAR reduction vs. placebo, 27.6% (95% CI 17.5–37.7%) — but this is well above the standard OTC 10mg dose
- Significant heterogeneity across studies, partly attributable to differing labs/methods
- Patient-reported (subjective) decongestion was NOT consistently better than placebo at any dose tested — objective NAR was described as the more sensitive efficacy measure
- No consistent effect on heart rate or blood pressure at doses ≤25mg

## Authors' Conclusion
"Insufficient evidence that oral phenylephrine is effective for nonprescription use as a decongestant." Recommended the FDA require additional studies to establish safety/efficacy.

## Relevance
This 2007 meta-analysis is the key early evidence base cited in the eventual 2023 FDA Nonprescription Drug Advisory Committee's unanimous determination that oral phenylephrine (at the standard OTC 10mg dose) is not GRASE as a decongestant. The critical finding: the standard **10mg dose showed no significant NAR effect**, while a **25mg dose (2.5x standard) did** show a significant objective effect — meaning the marketed OTC dose specifically is the one lacking demonstrated efficacy, not necessarily the drug at any dose.
