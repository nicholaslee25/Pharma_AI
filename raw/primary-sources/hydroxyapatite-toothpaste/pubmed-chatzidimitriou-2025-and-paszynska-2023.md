# Efficacy Evidence — Hydroxyapatite Toothpaste vs. Fluoride (condensed facts, not full text)

## Source 1: Chatzidimitriou et al. 2025 (systematic review/meta-analysis)
Chatzidimitriou K, et al. The role of hydroxyapatite-based, fluoride-free toothpastes on the prevention and the remineralization of initial caries lesions: A systematic review and meta-analysis. J Dent. 2025 May. PMID: 40107597.
**Retrieved:** 2026-08-26 (abstract viewed via PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40107597/)
**License note:** Journal article, copyrighted. Condensed facts from the free PubMed abstract, no full text reproduced.

### Condensed facts
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of in situ RCTs, children/adolescents/young adults aged <25, fluoride-free hydroxyapatite (HAP) toothpaste vs. conventional fluoride toothpaste, for remineralization/demineralization inhibition.
- Searched Medline (PubMed), Embase, Scopus, Cochrane CENTRAL, Dentistry & Oral Sciences Source, LILACS, plus grey literature and manual reference searches.
- **68 studies screened, only 4 met eligibility criteria** — a notably small evidence base compared to fluoride's (contrast: the Marinho fluoride Cochrane review had 74 studies/42,300 children — see [[fluoride-toothpaste]]).
- Development of new / progression of existing caries lesions (DMFS/ICDAS indices): **no significant difference between HAP and fluoride toothpastes.**
- Significant differences WERE found in lesion size (p<0.0001) and fluorescence value (p=0.01) after 6 months — direction (which product favored) not specified in the abstract text extracted; flagged as an open detail rather than assumed.
- Meta-analysis: **Risk Ratio 0.98 (95% CI 0.85–1.12, p=0.61)** and **Odds Ratio 0.90 (95% CI 0.57–1.42, p=0.68)** — both NOT statistically significant, nominally favoring HAP but not conclusively.
- Authors' conclusion: HAP toothpaste could be an effective fluoride-free alternative, with comparable performance to fluoride plus superior biocompatibility/lack of toxicity — but this is based on only 4 eligible trials, a real limitation on how much weight this conclusion can bear compared to fluoride's evidence base.

## Source 2: Paszynska et al. 2023 (18-month adult RCT)
Paszynska E, Pawinska M, Enax J, et al. Caries-preventing effect of a hydroxyapatite-toothpaste in adults: a 18-month double-blinded randomized clinical trial. Front Public Health. 2023. PMID: 37533523. Trial registration: NCT04756557.
**Retrieved:** 2026-08-26 (abstract viewed via PubMed, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37533523/)
**License note:** Open access (Frontiers in Public Health) — condensed facts recorded here regardless, per this vault's general practice.

### Condensed facts
- 18-month double-blind RCT, non-inferiority design: fluoride-free hydroxyapatite toothpaste (test) vs. sodium fluoride 1450ppm toothpaste (positive control), in adults.
- Primary endpoint: % of subjects with NO increase in overall DMFS index. Non-inferiority margin: Δ ≤ 20% (one-sided 95% CI).
- 189 adults in intention-to-treat analysis; 171 completed per-protocol.
- **Per-protocol: no DMFS increase in 89.3% of the hydroxyapatite group vs. 87.4% of the fluoride group.**
- **Hydroxyapatite toothpaste was NOT statistically inferior to fluoride toothpaste** on the primary endpoint.
- **Important conflict-of-interest disclosure: 3 of the study authors (JE, FM, ESc) are employees of Dr. Kurt Wolff GmbH and Co. KG (Bielefeld, Germany)** — a company that manufactures hydroxyapatite-based dental products. The remaining authors declared no competing interests. This is a real, disclosed funding/authorship conflict relevant to interpreting a positive-for-the-test-product result, and should not be omitted.
- Authors' conclusion: hydroxyapatite proven to be a safe and efficient anticaries agent in oral care.
