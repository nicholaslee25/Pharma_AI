## Key facts
- Background: during 2008–2019, **15 hepatitis B outbreaks** associated with assisted monitoring of blood glucose (AMBG) were reported in U.S. long-term care facilities. CDC recommends assigning glucometers to a single person (never sharing).
- This report: a 69-year-old skilled-nursing-facility resident with diabetes (10-month resident, assisted blood glucose monitoring) was diagnosed with acute hepatitis B (positive HBsAg, IgM anti-HBc, HBV DNA) immediately after hospital transfer, with symptoms of jaundice, nausea, fatigue, and elevated liver transaminases.
- Investigation found glucometers at the facility were SHARED among several residents, though they were disinfected per manufacturer instructions between uses — disinfection alone did not prevent transmission.
- Facility-wide testing identified one other resident with a previously undiagnosed chronic HBV infection, living near the acute-case resident, whose assisted blood glucose monitoring period overlapped with the acute case's incubation period — implicating shared-glucometer/lancet-adjacent practices as the transmission route.
- No other infection-control gaps (standard precautions, injection safety) were identified — the sharing of the glucose-monitoring device/process itself was the exposure route.
- Public health message: HBV transmission risk from shared glucometers can be reduced by routine HBV vaccination of persons with diabetes AND dedicating individual glucometers (and by extension lancets) to a single resident.

## Related background note
Separately, the CDC has documented that reused lancet endcaps/lancets have historically caused hepatitis B outbreaks by physically transferring blood from a used lancet/cap to a supposedly-unused one, later used on a different patient — the first such documented U.S. outbreak (26 cases) was reported in 1990. This underscores that the major real-world safety issue with lancets is behavioral (reuse / shared use across patients), not lancet hardware design itself.
