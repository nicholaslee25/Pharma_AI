## Introduction / Mechanism
Most abundant mineral in the body; structural component of bones/teeth, keeps tissue rigid/strong/flexible. Small ionized pool in circulation/extracellular fluid/tissues mediates vascular contraction/dilation, muscle function, blood clotting, nerve transmission, hormonal secretion. Absorbed via active transport (dominant at lower intakes) and passive diffusion (increasing share at higher intakes); vitamin D required for active-transport gut absorption.

## Assessing Status
Nearly all body calcium is stored in the skeleton — DXA bone mineral density scan reflects cumulative lifetime calcium status. Serum/plasma total calcium is NOT a good reflection of individual calcium status.

## Recommended Intakes
1,000–1,200mg for adults; 200–1,300mg for infants/children/adolescents, varies by age.

## Sources
Food: milk, yogurt, cheese (high); also kale, broccoli, Chinese cabbage, canned sardines/salmon with bones; fortified fruit juices, milk substitutes, tofu, cereals. Absorption ~30% from dairy/fortified foods; lower from certain plants (compounds forming indigestible calcium salts). Supplements: usually calcium carbonate or calcium citrate — absorbed percentage depends on calcium form and total elemental calcium taken at once. Medicines: some OTC antacids contain calcium.

## Deficiency
Can cause osteoporosis (weak/fragile bones, increased fall/fracture risk); can also cause rickets (children) and osteomalacia (children/adults), though these are more commonly from vitamin D deficiency.

## Groups at Risk of Inadequacy
Postmenopausal women, people avoiding dairy products. NHANES data: many in the US consume less than recommended; non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic Asian populations, and people living in poverty, more likely to have inadequate intake.

## Calcium and Health

**Bone health in older adults:** FDA has approved a health claim for calcium+vitamin D supplements reducing osteoporosis risk in older adults — **however, not all clinical trials have found these supplements improve bone health or reduce fracture risk** in this population.

**Cancer:** some evidence calcium may reduce certain cancer risk, but relationship unclear. Observational studies link higher calcium intake to lower colorectal cancer risk, not always corroborated by supplementation trials. High dairy calcium intake may be associated with HIGHER prostate cancer risk. Mixed results for ovarian/breast cancer.

**Cardiovascular disease:** large observational studies link lower calcium intake to higher hypertension/stroke/atherosclerosis risk — but some prospective cohorts and RCTs have found calcium SUPPLEMENTS might actually INCREASE cardiovascular disease risk, while other studies found no link either way. ODS states more research is needed to clarify.

**Preeclampsia:** calcium supplements might reduce preeclampsia risk in pregnant women with inadequate calcium intake.

**Weight management:** unclear — some studies show lower obesity prevalence with higher calcium intake, others find no association.

**Metabolic syndrome:** a few analyses/one clinical trial suggest higher calcium intake may reduce metabolic syndrome risk, especially in women, but more evidence needed.

## Health Risks from Excessive Calcium
Higher supplemental calcium intake might increase kidney stone risk. Per some research, calcium supplements have the potential to increase cardiovascular disease risk (see Bolland et al. 2010 meta-analysis, separate raw file, for the specific data behind this ODS statement). Higher calcium intake might also increase prostate cancer risk. **Tolerable Upper Intake Level: 2,000–2,500mg for adults; 1,000–3,000mg for infants/children/adolescents**, varies by age.

## Interactions with Medications
May interact with: dolutegravir, levothyroxine, lithium, quinolone antibiotics.
