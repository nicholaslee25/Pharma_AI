# PubMed Abstract — Bolland et al. 2010 Meta-Analysis (condensed)

**Citation:** Bolland MJ, Avenell A, Baron JA, Grey A, MacLennan GS, Gamble GD, Reid IR. Effect of calcium supplements on risk of myocardial infarction and cardiovascular events: meta-analysis. BMJ. 2010. PMID: 20671013. (Followed by a 2011 reanalysis including vitamin D co-administration, PMID 21505219.)

## Design
Patient-level and trial-level meta-analyses. Searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL (1966–March 2010), meta-analysis reference lists, 2 trial registries. Eligible: RCTs of calcium supplements (≥500mg/day, WITHOUT co-administered vitamin D), ≥100 participants, mean age >40y, duration >1 year. Lead authors of eligible trials supplied patient-level data where possible.

## Scale
15 trials total: 5 with patient-level data (8,151 participants, median follow-up 3.6 years) and 11 with trial-level data (11,921 participants, mean duration 4.0 years).

## Results
- **Patient-level data (5 trials):** 143 MIs in the calcium group vs. 111 in placebo — hazard ratio **1.31** (95% CI 1.02–1.67, p=0.035)
- Non-significant increases: stroke (HR 1.20, 95% CI 0.96–1.50, p=0.11); composite MI/stroke/sudden death (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.00–1.39, p=0.057); death (HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.96–1.23, p=0.18)
- **Trial-level data (11 trials):** 296 total MIs (166 calcium, 130 placebo) — pooled RR **1.27** (95% CI 1.01–1.59, p=0.038)

## Authors' Conclusion
Calcium supplements (WITHOUT co-administered vitamin D) are associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction. Given how widely calcium supplements are used, even this modest per-person risk increase could translate to a substantial population-level disease burden. The authors called for a reassessment of calcium supplements' role in osteoporosis management.

## Disclosed Conflicts of Interest
No author reported company support for this specific submitted work. Several authors' institutions had received calcium/vitamin D study drug supplies from Wyeth, Mission Pharmacal, Shire Pharmaceuticals, and Nycomed for OTHER, separate clinical trials in the prior 3 years — disclosed per ICMJE requirements, not indicating direct funding of this meta-analysis itself.

## Controversy Note
This finding was genuinely contested at the time — PubMed lists at least 5 separate published rebuttal/comment letters in BMJ 2010 (e.g., "No evidence for increased risk," "Making too much of a weak case," "Doesn't apply to most calcium prescriptions"), reflecting real disagreement in the field about the strength/generalizability of this conclusion. This vault presents the meta-analysis findings plainly while noting this was not a universally accepted conclusion — consistent with the NIH ODS fact sheet's own more hedged framing ("some prospective cohort studies and RCTs have found... other studies have found no link... more research is needed").
