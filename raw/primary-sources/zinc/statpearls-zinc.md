## Indications/Uses
Essential trace mineral for immune function, cell division, wound healing, sensory perception. WHO-recognized essential medicine for diarrhea treatment (may reduce global child mortality ~4%). Reduces incidence of childhood pneumonia. Oral zinc useful for zinc-deficient patients with leg ulcers (topical zinc superior to oral for this indication). Used to decrease copper levels in Wilson disease. Common cold: regular intake may reduce risk; lozenges can reduce symptom severity. Also implicated in blood pressure/glucose/LDL cholesterol support.

## Mechanism of Action
Inhibits 3 of 4 main intestinal ion secretion pathways (cAMP, calcium, nitric oxide) — relevant to antidiarrheal effect. Cofactor for transcription factors and enzyme systems including zinc-dependent matrix metalloproteinases. Antioxidant protection via metallothioneins. Competitive ICAM-1 inhibitor proposed as mechanism for common-cold symptom reduction. Decreases copper bioavailability by inducing copper-binding ligands (relevant to Wilson disease use and to copper-deficiency risk with chronic zinc overuse).

## Dosing — Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) / Adequate Intake (AI)
- Infants 0–6mo (AI): 2 mg/day
- Infants 7–12mo (AI): 3 mg/day
- Children 1–3y: 3 mg/day
- Children 4–8y: 5 mg/day
- Children 9–13y: 8 mg/day
- Males ≥14y: 11 mg/day
- Females 14–18y: 9 mg/day
- Females ≥19y: 8 mg/day
- Pregnant females ≥19y: 11 mg/day
- Lactating females ≥19y: 12 mg/day
- Supplementation range cited: 5–20 mg/day
- **Tolerable Upper Intake Level (adults): 40 mg elemental zinc/day**

## Contraindications
Doses above the 40mg/day tolerable upper intake level are contraindicated in well-nourished pregnant/lactating women.

## Adverse Effects
- GI: diarrhea, abdominal cramps, vomiting within 3–10 hours of excess intake
- Mineral imbalance: copper deficiency, iron deficiency, anemia (with chronic excess)
- Nasal zinc formulations specifically: loss of sense of smell (anosmia)
- Toxicity symptoms: nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain, lethargy, fatigue
- No specific antidote for toxicity — management is discontinuation of supplementation

## Special Populations
- Pregnancy: excessive supplementation (above UL) contraindicated in well-nourished women
- Developing countries: higher deficiency prevalence; WHO recommends supplementation
- Children: deficiency-related mortality is substantial — zinc deficiency estimated responsible for 10.4% of malaria deaths, 14.4% of diarrhea deaths, 6.7% of pneumonia deaths in children aged 6mo–5y (WHO-sourced figures per this article)

## Monitoring / Deficiency Signs
Frequent infection, hypogonadism (males), hair loss, poor appetite, taste/smell impairment, skin sores, slow growth, night blindness, delayed wound healing.
