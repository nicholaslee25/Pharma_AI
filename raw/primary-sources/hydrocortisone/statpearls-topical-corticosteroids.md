## Hydrocortisone Potency Classification
Hydrocortisone butyrate 0.1% cream = Class VI (low-potency). **Hydrocortisone 1% and 2.5% cream/lotion/ointment = Class VII (least-potent class)** — the OTC-available strength sits at the very bottom of the topical steroid potency scale.

## Mechanism of Action
Three pathways: (1) anti-inflammatory — vasoconstriction, inhibition of phospholipase A2 release, direct inhibition of DNA/inflammatory transcription factors; (2) anti-mitotic — decreased epidermal cell proliferation and collagen synthesis; (3) immunosuppressive — suppresses immune cell maturation/differentiation/proliferation.

## Indications
Inflammation and pruritus from steroid-responsive dermatoses: psoriasis, eczema, atopic dermatitis, lichen planus, alopecia areata, among others.

## Dosing/Application — Fingertip Units (FTU)
Face/neck: 2.5 FTU. Trunk front: 7 FTU. Trunk back: 7 FTU. One arm: 3 FTU. One hand: 1 FTU. Frequency: once-to-twice daily recommended — research shows more frequent application only increases adverse effects without added benefit. Vehicle selection: creams for acute exudative inflammation/intertriginous areas; lotions/gels for hair-bearing regions; ointments for thick hyperkeratotic lesions (avoid hairy areas).

## Pharmacokinetics
Absorption enhanced by inflammation, occlusive dressings, and thin epidermis — up to 300-fold variation in absorption between thin and thick skin regions. Hepatic metabolism; renal excretion primarily, some biliary elimination.

## Adverse Effects

**Local (most common):**
- **Skin atrophy** — the most common adverse effect, from the anti-mitotic effect; epidermal thinning, increased dermal ground-substance resorption; reversible but recovery may take months
- **Tachyphylaxis** — effectiveness loss from tolerance; capillaries regain vasoconstriction capacity after ~4 days off treatment, supporting "pulse therapy" (intermittent dosing) to avoid this
- Striae (permanent, scar-like), acne (follicular epithelium degradation), steroid rosacea, perioral dermatitis, hypertrichosis, pigmentation changes, delayed wound healing

**Systemic (mainly with prolonged high-potency use on thin skin — NOT typical for OTC 1% hydrocortisone, but worth noting the class-wide risk):** glaucoma, HPA axis suppression, Cushing syndrome, hypertension, hyperglycemia. **Pediatric patients are particularly vulnerable to HPA suppression** due to higher skin-surface-area-to-body-weight ratio. Risk factors: prolonged use, large application area, use over altered/broken skin barrier.

## Contraindications
**Absolute:** bacterial infections (impetigo, furuncles/carbuncles, cellulitis, erysipelas, lymphangitis, erythrasma) — steroid's anti-inflammatory/vasoconstrictive effect masks the infection.
**Relative:** Candida and dermatophyte infections — immunosuppressive effect may cause persistent fungal infection (tinea incognito, a fungal infection whose typical appearance is altered/obscured by steroid use).

## Drug Interactions
Hydrocortisone, fluocinolone, and mometasone may require careful consideration when combined with live vaccines, insulin, methotrexate, and diuretics — overall interaction severity is described as low.

## Special Populations
- **Pregnancy:** maternal topical corticosteroid use, regardless of potency, is not associated with significant adverse pregnancy outcomes
- **Breastfeeding:** short-term application unlikely to pose infant risk; use lowest potency on smallest area, wipe thoroughly before nursing
- **Pediatrics:** very potent topical corticosteroids should NOT be used in children without specialist dermatological advice; mild corticosteroids (like OTC hydrocortisone 1%) recommended initially
- **Elderly:** dose cautiously — increased skin fragility, higher body-surface-area-to-weight ratio

## Monitoring/Duration
**Treatment duration should not exceed 2–4 weeks regardless of potency.** High-potency steroids should not be used beyond 2 weeks and should then be tapered. For potent-steroid/large-area use, monitor with urinary free cortisol and ACTH stimulation testing; if suppression occurs, reduce frequency or switch to a less potent steroid.

## Topical Corticosteroid Withdrawal
A poorly characterized condition from chronic misuse — presents with erythema, itchiness, burning, secondary scaling lesions upon stopping long-term use.
