## Pathophysiology & Classification
Tear film is 2–5.5 µm thick, three layers: lipid (meibomian glands, reduces evaporation), aqueous (thickest, lacrimal glands — water/electrolytes/proteins), basal mucin (conjunctival goblet cells, regulates surface tension).

Dry eye disease (DED) classifies into two non-mutually-exclusive types:
- **Aqueous-deficient:** inadequate tear production (Sjögren syndrome, lacrimal gland dysfunction, systemic medications)
- **Evaporative:** normal tear quantity, excess evaporation (lipid deficiency, meibomian gland dysfunction, poor blinking, environment) — the most common subtype

Hallmark: tear-film hyperosmolarity (normal <300 mOsm/L; diseased up to 360 mOsm/L) triggers inflammatory mediator release, ocular surface damage, tear instability, self-perpetuating cycle.

## Epidemiology
More prevalent in women (12–22% prevalence, hormonal). Higher in Black and Asian vs. White populations. Up to 70% prevalence in visual-terminal (screen) users. Signs often present without symptoms (dissociation).

## Etiology
Medications (antihistamines, antihypertensives, diuretics, anticholinergics, isotretinoin causing meibomian gland atrophy); systemic disease (Sjögren, RA, lupus, diabetes, thyroid disease, MS); environmental (low humidity, heat, pollution, chemical fumes, smoke); behavioral (smoking, alcohol, poor sleep, diet); ocular (contact lens wear, refractive/cataract surgery, vitamin A deficiency); hormonal (decreased androgen at menopause); topical preservative toxicity from eye drops themselves is also a contributing factor.

## Clinical Presentation
Stinging/burning/pressure, sandy/gritty/foreign-body sensation, epiphora (reflex tearing from irritation), pain, redness (worsened by vasoconstrictor rebound), blurred/fluctuating vision, glare, halos, heavy eyelids, excessive blinking, contact lens intolerance, eye fatigue, inability to cry (severe cases).

## Evaluation
No single gold standard — requires symptoms AND signs (may dissociate). Symptom questionnaires: OSDI, DEQ-5, SANDE. Tear film breakup time (TBUT) <10s suggests DED. Schirmer I test <5–10mm wetting/5min indicates aqueous deficiency. Fluorescein staining ≥5 spots = corneal damage; lissamine green ≥9 spots = conjunctival/lid damage. Osmolarity: 308 mOsm/L threshold mild-moderate, 316 mOsm/L severe. MMP-9 point-of-care testing available for inflammation. Sjögren workup if suspected: Ro/SS-A, La/SS-B antibodies, RF, ANA, possible salivary gland biopsy.

## Treatment Approach (Stepwise by Severity)

**Initial tier:** patient education; environmental modification (eliminate fans/direct airflow, reduce screen time, humidifier); eliminate offending medications; topical ocular lubricants; lid hygiene (warm compresses, lid scrubs); oral essential fatty acid supplements.

**Intermediate tier:** preservative-free ocular lubricants; reversible punctal occlusion (plugs); nighttime ointment/moisture goggles; device-assisted meibomian gland heating/expression; intense pulsed light therapy; topical anti-inflammatories (corticosteroids, cyclosporine, lifitegrast); oral antibiotics (macrolide/tetracycline).

**Advanced tier:** serum eye drops; oral/topical secretagogues; therapeutic contact lenses; amniotic membrane grafting; surgical punctal occlusion; tarsorrhaphy.

Note: this article does not give specific dosing frequency/concentration guidance for artificial tears themselves — see the Semp et al. 2023 systematic review (separate raw file) for that.

## Complications
Mild-moderate DED: ocular irritation, visual disturbance. Severe DED: infectious keratitis, corneal ulceration, scarring, vision loss. Nonocular associations: depression, sleep/mood disorders, dyslipidemia, migraine.

## Prognosis
Minimal published natural-history data untreated. Typically chronic with exacerbations. Postsurgical dry eye (post-cataract/refractive surgery) often improves with time (possible corneal nerve regeneration/inflammation reduction).
