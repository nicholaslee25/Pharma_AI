## Mechanism of Action
Prodrugs that decrease gastric acid secretion by blocking the H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump) — the final step of gastric acid secretion — in parietal cells. Activated via acid-catalyzed cleavage in the secretory canaliculi. Hepatically metabolized primarily via CYP2C19 (varies by specific agent).

## FDA-Approved Agents (class, as of 2015)
Omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, dexlansoprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole. Omeprazole is among the top 10 most prescribed drugs in the US.

## Indications
**FDA-approved:** esophagitis, non-erosive reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, NSAID-induced ulcer prevention, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, H. pylori triple-therapy component, pediatric GERD (short-term), pediatric eosinophilic esophagitis healing.
**Off-label:** antiplatelet-therapy GI protection during high-risk endoscopy, functional dyspepsia, NSAID-ulcer prevention in ACS/chronic pain patients; investigational anti-tumor research (melanoma, multiple myeloma, colorectal cancer, lymphoma, metastatic breast cancer — not an established indication).

## Administration
Enteric-coated tablets, gelatin capsules, coated-granule suspensions, or bicarbonate combinations (to prevent premature gastric activation). IV formulations exist for lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole. **Take before food** — proton pumps activate during meals. Once-daily: first thing in the morning. Twice-daily: morning dose + ~30min before dinner. Nighttime-predominant symptoms: consider pre-dinner timing. Full effect may take a few days despite being the most potent acid-reduction drug class available.

## Pharmacokinetics / Special Populations
Metabolism varies by ethnicity/age: patients of Asian ethnicity show increased bioavailability, may need lower initial doses; elderly patients also show increased bioavailability, requiring closer monitoring/dose adjustment.

## Adverse Effects

**Hypomagnesemia (established, serious):** rare but can drop magnesium to levels not correctable by supplementation alone, requiring PPI discontinuation; risk of tetany, seizure, muscle weakness, delirium, cardiac arrhythmias. Possible mechanism: impaired intestinal magnesium absorption via TRPM 6/7 channels.

**Infections:** increased C. difficile infection, enteric foodborne infections, possibly increased community-acquired pneumonia risk — reduced gastric acidity permits bacterial overgrowth and aspiration risk.

**Rebound acid hypersecretion:** discontinuation after prolonged use can paradoxically raise acid levels above baseline (PPIs raise gastrin, stimulating ECL cell proliferation/histamine production) — doesn't negate efficacy during treatment, but complicates stopping.

**Vitamin B12 deficiency:** gastric acid activates proteases that release B12 from R-factor protein for intrinsic-factor binding/ileal absorption; PPI acid suppression impairs this — clinical deficiency remains rare despite the mechanism.

**Iron deficiency:** reported with prolonged use, exact mechanism unclear. Calcium malabsorption also occurs but appears subclinical in most cases.

**Osteoporosis/fractures:** conflicting data; proposed mechanisms (calcium malabsorption, increased gastrin, decreased B12, possible bone proton pumps) but no clearly established etiology.

**Dementia, kidney disease, heart disease:** isolated retrospective analyses suggested links, but these associations have NOT held up in prospective studies — significant confounding concerns, inconsistent findings.

**Malignancy:** initial animal-model concern re: gastrin, but not demonstrated in human patients. Fundic gland polyps increase with prolonged use but are not associated with malignancy.

**Other common:** headaches, dizziness, skin rash, abdominal pain, diarrhea, back pain, upper respiratory infections.

## Drug Interactions
Can delay clearance of: phenytoin (caution), warfarin (caution), diazepam (caution). Ketoconazole absorption decreased — use alternative antifungal during long-term PPI therapy. Digoxin absorption potentiated — extreme caution given digoxin's narrow therapeutic index/severe toxicity profile. (Note: this specific StatPearls article does not explicitly discuss the well-known clopidogrel–PPI CYP2C19 interaction, despite CYP2C19 being central to PPI metabolism generally — flagged as a gap in this particular source rather than fabricated.)

## Contraindications
Known PPI hypersensitivity. Severe hepatic disease is a relative contraindication (used clinically with increased monitoring despite this).

## Monitoring
Magnesium levels — evidence supports monitoring, particularly in kidney transplant patients. Vitamin B12 — more controversial but reasonable to consider for long-term therapy in select cases. Bone density scanning/calcium supplementation — currently limited evidence supporting these as effective osteoporosis-prevention strategies specifically for PPI users.

## Clinical Efficacy Note
~50% of patients taking PPIs for non-erosive GERD do NOT achieve complete symptom resolution — dosage increases and clinical reassessment are often necessary. This is a meaningfully large real-world efficacy gap worth stating plainly.

## Toxicity/Overdose
No FDA-approved antidote exists.
